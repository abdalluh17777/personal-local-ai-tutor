# Lesson 37: Final Project: Your Research Proposal in APA Style

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 5: Writing and Presenting Research in APA Style*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 2.9 Overview of the Scientific Method (Summary); 11.5 Presenting Your Research (Summary).*

## In this lesson
This lesson brings the stage together: you review the research cycle and the key ideas of the course, then assemble, revise and check your complete research proposal in APA style. In English, you practise precise definitions and contrasts, careful claims about evidence, and proofreading.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Describe the research cycle and place your own proposal in it.
- Define and contrast key ideas: theory and hypothesis, internal and external validity, Type I and Type II errors.
- Assemble a complete proposal in the correct APA order and format.
- Proofread a text at all three levels of APA style.
- Write an abstract that summarizes the whole proposal.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| research literature | all the published research in a particular field | journal articles and scholarly books |
| interestingness | a property of a research question: the answer is in doubt, fills a gap in the literature, has important practical implications | Is the answer in doubt? |
| feasibility | the extent to which a research question can be answered with available resources | time, money, skills, equipment, participants |
| theory | a coherent explanation or interpretation of one or more phenomena | broad: explains larger bodies of data |
| hypothesis | a prediction about a new phenomenon that would be observed if a particular theory were true | Students who nap after learning will recall more word pairs. |
| hypothetico-deductive method | the general way researchers use theories to generate new research and, in the process, test and revise the theories | theory, hypothesis, test, reevaluation |
| internal validity | how far the design supports the conclusion that the independent variable caused the differences in the dependent variable | high in laboratory experiments |
| external validity | how far the results can be generalized to people and situations beyond those studied | often higher in field studies |
| Type I error | concluding that an effect is real when it is not | "The nap helps" when it does not |
| Type II error | failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is false: concluding there is no effect when there is one | missing a real effect of the nap |
| that is | introduces a restatement or definition | "statistically significant, that is, ... unlikely to be due to chance alone" |
| in light of | considering | "reevaluate the theories in light of the new results" |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**The research cycle (2.9).** "A research question based on the research literature leads to an empirical study, the results of which are published and become part of the research literature." Your proposal covers the first steps; Lessons 33 to 36 showed the last one.

**What the two summaries say, and where it goes in your proposal**

| Key idea | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| Review the literature early: to refine the question, find methods, place the question in context, prepare to write | literature review; references |
| Interestingness (answer in doubt, a gap, practical implications) and feasibility (time, money, knowledge and skill, equipment, participants) | opening; method |
| A theory is broad and explains larger bodies of data; a hypothesis is specific and predicts the outcome of a particular study | literature review; closing |
| Variables are quantitative or categorical; a sample is a subset of a population (convenience or simple random sampling) | method: participants, materials |
| Experimental research manipulates an independent variable; non-experimental research measures variables as they naturally occur | method: design |
| Laboratory experiments: high internal validity; field studies: often more external validity | design; limitations |
| Descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode; range, standard deviation, variance) and inferential statistics (statistical significance) | planned results |
| Type I and Type II errors: we never know for certain that a conclusion is correct | limitations |
| Theories can be supported but not proved, and a disconfirmed hypothesis does not necessarily disprove a theory | discussion |
| The three levels of APA style and the standard sections of a report (Lessons 33 to 35) | the whole proposal |

> **APA 7th edition: the order of a complete student paper**
>
> 1. Title page: bold title, your name, department and university, course, instructor, date.
> 2. Abstract: a new page, "Abstract" bold and centered, one paragraph with no indent, 250 words at most.
> 3. Text: a new page headed by the bold, centered title; introduction, then Method, Results and Discussion, which do not start new pages.
> 4. References: a new page.
> 5. Tables, then figures (each on its own page, unless placed in the text), then appendices, such as Appendix A, your consent form, and Appendix B, your questionnaire.
>
> Throughout: double spacing, 1-inch margins, paragraphs indented 0.5 inch, a page number at the top right, and a readable font such as 12-point Times New Roman or 11-point Calibri.

### Language focus
**1. Defining and contrasting.** The summaries define with *be* or a simple present verb, add examples in parentheses, and contrast with *while*:
- "A theory is broad in nature and explains larger bodies of data. A hypothesis is more specific and makes a prediction about the outcome of a particular study."
- "Variables vary across people or situations and may be quantitative (e.g., age) or categorical (e.g., course subject)."
- "Laboratory experiments tend to have high internal validity ..., while field studies often have more external validity ..."
- *e.g.* gives examples; *i.e.* and *that is* restate: "measuring variables as they naturally occur (i.e., without manipulating anything)".

**2. Careful claims about evidence.** The summaries never claim certainty: "unlikely to be due to chance alone and therefore ... likely to represent a real effect"; "we never know if our conclusions are correct"; theories can be supported but not proved; "disconfirming a hypothesis does not necessarily mean that theory has been disproved". Use the same care in your discussion: *support*, not *prove*; *suggest*, *is consistent with*.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
>
> - Arabic nominal sentences need no verb "to be" in the present, so a definition can lose its verb in English: "A hypothesis more specific than a theory." Check that every definition has *is* or *are*.

## Answer key
**1. Reading questions**
1. A research question based on the research literature leads to an empirical study, whose results are published and become part of the literature.
2. To refine the research question, identify appropriate methods, place the question in the context of other research, and prepare to write an effective report.
3. Interestingness: whether the answer is in doubt, whether it fills a gap in the literature, whether it has important practical implications. Feasibility: time, money, technical knowledge and skill, and access to special equipment and participants.
4. They construct theories (or work with existing ones), derive hypotheses from them, test the hypotheses, and reevaluate the theories in light of the results.
5. Laboratory experiments allow strong causal conclusions (high internal validity); field studies, in real settings, generalize better to the real world (more external validity).
6. Type I: concluding that an effect is real when it is not. Type II: concluding there is no effect when there is a real effect in the population.
7. A participants subsection and a design and procedure subsection.
8. To present new research to interested researchers and to facilitate further interaction among them; they are much less detailed than reports.

**2. Book exercises**
1. (example answer) A theory makes general claims, but each study tests one hypothesis derived from it, with one sample, setting and set of measures. A future study may still produce results that do not fit, and because statistics are probabilistic, any single result could be a Type I error. So evidence can support a theory but never prove it. Likewise, a disconfirmed hypothesis does not necessarily disprove the theory: the method, the measures or a Type II error may be responsible.
2. (example answer, for the default proposal) Research literature: studies on sleep and memory consolidation, found in PsycINFO or Google Scholar. Research question: do students who nap after learning remember more than students who stay awake? Empirical study: 20 students learn 40 word pairs, nap or rest awake for the same period, then recall the pairs. Publication: a poster at a student conference, which adds the results to the literature.
3. (example answer) The default proposal is experimental: students are randomly assigned to a nap or an equal period awake (the independent variable), and the number of word pairs recalled is the dependent variable. A study of students' usual hours of sleep and their memory scores would be non-experimental (correlational).
4. (example answer) Usually measures of central tendency (means, sometimes medians) and of variability (standard deviations, ranges), often in a table, plus percentages for categorical variables such as gender.

1. "Theories can be supported but not proved."
2. "Because statistics are probabilistic in nature, we never know whether our conclusions are correct." (*Since* only for time, in APA style; a comma after the introductory clause; optionally, the more formal *whether* for *if*.)

## Writing task
**Your research project: the complete proposal.** Put together everything you wrote in this stage on sleep and memory in university students (or your own topic) as one APA-style student paper of about 2,000 to 3,000 words, plus references and appendices.
1. Assemble the parts in the order shown in the APA box: title page and introduction (Lesson 34), method, planned results and discussion (Lesson 35), references (Lesson 33), and appendices with your consent form (Lesson 09) and your measures (Lesson 12).
2. Write the abstract last (150 to 250 words): the research question, the planned method, the expected results and the main implication.
3. Make the parts consistent: the same title, variable names and operational definitions everywhere; a hypothesis that matches the planned analysis; the same design and invented numbers as in Lessons 27 to 36 (20 students, nap or wake, 40 word pairs), labelled; the future tense for the plan.
4. Revise with your tutor's earlier feedback, proofread with the checklist, and read the paper aloud once.

Self-editing checklist:
- Organization: all sections in APA order; the introduction has an opening, a literature review and a closing.
- The literature review is a balanced argument, and the closing states the hypothesis in operational terms.
- Another researcher could run the study from my method.
- High-level style: formal, straightforward and bias-free; the plan in the future tense; conclusions hedged (*support*, not *prove*).
- Citations: author and year, *et al.* for three or more authors, "&" only inside parentheses in the text; every citation matches a reference.
- References: alphabetical, hanging indents, sentence-case titles, DOIs as https://doi.org/ URLs.
- Format: double spacing, page numbers, bold headings (level 1 centered, level 2 flush left).
- Low-level errors from Lesson 33: numerals for 10 and above, hyphens, *since*, serial commas, abbreviations defined first.
