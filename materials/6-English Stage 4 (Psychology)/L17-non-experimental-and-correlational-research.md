# Lesson 17: Non-Experimental and Correlational Research

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 3: Research Designs*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 6.1: Prelude to Nonexperimental Research; 6.2: Overview of Non-Experimental Research; 6.3: Correlational Research.*

## In this lesson
You will learn when researchers measure variables instead of manipulating them, the main types of non-experimental research, how to read a correlation (Pearson's *r*), and why correlation does not imply causation. You will practise the difference between causal language and the language of association.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Define non-experimental and correlational research and explain when each is preferred.
- Distinguish cross-sectional, longitudinal and cross-sequential studies.
- Interpret the direction and strength of Pearson's *r*, and recognise nonlinear relationships and restriction of range.
- Explain the directionality and third-variable problems.
- Report relationships without claiming causation.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| non-experimental research | research that lacks the manipulation of an independent variable; variables are measured as they naturally occur | Milgram's original obedience study |
| correlational research | measuring two variables, with little or no control of extraneous variables, and assessing the relationship between them | self-esteem and GPA |
| cross-sectional / longitudinal study | comparing pre-existing groups (e.g., ages) / following one group over time | A difference between age groups may be a cohort effect. |
| positive / negative relationship | higher scores on one variable go with higher / lower scores on the other | stress and immune system functioning (negative) |
| Pearson's *r* | a measure of the strength of the correlation between two quantitative variables, from −1.00 to +1.00 | *r* = +.51 |
| nonlinear relationship | as X increases, Y does not change at a constant rate; best described by a curve | sleep and depression |
| restriction of range | a limited range of scores on X or Y in the sample, which makes a relationship look weaker | only 18- to 24-year-olds |
| directionality problem | X and Y are related, but Y may cause X instead of X causing Y | Does happiness cause exercise? |
| third-variable problem | a third variable, Z, may cause both X and Y | physical health |
| is statistically related to / is associated with | language of association, not causation | Higher stress is associated with lower immune system functioning. |
| it stands to reason that | it is logical that | It stands to reason that non-experimental research is appropriate when these conditions are not met. |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
#### Non-experimental research (Sections 6.1-6.2)
The four classic studies in Section 6.1 (Milgram, Loftus and Pickrell, Cacioppo and Petty, Rosenhan) are not experiments. Non-experimental research usually cannot show causation, but it is not less important. It is preferred when:
1. the question is about a **single variable** (How accurate are first impressions?);
2. the relationship is **not thought to be causal** (verbal and mathematical intelligence);
3. the question is causal, but the IV **cannot be manipulated** or participants cannot be randomly assigned (hippocampus damage and long-term memory);
4. the question is **broad and exploratory**, or about an experience (being a working mother diagnosed with depression).

To **explain** causes, experiments are preferred; to **describe** or **predict**, non-experimental research is appropriate. The approaches can work together: Milgram's original study measured one variable (obedience) under the same conditions for everyone; his later experiments manipulated, for example, the distance between participant and confederate.

**Types.** In **correlational research**, two variables are measured and their relationship assessed. **Observational research** observes behaviour without manipulating anything (in Loftus and Pickrell's study, nearly a third of participants "remembered" at least one event that never happened). Studies of change over time:

| Design | How | Strength / limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional | compare age groups at one time | quick, but differences may be **cohort effects** (due to the generation people come from, not age) |
| Longitudinal | follow one group as it ages | better for ageing, but very time-consuming |
| Cross-sequential | follow several age groups for a shorter period (e.g., ages 20-60, for ten years) | immediate comparisons, and it separates age effects from cohort effects |

All of these are **quantitative** (numbers analysed statistically); in **qualitative** research the data are usually non-numerical, such as the notes and hospital records in Rosenhan's study.

**Internal validity** is generally highest in experiments, lower in quasi-experiments and lowest in correlational studies, but the ranges overlap: a poorly designed experiment with many confounds can be lower than a good quasi-experiment.

#### Correlational research (Section 6.3)
Reasons to choose a correlational study:
- to describe and predict a relationship that is not thought to be causal;
- the IV cannot be manipulated (cannabis use and memory: manipulating cannabis use would be unethical);
- to establish the **reliability and validity** of measures (a brief and a long extraversion test); here *independent* and *dependent variable* do not apply;
- **external validity** is often higher, because nothing is manipulated or controlled;
- **converging evidence** with experiments (violent television and aggression).

What makes a study correlational is **how it is conducted**, not the variables or the statistics. Giving the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale to 50 American and 50 Japanese students "feels" like an experiment, but nationality was not manipulated. In the to-do list example, randomly assigning people to make lists makes it an experiment; asking whether they make lists makes it correlational.

#### Pearson's *r*
- In a **scatterplot**, each point shows one person's scores on both variables (stress and physical symptoms: *r* = +.51).
- *r* runs from −1.00 to +1.00; 0 means no relationship (a shapeless "cloud" of points).
- Guidelines: about ±.10 small, ±.30 medium, ±.50 large. The sign shows direction, not strength: +.30 and −.30 are equally strong. Most correlations in psychology are small or moderate.
- *r* can mislead in two situations: **nonlinear relationships** (people who sleep about eight hours are least depressed, so *r* is close to zero even though the relationship is fairly strong; always make a scatterplot first) and **restriction of range** (age and enjoyment of hip hop: *r* = −.77 overall but 0 for 18- to 24-year-olds).

#### Correlation does not imply causation
Chocolate consumption and Nobel prizes correlate (*r* = 0.79), but chocolate does not win prizes. Two reasons:
- **Directionality problem:** exercise may cause happiness, or happiness may cause exercise.
- **Third-variable problem:** European geography may explain both chocolate consumption and investment in education and technology; physical health may cause both exercise and happiness. Such correlations are called **spurious correlations**.

The most effective solution is an experiment: randomly assign people to 15 minutes on a treadmill or on a couch. Then mood cannot have affected how much they exercised, and no third variable can explain the difference.

### Language focus
**Causal language versus the language of association.** A correlational result must be reported with verbs and phrases that describe a relationship:

| Association (correlational studies) | Causation (experiments only) |
|---|---|
| "people under more stress **tend to have** more physical symptoms" | "making the daily to-do lists **reduced** participants' stress" |
| "higher stress **is associated with** lower immune system functioning" | "Lots of Candy **Could Lead to** Violence" |
| "these variables **are statistically related**" | "exercising **causes** happiness" |

Note *tend to* + base verb, and the fixed prepositions *associated* **with**, *correlated* **with**, *related* **to**, *the relationship* **between** *X and Y*.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Prepositions do not translate one to one. Learn each phrase whole: *associated with* (not *associated to*), *correlated with*, *related to*, *a relationship between X and Y*.

## Answer key
### 1. Reading questions
1. A single-variable question; a non-causal relationship; a causal question in which the IV cannot be manipulated or participants cannot be randomly assigned; a broad, exploratory question or one about an experience (6.2, "When to Use Non-Experimental Research").
2. His original study measured one variable, obedience, with all participants under the same conditions; later experiments manipulated IVs such as distance and location (same subsection).
3. Differences between age groups caused by the generation people come from rather than by age. A cross-sequential study follows several age groups for a period, so it can tell whether the original differences are age effects or cohort effects (6.2, "Cross-Sectional, Longitudinal, and Cross-Sequential Studies").
4. The researcher did not manipulate nationality; the variables were measured (6.3, box "Does Correlational Research Always Involve Quantitative Variables?").
5. How it was conducted: random assignment to making lists or not makes it an experiment; simply asking makes it correlational (same box).
6. The sign gives the direction; the absolute value gives the strength. −.40 is stronger (6.3, Pearson's *r* paragraph).
7. *r* is a good measure only for linear relationships; a nonlinear relationship can give *r* near zero (6.3, nonlinear relationships).

### 2. Book questions and invented data
a. (example answer; many answers are possible) Third variables: parenting (for example, parents who use candy as a reward and set few limits), low self-control or impulsivity, or family stress could lead to both daily candy and later violence. Headline: "Children Who Eat Candy Daily More Likely to Be Arrested for Violence Later in Life".
b. 1. Positive: students who sleep more tend to recall more words.
2. Large (above the ±.50 guideline) and positive.
3. Restriction of range: in this narrow range of sleep, the relationship disappears. Recruit students with a wide range of sleep habits, inspect the data and interpret *r* in light of the range.
4. No. Directionality: students with better memories may finish studying sooner and go to bed earlier. Third variable: stress (or caffeine, or workload) could reduce both sleep and recall. Only an experiment can show causation.
5. A nonlinear (upside-down U) relationship, like sleep and depression in the book; Pearson's *r* measures only straight-line relationships, so it would underestimate the relationship.

c. (example answers) (1) Unlikely: a long life cannot make people eat more lobster; third variable: wealth. (2) Present: weighing less may make exercise easier; third variable: age or general health. (3) Present: poor grades may lead students to drink more; third variable: low conscientiousness or a busy social life.

### 3. Language exercise
1. R. 2. C (*has a negative effect on*), although hedged with *perhaps*. 3. R.
4. (example answer) Students who slept more tended to recall more words; sleep duration was positively correlated with recall.
5. (example answer) Children who ate candy every day were more likely to be arrested for a violent offense later in life.

## Writing task
**Research project: a correlational alternative** (Lessons 17-18; see also Lessons 19-22 for other alternatives).

Write 200-250 words describing a correlational version of your study of sleep and memory in university students. Include:
- the two variables and how each will be measured, not manipulated (for example, a one-week sleep diary and a standard word-recall test);
- the expected direction of the relationship, checked with a scatterplot and Pearson's *r*;
- why a correlational design may be useful (real sleep habits, external validity, ethics);
- how you will avoid restriction of range;
- the directionality and third-variable problems, with one example of each;
- one sentence comparing this design with your experiment (internal versus external validity).

Language: association language (*tend to*, *is associated with*, *is positively correlated with*); no causal verbs in the conclusions.

If you work on your own topic, describe a correlational version of your own study.

Self-editing checklist:
- Both variables are measured; nothing is manipulated.
- The predicted direction (positive or negative) is stated.
- Restriction of range and nonlinearity are considered.
- One directionality example and one third variable are given.
- No sentence claims that sleep *improves* or *causes* anything.
