# Lesson 18: Complex Correlation

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 3: Research Designs*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 6.4: Complex Correlation.*

## In this lesson
You will learn how researchers measure several variables at once and analyse them with a correlation matrix, factor analysis, partial correlation and regression, and how far these methods can go towards causal conclusions. You will practise conditional sentences that interpret possible results ("this would suggest that ...").

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Read and build a correlation matrix.
- Explain what factor analysis does and what it does not tell us.
- Explain statistical control with partial correlation, and interpret partial correlations.
- Distinguish predictor and outcome variables and explain what multiple regression adds.
- Interpret hypothetical results with hedged conditional sentences.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| correlation matrix | a table that shows the correlations among several variables | need for cognition, intelligence, social desirability, dogmatism |
| factor analysis | a technique that organises many variables into clusters (factors) with strong correlations within a cluster and weak correlations between clusters | the Big Five personality factors |
| factor loading | how strongly a variable is related to a factor (shown in the book's table) | jazz: .83 on *Reflective and Complex* |
| statistical control | accounting for third variables by measuring them and including them in the analysis | controlling for socioeconomic status (SES) |
| partial correlation | the relationship between two variables while statistically controlling for one or more third variables | violent TV and aggression, controlling for SES |
| regression | a technique that predicts one variable from another | predicting GPA from IQ |
| predictor / outcome (criterion) variable | the variable used to make the prediction / the variable being predicted | IQ / GPA |
| regression weight (*b*) | how much the outcome variable changes for each one-unit change in a predictor | `Y = b1X1` |
| multiple regression | predicting one outcome from several predictors, showing the separate contribution of each | income and health predicting happiness |
| over and above | in addition to what others already explain | a contribution over and above the other predictors |
| this would suggest that | a hedged interpretation of a possible result | That would suggest that SES is indeed a third variable. |
| it is important to note that | introduces a key limitation | It is important to note that partial correlation does not solve the directionality problem. |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
#### Measuring several variables
Complex correlational research measures several variables (binary or continuous) and assesses the relationships among them.
- **Radcliffe and Klein (2002):** more optimistic middle-aged adults were healthier (they exercised more and had lower blood pressure), knew about heart attack risk factors, and correctly believed their own risk to be lower than their peers'.
- **Jouriles et al. (2009):** because measures of physical aggression gave highly skewed distributions, the researchers turned them into binary measures (0 = did not occur, 1 = did occur). Adolescents who experienced physical aggression were moderately likely also to have experienced psychological aggression, and psychological aggression was related to psychological distress.

#### The correlation matrix
Cacioppo and Petty (1982) tested their Need for Cognition Scale (how much people like and value thinking) by correlating it with intelligence, socially desirable responding and dogmatism:

| | Need for cognition | Intelligence | Social desirability | Dogmatism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Need for cognition** | — | | | |
| **Intelligence** | +.39 | — | | |
| **Social desirability** | +.08 | +.02 | — | |
| **Dogmatism** | −.27 | −.23 | +.03 | — |

Only half is filled in, because the other half would repeat the same values; the diagonal has dashes, because a variable always correlates +1.00 with itself. The pattern fitted the researchers' ideas, which supports the validity of the new measure.

#### Factor analysis
Factor analysis organises many conceptually similar variables into a smaller number of clusters; each cluster is interpreted as several measures of one underlying construct (a **factor**). Examples: mental tasks form a mathematical and a verbal intelligence factor; warmth, gregariousness, activity level and positive emotions form extraversion, one of the Big Five. Rentfrow and Gosling asked more than 1,700 students to rate 14 music genres and found four factors: *Reflective and Complex* (blues, jazz, classical, folk), *Intense and Rebellious* (rock, alternative, heavy metal), *Upbeat and Conventional* (country, soundtracks, religious, pop) and *Energetic and Rhythmic* (rap/hip-hop, soul/funk, electronica). (The book dates this study 2008 in the text and 2003 in the table; the article was published in 2003.)

Two points:
1. **Factors are not categories.** People are not *either* extraverted *or* conscientious; factors operate independently.
2. **Factor analysis only reveals structure.** Researchers must interpret and label the factors and explain their origin (for example, the Big Five appear to be controlled by different genes).

#### Exploring causal relationships: partial correlation
Correlational research cannot establish causation unambiguously, but it can **rule out** some interpretations by **statistical control**: measuring possible third variables and including them in the analysis. In **partial correlation**, researchers examine the relationship between two variables while controlling for a third.

The book's example: violent television viewing and aggression correlate +.35 (moderate). The researcher also measures SES and controls for it:

| Partial correlation (controlling for SES) | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| +.34 | The relationship is largely independent of SES; SES is not a third variable driving it. |
| +.03 | SES is indeed a third variable driving the relationship. |
| +.20 | SES accounts for some, but not all, of the relationship. |

Limits: partial correlation does not solve the **directionality problem**, and there may be other third variables that the researcher did not measure.

#### Regression
Once a relationship is established, **regression** predicts one variable from another (IQ predicting GPA). The book gives the simple form `Y = b1X1`: the predicted score equals the person's score on the predictor multiplied by the **regression weight**. (Full regression equations usually also contain a constant, the intercept; the book's simplified form leaves it out.) **Multiple regression** uses several predictors:

`Y = b1X1 + b2X2 + b3X3 + ... + biXi`

Its advantage: it shows whether a predictor contributes **over and above** the others. Income and health are related to each other, so either might seem to predict happiness only because of the other; multiple regression shows the contribution of each with the other statistically controlled. Such research has shown that both make extremely small contributions to happiness, except in severe poverty or illness (Diener, 2000).

Purely correlational approaches can only show patterns "consistent with some causal interpretations and inconsistent with others".

### Language focus
**Interpreting possible results with conditionals.** The section imagines several results and interprets each with *if* + past tense, *would* + verb:
- "If she found that the partial correlation ... was +.34, that **would suggest that** the relationship ... is largely independent of SES."
- "If, however, she found that statistically controlling for SES reduced the magnitude of the correlation from +.35 to +.20, then this **would suggest that** SES accounts for some, but not all, of the relationship."

It then states limits with a fixed frame: "**It is important to note that** while partial correlation provides an important tool ..., researchers using this technique are still limited in their ability to arrive at causal conclusions."

Use *would suggest* (not *proves*) for planned or hypothetical results, and keep *if* + past simple, not *if* + *would*.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Do not put *would* in the *if* clause: write "If the correlation **dropped** to +.03, this **would** suggest ...", not "If the correlation would drop ...".

Self-editing checklist:
- Each third variable has a clear reason and a measure.
- The matrix has every variable in both rows and columns.
- Predictor and outcome variables are named correctly.
- At least two possible results are interpreted.
- No *would* in an *if* clause.
- Causal language is avoided in the conclusions.
