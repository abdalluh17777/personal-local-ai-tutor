# Lesson 27: Describing Single Variables

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 4: Statistics in Research Reports*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 12.1 Describing Single Variables.*

## In this lesson
Before researchers test relationships, they describe each variable on its own: its distribution, its central tendency, its variability and the position of single scores. You will compute these statistics and practise the English for describing distributions and for reading formulas and numbers aloud.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Display a distribution in a frequency table and describe its shape in words.
- Compute the mean, median and mode, and explain why the median suits highly skewed distributions.
- Compute the range, standard deviation, percentile ranks and *z* scores, and explain why software divides by *N* − 1.
- Read formulas aloud and report descriptive statistics in academic sentences.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| descriptive statistics | techniques for summarizing and displaying data | It is also important to describe each variable individually. |
| distribution | the way the scores on a variable are distributed across its levels | the distribution of "number of siblings" |
| frequency table / histogram | a table / a graph showing each value and how often it occurs | the Rosenberg self-esteem table |
| skewed | asymmetrical: positively skewed = long positive tail; negatively skewed = long negative tail | reaction times |
| outlier | an extreme score far removed from the rest | one clinically depressed person among happy peers |
| central tendency | the middle of a distribution (mean, median, mode) | the point around which the scores tend to cluster |
| mean (*M*) | the sum of the scores divided by the number of scores | The mean is 245 ms. |
| median | the value with half the scores below it and half above | In 2 3 3 4 8 12 14, the median is 4. |
| mode | the most frequently occurring score | The mode is 22. |
| variability; range | how much scores vary around the middle; highest minus lowest score | The range is 24 − 15 = 9. |
| standard deviation (*SD*); variance (*SD*²) | the square root of the mean of the squared differences between the scores and the mean; the variance is that mean | *SD*² = 3.50; *SD* = 1.87 |
| percentile rank | the percentage of scores below a particular score | a percentile rank of 80 |
| *z* score | the score minus the mean, divided by the standard deviation | (110 − 100) / 15 = +0.67 |
| tend to cluster around | are mostly close to | The self-esteem scores tend to cluster around the values of 20 to 22. |
| ... standard deviations above / below the mean | distance from the mean in *SD* units | 85 is one standard deviation below the mean. |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**Displaying a distribution.** A **frequency table** lists values from highest (top) to lowest and usually stops at the highest and lowest scores observed (the Rosenberg table shows 24 to 15, though the scale runs from 0 to 30). With many different scores, a **grouped** table uses ranges of equal width, usually five to 15 of them. A **histogram** shows the same information (*x*-axis: values; *y*-axis: frequency), without gaps between bars for quantitative variables (the gap at 17 means nobody scored 17) and with small gaps for categorical ones.

**Shape.** A distribution can be **unimodal** or **bimodal**, and **symmetrical** (mirror-image halves) or **skewed**. A *negatively* skewed distribution has its peak toward the upper end and a long negative tail; a *positively* skewed one has its peak toward the lower end and a long positive tail. An **outlier** may be a truly extreme score or an error (a misunderstanding, an equipment malfunction).

**Central tendency**

| Measure | How to find it | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| mean, *M* = Σ*X* / *N* | add the scores, divide by their number | most common; useful in inferential statistics |
| median | order the scores and take the middle one; with an even number, the value halfway between the two middle scores (2 3 3 4 8 12 14 15: 6) | highly skewed distributions |
| mode | the most frequent score | the only measure for categorical variables |

In a unimodal symmetrical distribution the three are close; in a skewed one the mean is pulled toward the longer tail. **Book example:** reaction times of 200, 250, 280 and 250 ms have a mean of 245 ms. Adding a score of 5,000 ms raises the mean to 5,980 / 5 = **1,196 ms** (the book prints 1,445 ms, a calculation error). The point still holds: this mean is greater than 80% of the scores, while the median (250 ms) represents the group well.

**Variability.** The **range** is misleading with outliers (exam scores of 90 to 100 have a range of 10; one score of 20 makes it 80). The **standard deviation** is roughly the average distance between the scores and the mean: *SD* = √[Σ(*X* − *M*)² / *N*]. Subtract the mean from each score, square each difference, take the mean of the squares (the **variance**), then the square root. Book example: 3, 5, 4, 2, 7, 6, 5, 8 (*M* = 5): sum of squares 28, *SD*² = 28/8 = 3.50, *SD* = 1.87. Dividing by *N* describes the sample; software divides by *N* − 1 because a sample's *SD* tends to be a little lower than the population's (here 28/7 = 4.00, *SD* = 2.00).

**Where is one score?** The **percentile rank** of 23 in the self-esteem table is 80, because 32 of the 40 scores are lower. A ***z* score** = (*X* − *M*) / *SD*: with IQ scores (*M* = 100, *SD* = 15), 110 gives +0.67 and 85 gives −1.00. Outliers are sometimes defined as *z* scores beyond ±3.00. **PSYC 110:** in Lecture 13 (Why Are People Different?), Paul Bloom explains that IQ tests are scored so that 100 is average and 68% of people score between 85 and 115, that is, between *z* = −1.00 and +1.00.

### Language focus
**1. Reading formulas and numbers aloud**

| Written | Spoken |
|---|---|
| *M* = Σ*X* / *N* | "*M* equals the sum of *X* divided by *N*" (Σ: "sigma") |
| √3.50 = 1.87 | "the square root of three point five oh equals one point eight seven" |
| *z* = −1.00 | "*z* equals minus one point oh oh" (or "negative one") |

**2. Describing a distribution.** The book joins a statistic to its meaning:
- "The self-esteem scores **tend to cluster around** the values of 20 to 22."
- "The distribution ... is unimodal, **meaning** it has one distinct peak." (*meaning* + definition)
- "A score of 110 is 0.67 standard deviations (**approximately two thirds of** a standard deviation) **above the mean**."

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - English uses a decimal **point** (1.87, "one point eight seven") and a **comma** to group thousands (5,000). Never write 1,87.

## Answer key
**Part 1**
1. From highest to lowest. They usually stop at the highest and lowest scores in the data, and this data set only has scores from 15 to 24.
2. When there are many different scores across a wide range. The ranges must be of equal width, usually five to 15 of them.
3. (example answer) Most scores are high, so the peak is near the top of the range, and a few low scores form a long tail toward the low end.
4. Reaction times are often highly skewed: one very slow response (an inattentive participant) pulls the mean far from most scores but hardly affects the median.
5. One extreme score changes it greatly: the exam range goes from 10 to 80 although only one student differed.
6. A sample's *SD* tends to be a bit lower than the population's; dividing by *N* − 1 corrects this and better estimates the population *SD*.
7. 8 + 5 + 3 + 3 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 22 scores are lower; 22/40 = 55%, so the percentile rank is 55.
8. *M* = 5,980 / 5 = 1,196 ms; median = 250 ms. The book's 1,445 ms is wrong, but its conclusion stands: 1,196 is greater than 80% (4 of 5) of the scores.

**Part 2**
1. Frequency table (score: frequency): 17: 1; 16: 0; 15: 0; 14: 2; 13: 3; 12: 5; 11: 3; 10: 2; 9: 2; 8: 1; 7: 0; 6: 1. Histogram: tallest bar at 12; no bars at 7, 15 and 16. Shape (example answer): unimodal, with a peak at 12, and roughly symmetrical; most scores lie between 9 and 14, with single scores of 6 and 17 at the ends (17 is not an outlier by the ±3.00 rule: *z* = +2.36).
2. *M* = 229 / 20 = 11.45; median = 12; mode = 12; range = 17 − 6 = 11; *SD* = 2.36 dividing by *N* (2.42 dividing by *N* − 1).
3. Percentile ranks: 9 → 10 (2 of 20 scores lower); 14 → 85 (17 of 20 lower). *z* scores (*SD* = 2.36): 8 → (8 − 11.45) / 2.36 = −1.46; 12 → +0.23. (With *SD* = 2.42: −1.43 and +0.23.)

**Part 3** (example answers)
- A1. The mode was 22: more students had this score than any other.
- A2. An IQ score of 130 is two standard deviations above the mean (*z* = +2.00).
- A3. Students who scored 23 scored at the 80th percentile.
- A4. The distribution is positively skewed, with its peak toward the lower end of its range and a relatively long positive tail.
- B. (a) "*M* equals the sum of *X* divided by *N*." (b) "*z* equals eighty-five minus one hundred, divided by fifteen, which equals minus one point oh oh."
