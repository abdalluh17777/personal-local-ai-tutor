# Lesson 33: Pronunciation: Thought Groups

*English Stage 1 (B1) · Unit 4: Education and Creativity*
*Adapted from Speaking, Listening and Pronunciation Projects for ELLs - Intermediate Level by Brittany Zemlick, Grossmont College (CC BY 4.0): 4.5: Thought Groups.*

## In this lesson
English speakers divide what they say into short phrases called thought groups. In this lesson you learn to hear the pauses between thought groups and the one strong word in each group. You practice with four recordings you know from this course, and you record yourself reading part of Sir Ken Robinson's story.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain what a thought group and a focus word are.
- Mark the pauses (/) in a transcript while you listen.
- Find the focus word in each thought group.
- Use the right intonation on the focus word: up on the focus word, then down at the end of the group.
- Read a paragraph aloud with natural pauses and record yourself.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning in simple English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| thought group | a short phrase with one idea; you say it together and then pause | While I love learning English, / I don't have time to do the homework, / ... |
| pause | a short stop when you speak | Thought groups are usually followed by a brief pause. |
| brief | short | a brief pause |
| focus word | the word with the strongest stress and the most importance in a thought group | taking tests makes me **NER·vous** |
| stress | saying a word or syllable more strongly than the others | Which words are stressed in sentence 2? |
| intonation | the music of speech: the voice goes up and down | The intonation usually goes up on the focus word. |
| transcript | the written words of a recording | Listen to the recording and read the transcript. |
| underline | draw a line under a word | Underline the stressed word in each thought group. |
| emphasize | say a word more strongly | Emphasize the most important word in each thought group. |
| slash ( / ) | the mark you write where the speaker pauses | Write a "/" when he pauses. |

## Rules and explanations

### What is a thought group?
- English speakers divide language into phrases called thought groups.
- Compare the book's warm-up sentence. The second version has pauses between the thought groups:
  1. While I love learning English, I don't have time to do the homework, and taking tests makes me nervous.
  2. While I love learning English, / I don't have time to do the homework, / and taking tests makes me nervous.

### Rules to remember
- A thought group usually has **one focus word**.
- The focus word (the word with the strongest stress and the most importance) is **often at the end** of the thought group.
- The intonation usually **goes up on the focus word** and then **down at the end** of each thought group.
- A thought group is usually followed by a **brief pause**.

### How to say a thought group
- Say the words inside a group together, without stopping.
- Make the focus word stronger. Then pause briefly and start the next group.
- Commas often show a pause, as in the warm-up sentence. Keep the words of one phrase together, for example "a learning disorder" or "to her parents".

### How to mark a transcript
- Draw a / each time the speaker pauses.
- Underline the focus word in each thought group. In this guide, **bold** shows the focus word, and CAPITALS show its stressed syllable.

## Answer key
**1.** The book gives no answer. Sentence 2 has pauses between the thought groups, and that is what makes speech easier to understand. For the stressed words, check what you hear in the recording. The rules in this lesson say the focus word is often the last important word of each group: here that would be ENG·lish, HOME·work and NER·vous. Focus words are stressed because they carry the most important information.

**2.** No answer needed.

**3.** The book gives no answer key. Your marks must follow what you hear in each video. Then read your marked transcript aloud to your tutor and ask if the pauses sound natural.

**4.** The book gives no answer key: mark the pauses that you hear in the video. For your own reading, here is one natural way to divide the paragraph (example answer). It is not a record of Sir Ken Robinson's pauses.
Gillian and I had **LUNCH** one day. / I said, / "How did you get to be a **DAN·cer**?" / It was **IN·ter·est·ing**. / When she was at **SCHOOL**, / she was really **HOPE·less**. / And the **SCHOOL**, / in the **'30s**, / wrote to her **PAR·ents** / and **SAID**, / "We think Gillian has a learning **dis·OR·der**." / She couldn't **CON·cen·trate**; / she was **FID·get·ing**. / I think now / they'd say she had **ADHD**. / Wouldn't **YOU**? / But this was the **1930s**, / and ADHD hadn't been **in·VENT·ed** / at this **POINT**. / It wasn't an **a·VAIL·a·ble** condition. / People weren't **a·WARE** they could have that.
Note: "the '30s" and "the 1930s" are said "the thirties" and "the nineteen thirties". ADHD is said as four letters: A-D-H-D.

## Speak with your tutor
- "Read this sentence two ways, without pauses and then with pauses: While I love learning English, I don't have time to do the homework, and taking tests makes me nervous. Ask me which way was easier to understand."
- "I will read the Ken Robinson paragraph with pauses. Tell me if any pause sounded strange or if I stopped in the middle of a phrase."
- "Ask me three questions about my school days. I will answer in long sentences and pause between thought groups. Tell me if my answers were easy to follow."
- "I will retell what Andy does in his job, in short thought groups. Then ask me one question about it."
