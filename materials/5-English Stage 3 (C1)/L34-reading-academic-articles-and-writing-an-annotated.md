# Lesson 34: Reading Academic Articles and Writing an Annotated Bibliography

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 3.8 Reading Academic Articles for Research; 3.9 Writing an Annotated Bibliography; 3.10 Language Toolkit; 3.11 Takeaways.*

## In this lesson
You learn how academic and popular sources differ, what the parts of a journal article are, and how fast to read each part, using an article that Lily used. Then you study Lily's annotated bibliography and write one for your project. These skills save time and help you plan a paper.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Distinguish academic and popular sources.
- Name the parts of a journal article and decide how carefully to read each one.
- Write annotated bibliography entries with a citation, a summary and an evaluation.
- Ask for research help clearly and politely.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| academic (scholarly) source | contains original research; written by experts for other academic readers | Han and Onchwari's journal article |
| popular source | a newspaper or magazine article for a general audience | the EdSource article by Peele and Willis |
| credentials | qualifications that show expertise | Insoon Han, PhD |
| abstract | a short summary at the start of an article | "This paper describes a mentoring program ..." |
| literature review | the part of an article that reports what others have already written | "What have others already said about my topic?" |
| annotated bibliography | a list of sources (entries), each with a full citation and notes (an annotation): summary and evaluation | Lily's annotated bibliography |
| working thesis | a first thesis that may change as you research | Although minority faculty members play an important role ... |
| reporting verb | a verb that introduces what a source says | explain, claim, suggest, state, report |
| This source seems credible because ... | an evaluation frame | ... Han and Onchwari are professors of education. |
| I was wondering if you could assist me with ... | a polite request | ... with evaluating my sources. |

## Rules and explanations

### Academic and popular sources (Table 3.8.1)
| Feature | Academic | Popular |
|---|---|---|
| Content | original research data, such as scientific studies | popular-interest topics, or summaries of research done by others |
| Authors | expert scholars, with credentials listed | often journalists or writers |
| Readers | scholars, researchers | general public |
| Purpose | to share research findings and expand knowledge | to inform or entertain |
| Style | straightforward design, complex language | flashy design, accessible language |
| Citations and review | citations; peer-reviewed | few or no citations; not peer-reviewed |

Both are useful: academic sources give specific, researched facts; popular sources give current news, opinions and examples.

### Parts of a journal article and how to read them (Table 3.8.2)
The lesson material lists the usual sections and what each contains: Title, Author Information, Abstract, Introduction or Background, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References. Not every article has every section. You do not need to read everything slowly:

| How to read | Parts |
|---|---|
| carefully | Title, Abstract, Background or Literature Review, Discussion, Conclusion |
| somewhat carefully | Introduction |
| quickly | Authors (good credentials?) |
| very quickly | Methods, Results (mostly for other researchers), References (anything else to read?) |

Do not cite from the abstract: find the relevant part of the article and cite that.

### The annotated bibliography (3.9)
- It helps you organize sources before you write and think critically about how reliable and useful they are.
- Each entry has a **complete citation** and one paragraph with a **summary** and an **evaluation**. Lily also puts her working thesis at the top.
- **Summary:** the first sentence gives the author's name + a reporting verb, the main idea, and the type of source. Frames: *In this [type of source], [author's name] explains [main idea].* / *According to [author's name] in this [type of source], [main idea].* / *[Author's name] claims in this [type of source] that [main idea].*
- **Evaluation:** How credible is the source? Are there biases? Why is it relevant to your topic? How will you use it? Frames: *This source seems credible because ...* / *This source may be considered biased because ...* / *The research strongly supports my research question because ...* / *I plan to use this in the [introduction/background/cause/effect, etc.] section of my paper to show ...*

### Asking for help (3.10)
Use a sentence starter + a gerund (Table 3.10.2): *I need some help with / I am having trouble with / I was wondering if you could assist me with* + *narrowing down my topic, finding key word synonyms, citing this information correctly ...* Also say what you have already done. Your tutor can play the librarian.

> **Psychology note:** In APA style, the list is called References and each entry gives the year after the authors. Han and Onchwari's article in APA: Han, I., & Onchwari, J. A. (2018). Development and implementation of a culturally responsive mentoring program for faculty and staff of color. *Interdisciplinary Journal of Partnership Studies, 5*(2), Article 3. https://doi.org/10.24926/ijps.v5i2.1006 (The book's MLA citation, like the journal's web page, gives the second author's initials as "A. J."; the article itself names her Jacqueline Ariri Onchwari, so use "J. A.")

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic script has no capital letters, so capitals in titles are easy to forget. MLA capitalizes the main words of every title ("Maintaining Heritage Language: Perspectives of Korean Parents"). In APA article titles, capitalize only the first word, the first word after a colon, and names; in journal names, capitalize the main words.

## Read aloud
Stressed syllables are in CAPITALS. Pause briefly at each /.
1. An annotated bib·li·OG·ra·phy / is a list of sources / with a summary and an evaluation of each.
2. In their academic article, / Han and Onchwari describe a mentoring program / that was set up to support faculty of color / at a university that is mostly white.
3. This is a credible article / because Han and Onchwari are professors of education.
4. I was wondering if you could assist me / with evaluating my sources.

## Writing task
Write an annotated bibliography for your research project from Lessons 32 and 33 (for example, sleep and memory in university students).
- At the top: your name and your working thesis (or your research question).
- Three to five sources, at least two of them scholarly journal articles, in alphabetical order.
- Each entry: a complete citation (MLA as in the book, or APA for psychology), then a paragraph of 100 to 150 words with a summary and an evaluation (credibility, bias, relevance, planned use).

Self-editing checklist:
- Is every citation complete (author, title, publication, date, pages or DOI) and correctly capitalized?
- Does each summary begin with the author, a reporting verb, the type of source and the main idea?
- Did I summarize in my own words, with any exact words in quotation marks?
- Did I explain why each source is credible (or what its limits are)?
- Did I say how each source relates to my research question and where I will use it?
