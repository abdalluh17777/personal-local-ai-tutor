# Lesson 36: Choosing Evidence and Working from Sources

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 4.5 Choosing Appropriate Evidence; 4.6 Working from Sources.*

## In this lesson
You learn to choose the evidence that really supports your point, not just evidence about the same topic. You also learn to treat sources as a conversation ("They Say / I Say"): report what published writers say, check the context of their words, then add your own thinking. Every university paper needs evidence and the writer's own ideas to fit together like this.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Choose the evidence that supports a specific point most directly.
- Balance source ideas and your own ideas with the "They Say / I Say" approach.
- Use five strategies for combining sources in your own argument.
- Check the context of a quotation so that you do not misrepresent a source.
- Decide when to use a short quotation, a paraphrase or a summary.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| evidence | facts, findings, quotations or examples from sources that support a point | an interview study of California employers |
| point (claim) | the idea you want the reader to accept | Bilingual children of immigrants have an economic advantage as adults. |
| directly support | prove the exact point, not just talk about the same topic | Quotation 3 directly supports the point. |
| They Say / I Say | first report what others say, then respond with your own view | Vince reports ...; I agree, but ... |
| implications | the possible future results of a finding | warn about the future consequences of language loss |
| underlying | not obvious; under the surface | identify underlying areas of agreement |
| out of context | taken away from the words around it, so the meaning changes | quoting a view the author rejects |
| misrepresent | describe someone's ideas falsely | Inexperienced writers sometimes misrepresent their sources. |
| refute | show that an idea is wrong | The author goes on to refute it. |
| block quote | a long quotation set apart from your text, indented, without quotation marks | used only with a good reason |

## Rules and explanations

### Evidence must support your exact point
Evidence about the same topic is not enough: it must be directly connected to your point. Ask:
- Does it address the exact claim (for example, an **economic** advantage **as adults**, not bilingualism in general)?
- Does it add something more specific, or does it only repeat your point?
- If it contains a statistic, is the statistic about your point? Numbers are tempting, but a number about something else is not evidence.
- Good evidence for one paragraph may be the wrong evidence for another.

### Joining the conversation: They Say / I Say
A research paper joins a conversation that expert writers have already started. First you report what "they" say (published authors, common ideas in society, people in a public debate). Then you respond: do you agree, disagree, or a little of both? You may cite a finding as evidence for your point, or summarize an idea to show how your view differs or how the idea applies to a new situation.

### Two mistakes to avoid
| Mistake | The book's example |
|---|---|
| Your claims go beyond your sources. | Your research shows that children who keep their home language do better on cognitive tests, but you claim they also have higher high school graduation rates. The second claim needs its own evidence. |
| Only sources, no you. | The paper only quotes, summarizes and paraphrases. A research paper is not just a report: it is organized around your own thinking, supported by your research. |

### Five strategies for combining sources with your ideas
- a. Combine findings from several sources to make a larger summary argument.
- b. Combine findings from several sources to make a claim about their implications.
- c. Identify underlying areas of agreement.
- d. Identify underlying areas of disagreement.
- e. Identify unanswered questions.

The book's example for each strategy is in Exercise 3. When you cite sources you agree with, choose quotations or paraphrases that work as building blocks in your own argument.

### Respect the context
Writers misrepresent sources when they quote a very minor point, something mentioned in passing, or a position the author disagrees with. This often happens when you read sources only to find sentences that match your own opinion. Before you quote, read the whole paragraph and ask: is this the author's own view?

### Use sources efficiently
- You can quote or paraphrase whole sentences or just phrases. Most quotations should be short: key terms, phrases, parts of sentences or single sentences.
- A long passage of more than one sentence can be a **block quote**: indented, with no quotation marks. (More exactly: MLA uses a block quote only for more than four lines of prose, APA for 40 words or more.) A long quotation needs a good reason to be there.
- Every part of a quotation should feel necessary. Many long quotations usually mean that your own argument is undeveloped.
- A good college research paper typically includes few direct quotations. Paraphrasing is harder, and quoting can feel safer, but a paraphrase usually fits most smoothly into your writing.

> **Psychology note:** Psychology papers use APA style. Writers paraphrase most source material, and an in-text citation includes the year: (Callahan, 2015) instead of the book's MLA (Callahan).

Which quotation provides the best support? Say why each of the others is weaker.
1. "For all the research that supports childhood bilingualism, it is only recently that scholars have begun to understand bilingualism in adults' professional lives."
2. "Historically notorious for their English monolingualism, a recent Gallup poll reports that in this nation of immigrants, only one in four American adults now reports being conversationally proficient in another language."

Even when being bilingual is not a requirement, an interview study of California employers shows that employers prefer to both hire and retain bilinguals."
4. "Research links bilingualism to greater intellectual focus, as well as a delay in the onset of dementia symptoms. Frequent use of multiple languages is also linked to development of greater empathy."

## Writing task
**Your research project: evidence for one claim.** Use your research question and annotated bibliography from Lessons 32 to 34. No project yet? Choose the book's topic (keeping a heritage language) or a psychology topic, such as sleep and memory in university students, or stress and coping among international students, and find two or three sources in Google Scholar, PubMed Central, ERIC, the Directory of Open Access Journals or the free OpenStax "Psychology 2e" textbook.
1. Write one claim (one sentence) that will be the point of a body paragraph in your paper.
2. Find three pieces of evidence for it in at least two of your sources. For each one, note the exact words (and page number, if there is one), the source, whether you will quote or paraphrase, and one sentence explaining how it supports your claim.
3. Check the context of each piece of evidence.
4. Write a paragraph of 150-200 words in They Say / I Say form: report what your sources say (at least one short quotation and one paraphrase, each with an in-text citation), then add your own response using one of the five strategies.

Self-editing checklist:
- Each piece of evidence supports my exact claim, not just my topic.
- Each one adds something more specific than my claim.
- Every statistic is directly about my claim.
- I am not quoting a view the author rejects or a point made only in passing.
- My quotations are short, and I paraphrase more than I quote.
- My claims do not go beyond what my sources show.
- My own thinking is clear, and I know which strategy I used.
- Every quotation and paraphrase has an in-text citation.
