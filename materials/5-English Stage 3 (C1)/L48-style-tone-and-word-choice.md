# Lesson 48: Style, Tone and Word Choice

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 5: Argument and Academic Style*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 6.9 Writing with Style; 6.10 Reading for and Writing with Tone; 6.11 Choosing Effective Words; and from College ESL Writers: Applied Grammar and Composing Strategies for Success by Barbara Hall and Elizabeth Wallace, Georgia State University - Perimeter College (CC BY-NC-SA): 6.3 Word Choice.*

## In this lesson
You will learn what a writer's style is and work on two of its three parts: tone and word choice. You will compare a magazine article with a research article, judge a student's tone and word choice, and practise connotation, formal vocabulary, fresh and specific language, and antonyms. These skills make your university essays sound serious, precise and consistent.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Describe the tone of a text with precise adjectives and judge whether it fits the context, purpose and audience.
- Keep an appropriate and consistent tone in academic writing.
- Use a dictionary and a thesaurus to choose words with the right denotation and connotation.
- Replace slang, clichés, overly general words and weak "to be" verbs with precise, formal language.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| style | the choices a writer makes to engage the reader and fit the context, purpose, content and audience | Tone, word choice and sentence variety are strategies for writing with style. |
| tone | the attitude a writer shows toward the subject (also called "voice") | The research article has a formal, factual tone. |
| jarring | sudden and unpleasant; used for a break in tone | a sarcastic comment in a professional essay |
| denotation | the dictionary definition of a word | Scrawny, skinny and lean have similar denotations. |
| connotation | the emotional or cultural meaning of a word: positive, negative or neutral | Scrawny has a negative connotation. |
| slang | informal, nonstandard words that change with fashion | "I still bombed" (= I still failed) |
| cliché | an expression that has lost its effect through overuse | "...makes my blood boil" |
| overly general word | a word that gives the reader no clear picture | "My new puppy is cute." |
| synonym | a word with the same, or almost the same, meaning | easy / simple; city / metropolis |
| antonym | a word with the opposite meaning | abundant / scarce; conceal / reveal |
| thesaurus | a reference that lists synonyms and antonyms | Look for alternatives in a thesaurus. |
| etymology | the history of a word | myth: from Greek mythos, "a fable" |

## Rules and explanations
### Style: three strategies
A writer's style is what sets their writing apart. The book gives three strategies:
1. intentionally creating an appropriate **tone**;
2. choosing **words** that illustrate and enliven your writing;
3. using a **variety of sentence types** (Lesson 49).

### Tone
Tone is the attitude your words show toward your subject. The photo caption "Way cool! This used to be a liquor store in my neighborhood. Now it's a great little grocery!" has a casual, enthusiastic tone: perfect for someone walking past the store, wrong in a statistical report.

| Tones that usually fit academic writing | Tones that usually do not |
|---|---|
| formal, serious, professional; dry, factual, direct; incisive, critical, thoughtful; cautious | informal, casual, conversational, breezy; humorous, witty; sarcastic, flippant; angry, bitter, cynical |

When you revise, ask:
- **What is your tone?** Is it how you intend to sound?
- **Is it appropriate** for the context, purpose and audience?
- **Is it consistent?** Look for jarring breaks: slang, a very informal phrase or a sarcastic comment in an otherwise professional text.

### Word choice for style
- Replace "to be" verbs (is, was, were) with more action-packed verbs where you can.
- Replace vague words such as "things", "very" and "many" with precise terms.
- If you repeat a word or phrase too much, find alternatives in a thesaurus, then check them in a dictionary.

### Dictionary and thesaurus
A dictionary gives a word's spelling, pronunciation, part of speech, definition, synonyms and etymology (see the entry for **myth** in your material). A thesaurus lists synonyms and antonyms: **precocious**: uncommonly smart, mature, advanced, smart, bright, brilliant, gifted, quick, clever, apt. Ant. slow, backward, stupid.

### Denotation and connotation
**Scrawny**, **skinny** and **lean** all mean "very thin", but their connotations differ:
- "Although he was a premature baby and a **scrawny** child, Martin has developed into a strong man." (negative: weakness)
- "**Skinny** jeans have become very fashionable in the past couple of years." (positive: fashion)
- "My brother has a **lean** figure, whereas I have a more muscular build." (neutral)

### Language to avoid in academic writing
| Problem | Book's example | Better |
|---|---|---|
| **Slang** (fine between friends, in texts and e-mails) | "I still bombed." | I still failed. |
| **Cliché** (overused, so unoriginal) | ...he always says something that makes my **blood boil**. | ...makes me want to go to the gym and punch the bag for a few hours. |
| **Overly general words** | My teacher told us that plagiarism is bad. | My teacher, Ms. Atwater, created a presentation detailing exactly how plagiarism is illegal and unethical. |

### Synonyms and antonyms
- Synonyms are rarely fully interchangeable: **insipid** means nearly the same as **boring** but sounds more scholarly, perhaps pretentious.
- Learning antonyms in pairs (absence / presence, borrow / lend, permanent / temporary) sharpens your sense of language. Study the table of common antonyms in your material.

> **In psychology:** in research writing (APA style), "significant" usually means "statistically significant", as in the Chen et al. excerpt. When you only mean "important" or "large", use those words.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic often adds emphasis by repeating an idea with two near-synonyms or a colourful fixed expression. In English academic writing this sounds overstated: choose one precise word.
> - Translated proverbs and set phrases usually become clichés in English. Say the idea plainly.

## Writing task
**Part A (the book's Writing Application).** In 100-150 words, describe your favourite dish. Use a thesaurus and as many synonyms as you can, including words you have never used before.

**Part B (Apply this).** Take a paragraph of 150-250 words from one of your essays in this course, or write a new academic paragraph on a psychology topic (how the food environment affects what people eat, from the PSYC 110 guest lecture on food, or how sleep affects memory). Revise it for tone and word choice; keep both versions.

Self-editing checklist:
- Is my tone formal, serious and consistent, with no slang, exclamations or sarcasm?
- Does it fit my purpose and audience?
- Have I replaced "to be" verbs with action verbs where possible?
- Have I replaced vague words ("things", "very", "many") with precise terms?
- Have I removed clichés and overly general words?
- Does every word have the connotation I intend?
- Have I avoided repeating words, and checked each new synonym in a dictionary?
