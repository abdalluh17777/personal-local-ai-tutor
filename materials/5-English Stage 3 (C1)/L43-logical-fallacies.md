# Lesson 43: Logical Fallacies

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 5: Argument and Academic Style*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 5.7 Finding and Refuting Logical Fallacies.*

## In this lesson
You will learn twelve common logical fallacies, grouped by the appeal they misuse (ethos, pathos or logos), and name them in short arguments. Then you will use a three-sentence pattern to refute a fallacy in academic language. Spotting false causes and hasty generalizations is especially useful in psychology, where many claims rest on correlations between two events.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain what a logical fallacy is and why it is more than a false fact.
- Recognize twelve common fallacies and the appeal each one misuses.
- Explain why a correlation between two events does not prove that one caused the other.
- Name, explain and refute a fallacy in three sentences of academic English.

## Rules and explanations

### What is a logical fallacy?
- A logical fallacy is an error in reasoning. Some arguers use fallacies on purpose to manipulate people, but more often we make these mistakes by accident.
- A fallacy is **not just a false fact**. It is a flawed structure of claim + reason + evidence. A writer can state an idea that may be true but support it with illogical reasons.
- Some fallacies are bad logic: a syllogism with a missing piece (Lesson 42). Others use pathos or ethos **in place of** logos. Good arguments do use pathos and ethos; the problem is an argument that pretends to be logical but rests only on them.
- One flawed argument can fit more than one fallacy, and some have no clear label. That is OK.

### Twelve common fallacies
| Fallacy | What happens | Example (shortened from the book) |
|---|---|---|
| **Misused ethos:** | **we believe the source, not the evidence** | |
| appeal to false authority | an authority's opinion replaces an argument; the "expert" lacks credentials in the field, disagrees with most experts, or is biased (for example, has a financial stake) | Dr. X is an engineer, and he doesn't believe in global warming. |
| ad hominem | attacks the person, not the argument | That CEO advocates for organic cotton - she probably owns stock in a company that makes alternative pesticides. |
| bandwagon / appeal to popularity | many people do or believe it, so it must be right | Everyone wears a new outfit every weekend. |
| appeal to tradition | it has always been done this way, so it is right | College classes have always been taught mostly by lecture. |
| **Misused pathos:** | **fear, anger, pity, love or the wish to belong replace logic** | |
| appeal to emotion | seeks an emotional response instead of a valid argument | Without this insurance, you could find yourself broke and homeless. |
| slippery slope | claims without enough support that A will inevitably lead to Z | If we legalize marijuana, people will be strung out on heroin. |
| **Misused logos** | | |
| circular reasoning / begging the question | a premise is the same as the claim it should prove | This legislation is impractical because we cannot possibly make the changes needed. |
| anecdotal evidence / hasty generalization | a personal experience or isolated example replaces a valid argument, often to dismiss statistics (hasty = too fast, not careful) | My grandmother smoked for 80 years and never got cancer. |
| false cause / post hoc | two events are related, so one is assumed to cause the other | People got sick after drinking soda at the festival, so the soda made them sick. |
| false dilemma / false dichotomy | only two choices are presented when more exist | Either you agree with my decision to go to war, or you agree with our enemy. |
| false analogy | two situations with something in common are treated as alike, so they "should" have the same solution ("comparing apples and oranges") | The government is like a business, so it should prioritize making money. |
| straw man | the opponent's argument is misrepresented (only its weakest point, or something never said) to make it easier to attack | If the workers think they should be millionaires, they need to buy their own factories. |

### Correlation is not causation
- Post hoc comes from the Latin *post hoc ergo propter hoc*: "after this, therefore because of this." Superstitions are good examples.
- If one event caused another, they will also be correlated, but a correlation does not prove causation. The events may be related by **coincidence** (the book's graph: the length of the winning word in a spelling bee and the number of deaths from spider bites rise and fall together), or a **third factor** may cause both. Sometimes another cause explains the effect (the sick festival visitors may have had a virus or food poisoning).
- This idea is central in psychology research, where such a third factor is called a confounding variable (or confounding factor).

### Naming, explaining and refuting a fallacy
Use three sentences. The pattern also works for answering counterarguments that contain no clear fallacy.

| Sentence | What to write | Language from the book |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the source + a reporting phrase that shows doubt + a fair paraphrase of their argument (often: X, therefore Y) | The author / Proponents of X / Opponents of X + would have us believe that / want(s) to convince us that / wrongly suppose(s) that / insist(s) that |
| 2 | a transition + the source again + a special verb + the fallacy | Clearly, / On closer inspection, / The problem with this argument is that... + is/are engaging in / fall(s) into the trap of + the X fallacy / a manipulative appeal to Y / flawed reasoning |
| 3 | an explanation of the error | Just because X doesn't mean Y. / There might be another factor that caused Y. / Although X and Y are correlated, this doesn't necessarily mean that X causes Y. / They fail to consider that... / While the individual example is compelling, it alone doesn't prove Y. |

Book example: *One critic of further smoking restrictions wants to convince us that tobacco use is not actually unhealthy, based on the fact that his grandmother smoked for 80 years and did not get cancer. Clearly, this author is engaging in the anecdotal evidence fallacy. Just because this one smoker escaped the harms of tobacco use doesn't mean that statistically, smoking is a safe practice.*

## Answer key

**Exercise 1.** (book)
1. bandwagon / appeal to popularity
2. appeal to false authority (the head of a chemical manufacturers' association is biased: the industry has a financial stake in the answer)
3. appeal to tradition
4. ad hominem
5. slippery slope
6. circular reasoning / begging the question (the reason only repeats the claim in other words)
7. false analogy
8. anecdotal evidence / hasty generalization
9. false cause / post hoc
10. straw man (the argument attacks a claim that supporters of organic cotton do not make: their main reasons are about chemicals and the environment, not durability)
11. false dilemma / false dichotomy (there are other choices, such as buying less or buying second-hand clothes)
12. appeal to emotion (it creates fear and anger instead of giving reasons)

**Exercise 2.** (a) The advertisers (b) imply that (c) if we buy their brand of jeans, we will experience the same joy and beauty as the group of young people with their puppy (d) On closer inspection, (e) is engaging in (f) a manipulative appeal to emotion (g) They provide no evidence that their product is actually higher quality than any other brand. (In sentence 2, the source is renamed "this marketing campaign".)

**Exercise 3.** (example answer)
- Item 5: Opponents of a raise for factory workers insist that even a small raise will lead to the collapse of the whole industry and to economic disaster. The problem with this argument is that these opponents fall into the trap of the slippery slope fallacy. There is no solid proof that if workers receive a small raise, suppliers will go out of business; the opponents are exaggerating the effect of a small increase in labor costs.
- Item 7: Some people seem to think that because sweatshops were a stage in the development of the U.S. economy in the 1900s, they are necessary for the development of the Cambodian economy now. When we examine their logic, however, they are using a false analogy. The two economies may have something in common, but the United States a century ago and Cambodia today are very different situations, and just because sweatshops were part of one country's history doesn't mean that another country needs them now.

## Writing task
Write a paragraph of 150-200 words that names, explains and refutes a logical fallacy.
- Topic: a fallacy in an advertisement, a speech, a social media post or a news article, or a psychology claim such as "My friend sleeps only five hours a night and gets top grades, so students don't need more sleep" or "Thousands of people use this brain-training app, so it must improve memory."
- Required parts: the three sentences of the pattern; two or three sentences on the evidence that would really be needed (for example, a study with many participants); a final sentence that returns to your own position.

Checklist:
- Is your paraphrase of the other argument fair (not a straw man of your own)?
- Did you use a reporting phrase that shows doubt (would have us believe that, wrongly supposes that)?
- Did you signal the change to your rebuttal (On closer inspection, However)?
- Did you name the fallacy correctly, or write "flawed reasoning" if no label fits?
- Did you explain the error, not just name it?
- For a claim about cause, did you consider coincidence or a third factor?
- Did you avoid attacking the person (ad hominem) yourself?
