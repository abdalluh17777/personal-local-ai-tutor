# Lesson 37: Introducing and Explaining Evidence

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 4.7 Introducing and Explaining Evidence.*

## In this lesson
You learn to wrap every piece of evidence in context and explanation (the "sandwich"), to introduce a source with a credential and a reporting word, and to choose the reporting word that matches what the source did: argue, show, suggest, warn and many more. Then you write evidence sandwiches with your own research sources, as you will in every university paper.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Introduce a quotation or paraphrase with the source's name, credential and a reporting word.
- Choose reporting words that match the meaning and type of the evidence.
- Use the correct tense and grammar with reporting verbs, "tell" and "according to".
- Explain evidence after you present it, so that it supports your point.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| dropped quote (dumping a quote) | a quotation put into a paragraph with no introduction | a quotation followed only by (Abutalebi qtd. in Vince) |
| context | information before the evidence: who the source is and what kind of evidence it is | Cognitive neuropsychologist Jubin Abutalebi ... has found through brain scans that ... |
| cre·DEN·tial | information that shows why we should trust a source (job, field, institution) | of the University of San Raffaele in Milan, Italy |
| qtd. in | "quoted in": the person's words appear in another source | (Abutalebi qtd. in Vince) |
| reporting word | a word or phrase that tells who said what, and what kind of thing they said | says, argues, according to |
| explanation (analysis, significance) | sentences after the evidence: why it matters and how it connects to your point | In other words, ... |
| conclude / show | report a result based on research evidence | Bialystok concludes that being bilingual can delay the onset of dementia by about five years. |
| suggest | give evidence for an idea without being 100% definite | Bialystok suggests that dementia may be delayed ... |
| admit / acknowledge | report a concession or a different perspective | Vince acknowledges that many educators still discourage students from keeping their home language. |
| question | doubt or criticize (not "ask") | Bak questions the methods of the researchers ... |

## Rules and explanations

### Context first: no dropped quotes
Your lesson material compares two uses of the same evidence:
- **Dropped quote:** the quotation stands alone, followed by (Abutalebi qtd. in Vince). This meets the minimum citation requirement, but readers wonder: Who is Abutalebi? Why is he here? How does he know this? Why should we believe it?
- **Introduced and explained:** "Cognitive neuropsychologist Jubin Abutalebi, of the University of San Raffaele in Milan, Italy, has found through brain scans that ..." The writer says who he is, gives his credentials, and names the type of evidence (a research finding) before the quotation. After it, the writer explains why the evidence is important.

After the first mention, the last name is enough (Abutalebi), because readers already know who he is.

### The sandwich
| Part | What it contains |
|---|---|
| top bread: context | source + credential (first mention) + reporting word |
| filling: evidence | the quotation or paraphrase, with its citation |
| bottom bread: explanation | why the evidence matters and how it supports your point |

Text evidence does not usually go in the topic sentence or in the last sentence of a body paragraph.

### Tense of reporting verbs
- Usually **simple present**: we write as if all the authors were in one room, each saying their idea, even if they wrote it years ago. Remember the -s for a singular person or organization: Smith argues ..., the Department of Agriculture reports ...
- For a **past event or action** (for example, research someone did), use the simple past or present perfect: Abutalebi **has found** through brain scans that ...

### Grammar: say, state, tell, according to
"Say" can go in three places; the first is the most common in academic writing.

| Position | Example |
|---|---|
| before the quote | Science editor Gaia Vince says, "Multilingualism has been shown to have many social, psychological and lifestyle advantages." |
| after the quote | "Multilingualism has been shown to have many social, psychological and lifestyle advantages," says science editor Gaia Vince. |
| inside the quote | "Multilingualism," says science editor Gaia Vince, "has been shown to have many social, psychological and lifestyle advantages." |

(In the book, the second example is missing its closing quotation mark; it is correct here.)
- **state** follows the same patterns. **tell** needs an indirect object: Gaia Vince tells **us**, "..."
- **according to** is a prepositional phrase, not a verb. Pattern: According to (source), (complete sentence). Add a citation if the source has a page number or the author's name is not in the sentence. Two common mistakes: using "according to" together with a reporting verb (According to Abutalebi, he reports that ...), and not putting a complete sentence after it.

### Reporting words with special meanings
| What the source writer does | Reporting words |
|---|---|
| makes a generalization based on observations | observe |
| focuses the reader's attention on something | point out |
| says the same thing as another source you just referred to | agree, confirm |
| refers to another source | cite |
| gives careful reasons | explain |
| emphasizes something not everybody agrees about | maintain, insist, argue |
| makes a statement based on research evidence | conclude, show |
| says something in passing, not as a main point | mention |
| talks about future possibilities | predict, warn, hope |
| discusses an idea but is not 100% definite | suggest |
| does not believe something is true | deny, doubt, question |
| asks a question | ask, wonder |
| makes a concession or describes a different perspective | admit, acknowledge |

In academic English, **question** means "doubt" or "criticize". To report a question, use **ask** or **wonder**.

### Explaining evidence
Add at least a sentence or two after the evidence: why is it important, and how does it connect to your topic sentence and thesis?
- **You may write:** interpretation (the deeper meaning), emphasis or impact (why it matters), cause and effect, comparison, condition (if/unless ...), evaluation (why something is better or worse), personal connection (only if the assignment allows it).
- **Do not:** end the paragraph with the evidence; repeat the evidence or context; tell what happened next in a story or in reality; use personal experience if the assignment does not allow it.
- **Sentence starters:** In other words, his/her/their point is ... · As this quote/example shows, ... · Basically, X is saying Y. · [The author's] point is that Y. · The essence of [the author's] argument is that Y. · Given these findings, ... · Based on this surprising increase/decrease, ...

> **Psychology note:** In APA style (psychology), the year goes next to the author's name, and verbs that report published research are usually in the past tense or present perfect: Smith (2020) found that ...; researchers have shown that ...

## Answer key

**Exercise 1** (book)
1. says
2. concludes
3. explains
4. suggests
5. tells

**Exercise 2**
1. Incorrect (a reporting verb together with "according to"): According to Vince, European imperialism may have led ... **or** Vince argues that European imperialism may have led ...
2. Incorrect ("tell" needs an indirect object): Science editor Gaia Vince tells us, "Multilingualism ..."
3. Correct.
4. Incorrect ("question" means doubt or criticize): Vince asks, "What will happen ...?"
5. Incorrect (no complete sentence after "according to"): According to several recent studies, bilinguals show more empathy and can guess other people's feelings more accurately.
6. Incorrect (singular subject): Language expert Alex Rawlings insists that ...
7. Correct.
8. Correct (the simple past reports a past event: a study done in 1962).

**Exercise 3**
1. He is a cognitive neuropsychologist at the University of San Raffaele in Milan, Italy.
2. "has found through brain scans": it is a research finding.
3. It reports a past action (research he did), so the present perfect is used, not the simple present.
4. The two sentences after the quotation: "It may sound surprising that knowing two languages can literally change the actual physical structure of our brains, but that is the key to the mental benefits of bilingualism beyond the languages themselves. This extra gray matter makes our brains more flexible and resilient, and helps us understand other people better." (example answer) They show emphasis/impact ("It may sound surprising ... that is the key ..."), interpretation (the deeper meaning: bilingualism changes the brain's structure) and cause and effect (extra gray matter makes the brain more flexible).

**Exercise 4** (example answer)
In other words, her point is that a second language does more than help people communicate: it may also protect the brain as it ages. Given these findings, keeping a home language may matter not only for children's education but also for their health much later in life.

## Writing task
**Your research project: two evidence sandwiches.** Use the claim and evidence you chose in Lesson 36. Write one body paragraph of 180-250 words:
- a topic sentence with your claim (no evidence in it);
- two pieces of evidence from two different sources: one short quotation and one paraphrase;
- before each one: the source's name, a credential (the first time) and an accurate reporting word;
- after each one: one or two sentences of explanation;
- a last sentence in your own words that connects back to your claim.

Self-editing checklist:
- Each piece of evidence is introduced with a source and reporting words.
- The first time a source appears, I give a credential.
- My reporting words match the type of evidence (for example, "argues" for an opinion, not a fact).
- The grammar patterns are correct ("tells us"; "according to" + a complete sentence; never both "according to" and a reporting verb).
- I use the present tense for what authors say, the past or present perfect for research events, and the -s for singular subjects.
- I explain every piece of evidence, and my explanation connects it to my point instead of repeating it.
- There is no evidence in the topic sentence or the last sentence.
