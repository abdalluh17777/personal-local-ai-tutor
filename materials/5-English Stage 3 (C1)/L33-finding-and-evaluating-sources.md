# Lesson 33: Finding and Evaluating Sources

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 3.6 Strategies for Finding Information; 3.7 Evaluating Your Sources.*

## In this lesson
You compare types of sources, turn a research question into keywords, and search the internet and free academic databases instead of a college library. Then you learn six criteria for evaluating sources and find and evaluate the first sources for your project, a core skill for university research.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Compare types of sources by how they are created, reviewed, written and read.
- Choose background sources and research sources for different purposes.
- Turn a research question into keywords, synonyms, narrower terms, related terms and subject terms.
- Search Google Scholar and free databases, and make a search narrower or broader.
- Evaluate a source for authority, accuracy, relevance, currency, objectivity and style.

## Rules and explanations

### Types of sources (Table 3.6.1)
The table in the lesson material compares sources by where you find them, the time to create them, the review process, the writers and the readers. The key difference: only **scholarly journal articles** have a formal peer review process; they take one to a few years to create and are written by specialists (usually people with PhDs, graduate students or professors) for scholars and graduate students. Most other sources are written for the general public, with editorial review or no review at all.

### Which source for which purpose?
- **Background** at the start of a project: Wikipedia, newspaper articles, encyclopedias (in psychology, also a free textbook such as OpenStax *Psychology 2e*).
- **Proof** of an idea in your paper: scholarly journal articles, which show the results of original research. They are very specific, so they are not good for background.

### Internet and database searching
- A web search gives millions of results (Lily got over 400 million), and the first page is not usually the most credible. Use it to understand your topic and find keywords; Google's "Tools" can limit results by date or country.
- A library database is not free. It collects articles (one journal article can cost $25) and tags each with keywords. Search more than one. Drawbacks: new information appears late, and some topics are not covered.

**Without a college library**, use these free tools (links in "Watch online"):
| Free tool | What it is | Useful features |
|---|---|---|
| Google Scholar | a search engine for scholarly articles and books | date filter on the left ("Since ..."); free [PDF] links on the right; "Cited by" shows how often others cite a work |
| PubMed Central | free full-text articles in medicine, health and the life sciences, including much psychology | an article's PubMed record lists its MeSH terms (subject terms) |
| ERIC | a database of education research | "Peer reviewed only" and "Full text available on ERIC" boxes; each record lists Descriptors (subject terms) |
| DOAJ | the Directory of Open Access Journals | every article is free to read |
| OpenStax *Psychology 2e* | a free introductory psychology textbook | background information and key terms |

### Keywords and subject terms
- Google guesses what you mean from a whole question; databases do not. Type only **keywords**: nouns for the main concepts, without verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions or words like *affect/affected*.
- Book example: "How are students of color affected by professors of color?" Keywords: "students of color", "professors of color".
- For each concept, list **synonyms** (minority students; minority faculty, minority professors), **narrower terms** (African American students, community college students) and **related terms** (retention, educational achievement). Type each concept in its own search field.
- Too few results: use more general words. Too many: use more specific words or filters (date, type of article, language, geography).
- Find synonyms in a dictionary or thesaurus (such as Longman), in news or general websites, and in **subject terms** on an article's record page. Databases may use different terms for the same idea ("stadium", "sports arena").

### Evaluating sources (Table 3.7.1)
Every source comes from a person or group with a motive, and every author has an opinion.
| Criterion | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Authority/Credibility | Who is the author, and is there evidence of expertise? Are there reputable citations? |
| Accuracy | Can the facts be verified in other sources? Are there spelling or grammar errors? |
| Relevance | Does it answer your research question? Is it popular or scholarly? |
| Currency/Date | When was it published or updated? Does currency matter for your topic? |
| Objectivity/Bias/Reliability | What is its purpose (educational, commercial, promotional ...)? Is it pretending to be objective while selling or persuading? |
| Style/Functionality | Is it well written and organized? Does a website work, without broken links? |

The book's **Source Evaluation Checklist** (your tutor has it) asks Yes/No questions on credibility, relevance, date, audience and purpose. A "no" means look more closely, not always reject. Careful: for "Is the information in this source biased or limited to one point of view?", the warning answer is "yes". (Its last line says "quality of your service"; it means "source".)

> **Psychology note:** In psychology, the strongest evidence usually comes from peer-reviewed journal articles that report empirical studies (studies based on observation or experiment). The main psychology database, APA PsycInfo, needs a library subscription, so use Google Scholar and PubMed Central.

## Read aloud
Stressed syllables are in CAPITALS.
1. The results on the first page of a search / are not usually the most credible sources.
2. Select nouns that are important / and leave out words that are adverbs, verbs, adjectives, and prepositions.
3. If you're getting too many results, / use more specific words.
4. The organization may not be unbiased, / but it makes it clear who is funding the research.
5. Check the author's cred·i·BIL·i·ty / before you use the source.

## Writing task
Find and evaluate the first sources for your project (your own topic, sleep and memory in university students, stress and coping among international students, or the book's topic, faculty of color).
1. Complete your keyword worksheet (Exercise 6).
2. With the free tools, find four to six sources: one background source, at least two scholarly articles with free full text, and one popular source. Note the author, title, publication, date and link or DOI of each.
3. Evaluate two of them (100 to 150 words each), like the book's answers for Sources 1 and 2: the author's credibility, accuracy, relevance, date and possible bias, then a decision: is it a good source for my paper, and why?

Self-editing checklist:
- Did I name the author and describe the author's background?
- Did I say what kind of source it is (scholarly or popular) and where it was published?
- Did I check the date and say whether currency matters for my topic?
- Did I consider purpose, funders and possible bias?
- Did I explain how the source relates to my research question?
- Did I record all the details I will need for my citation?

Paste your draft into the chat and ask your tutor for feedback. Then rewrite it.
