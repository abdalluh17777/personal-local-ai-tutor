# Lesson 51: Word Forms, Prefixes and Suffixes

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 5: Argument and Academic Style*
*Adapted from College ESL Writers: Applied Grammar and Composing Strategies for Success by Barbara Hall and Elizabeth Wallace, Georgia State University - Perimeter College (CC BY-NC-SA): 6.4 Word Form: Adjectives and Adverbs / Prefixes and Suffixes; 6.6 Working with Words (Exercises).*

## In this lesson
You will use adjectives and adverbs correctly, form comparatives and superlatives (including good, well, bad and badly), learn ten common prefixes and five suffix spelling rules, and then review the whole chapter on words. Precise comparisons ("more likely", "the most effective") are everywhere in academic writing, and prefixes and suffixes help you understand and spell new words in your psychology reading.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Choose between an adjective and an adverb, including good / well and bad / badly.
- Form the comparative and superlative of regular and irregular adjectives and adverbs.
- Use the meanings of ten common prefixes to understand and build words.
- Spell words correctly when you add a suffix.
- Proofread a paragraph for word-form, confused-word and spelling errors.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| adjective | describes a noun or pronoun; answers which one? what kind? how many? | The green sweater belongs to Iris. |
| adverb | describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb; answers how? to what extent? why? when? where? | Bertrand sings horribly. |
| linking verb | a verb such as be, look, feel or seem that is followed by an adjective describing the subject | She looks beautiful. |
| comparative | the form that compares two people or things | Steven is thinner than Jorge. |
| superlative | the form that compares more than two | Fatoumata is the loudest cheerleader on the squad. |
| prefix | a word part added to the beginning of a word to create a new meaning | mis + spell = misspell |
| suffix | a word part added to the end of a word | dark + ness = darkness |
| root word | the word before a prefix or suffix is added | care in caring, careful |
| silent e | a final e that is not pronounced | care, use, replace |
| accent (stress) | the syllable said with more force | re·GRET, CAN·cel |

## Rules and explanations
### Adjectives and adverbs
- An **adjective** describes a noun or pronoun: "The **green** sweater belongs to Iris." After a linking verb, the adjective describes the subject: "She looks **beautiful**."
- An **adverb** describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb: "Bertrand sings **horribly**." "My sociology instructor is **extremely** wise." "He threw the ball **very** accurately." Most adverbs are formed by adding -ly to an adjective.

### Comparatives and superlatives
| Word | Comparative (two) | Superlative (more than two) |
|---|---|---|
| one syllable: add -er / -est | big: bigger, fast: faster | biggest, fastest |
| two or more syllables: more / most | more comfortable, more happily | most comfortable, most jealous |
| two syllables ending in -y: change y to i | happier, healthier | happiest, healthiest |

(The book gives the -y exception only for superlatives; it applies to comparatives too.) Use "than" after a comparative and "the" before a superlative. Never use both forms together, such as "more" with -er.

### Irregular forms: good, well, bad, badly
| | | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|---|
| adjective | good | better | best |
| adverb | well | better | best |
| adjective | bad | worse | worst |
| adverb | badly | worse | worst |

- Incorrect: Cecilia felt that she had never done so ~~good~~ on a test. Correct: ...done so **well** on a test.
- Incorrect: Cecilia's team received a ~~well~~ score. Correct: ...received a **good** score.
- Incorrect: I did ~~bad~~ on my accounting test. Correct: I did **badly**...
- Incorrect: The coming thunderstorm looked ~~badly~~. Correct: ...looked **bad**.
- **Better** and **worse** compare two people or things; **best** and **worst** compare three or more.

(The book says "well" is "always an adverb". It is also an adjective meaning "healthy": "I don't feel well today.")

### Prefixes
The main rule: do not add letters or leave out letters (dis + satisfied = dissatisfied; mis + spell = misspell).

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| dis | not, opposite of | dissatisfied |
| mis | wrongly | misspell |
| un | not | unacceptable |
| re | again | reelection |
| inter | between | interrelated |
| pre | before | prepay |
| non | not | nonsense |
| super | above | superscript |
| sub | under | submerge |
| anti | against, opposing | antibacterial |

### Suffix rules
| Rule | Examples | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| 1. With -ness and -ly, the word does not change. | darkness, scholarly | consonant + y: change y to i (readily, happiness) |
| 2. Suffix begins with a vowel: drop the silent e. | caring, usable | after ce or ge, keep the e before a or o (replaceable, courageous) |
| 3. Suffix begins with a consonant: keep the silent e. | careful, careless | truly, argument |
| 4. Consonant + y: change y to i, unless the suffix begins with i. | sunnier | hurrying (the suffix -ing begins with i, so the y stays) |
| 5. Suffix begins with a vowel: double the final consonant only if the word has one syllable or is stressed on the last syllable, **and** it ends in one vowel + one consonant. | tanning, regretting, preferred | canceled (CAN·cel: stress not on the last syllable; British English writes cancelled) |

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic builds words mainly from roots and patterns (k-t-b: kataba, kitab, maktab), while English often adds prefixes and suffixes. Learn whole word families (succeed, success, successful, successfully) so that you do not use the right root in the wrong form ("a well score", "she is very success").

## Writing task
**Part A (the book's task).** In a formal, business-like tone, write eight separate sentences, one with each of these words: good, well, bad, badly, better, best, worse, worst. Imagine that a current or future employer will read and judge them.

**Part B.** Write a paragraph of 150-200 words that compares two ways to study for an exam, for example rereading your notes and testing yourself (memory is a topic in PSYC 110). Use at least four comparative or superlative forms and at least three words with prefixes from the table.

Self-editing checklist:
- Did I use an adjective after linking verbs (be, look, feel, seem) and an adverb to say how something is done?
- Are good and bad adjectives, and well and badly adverbs?
- -er / -est for one syllable, more / most for two or more (except two-syllable words ending in -y)?
- No double forms such as "more smarter" or "most dumbest"?
- Better / worse for two things, best / worst for three or more; "than" after comparatives, "the" before superlatives?
- Did I add each prefix without adding or dropping letters?
- Did I follow the suffix rules for silent e, final y and doubled consonants?
