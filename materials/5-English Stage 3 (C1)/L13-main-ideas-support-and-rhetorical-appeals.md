# Lesson 13: Main Ideas, Support and Rhetorical Appeals

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 2: Critical Reading*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 1.6: Finding Main Ideas and Support; 1.7: Finding Ethos, Pathos, and Logos.*

## In this lesson
You will find the topic, main idea and supporting details in a research article and a student essay, and identify the three rhetorical appeals: pathos, ethos and logos. At university, these skills help you ask of every article or lecture: What is the main claim? What supports it? How does the writer try to persuade me?

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Distinguish the topic, the main idea (thesis), supporting reasons and supporting details.
- Find the thesis and the topic sentences of an academic essay, and the main idea of an article.
- Separate major supporting details from minor ones.
- Identify pathos, ethos and logos in quotations and in a talk.
- Judge which appeals are most effective, and explain why.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| topic | a word or phrase that answers "What is the text about?" | undocumented immigrant rights; the problem with stereotypes |
| main idea / thesis | the most important point the writer makes about the topic | We can find the thesis at the end of the introduction. |
| thesis statement | 1-3 sentences that show what the writer intends to prove; the controlling idea of an essay | A thesis statement drives an essay. |
| topic sentence | the first sentence of a body paragraph: an opinion that gives one reason for the thesis | Each body paragraph should begin with a topic sentence. |
| supporting reasons | points that explain the main idea and present facts | Supporting reasons explain the main idea. |
| supporting details | examples and other points that clarify a reason | Supporting details clarify the major reasons with examples. |
| major / minor detail | a detail that directly supports the point / a smaller example that explains a major detail | Which ones seem to be major ones and which ones are minor ones? |
| rhetoric | the art of effective, persuasive writing and speaking | Writers and speakers use rhetoric to convince their audience. |
| rhetorical appeal | a method a writer or speaker uses to persuade | Aristotle called these three rhetorical appeals. |
| pathos | an appeal to emotions and values | stories, anecdotes (short personal stories), powerful words, images |
| ethos | an appeal to credibility and trust | experts with their credentials; accurate citations; first-hand testimony |
| logos | an appeal to logic and evidence | facts, case studies, experiments, statistics |

## Rules and explanations
### Topic, main idea and support
First identify the topic, then the main idea; then check that the most important details explain the main idea. (Table 1.5.1 in the material.)

| Element | What it is | Where to find it in an academic essay |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | a word or phrase: what is the text about? | the title and the introduction |
| Main idea (thesis) | the writer's most important point or message about the topic; all key information supports it; the title may give a clue | at the end of the introduction, usually 1-3 sentences |
| Supporting reasons | points that explain the main idea and present facts | the topic sentence at the start of each body paragraph: an opinion statement related to the thesis |
| Supporting details | examples and other points that clarify each reason | after each topic sentence; each paragraph focuses on one point and relates back to the topic sentence and the thesis |

- In other kinds of text, such as magazine articles, the same elements exist, but in different places: paragraphs may be shorter, and the main idea may come later or be less directly stated.

### The three rhetorical appeals
How do we know whom to trust? How do writers convince readers? The Greek philosopher Aristotle (384-322 BC) described three methods, or rhetorical appeals. Texts and talks often combine them. Academic writing emphasizes ethos and logos, but pathos can also be useful.

| Appeal | The writer's goal | Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| **Pathos** (emotions and values) | convince the audience by appealing to their heart and emotions | stories, anecdotes, powerful words or phrases, images that awaken strong emotions such as anger, joy or fear, connected to people's beliefs |
| **Ethos** (credibility and trust) | build trust by presenting the argument thoughtfully and thoroughly, with expert opinion | introducing recognized experts with their credentials, citing sources accurately, first-hand testimony, appropriate language (for example, "This suggests ...") |
| **Logos** (logic) | develop the argument logically, with evidence we can measure, count or agree on | facts, case studies, experiments, statistics, logical reasoning and organization, defining key terms, explaining ideas, being objective |

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic persuasive writing often relies on repetition, parallel structures and emotional emphasis. English academic writing relies more on logos and ethos: state the main idea directly, then support it with evidence and credible sources.

## Answer key
**Exercise 1**
1. Undocumented immigration and crime in U.S. metropolitan areas.
2. Undocumented immigration does not increase violent crime in U.S. metropolitan areas, and it may reduce property crime. It is stated at the very beginning, under the heading "The big idea", not at the end of an introduction as in an academic essay. This is typical of a short research brief in a magazine.
3. (1) The authors' new study of 154 metropolitan areas, using two estimates of the undocumented population and FBI crime data, found that undocumented immigration, on average, has no effect on violent crime. (2) Its regression analysis showed that as the undocumented population increases, the property crime rate decreases, on average (for example burglary). (3) Earlier research agrees: the authors' 2005 analysis found that immigration did not increase crime; their 2017 study found less property crime in areas with more immigrants (1970-2010); and the new findings build on a 2018 review of 51 studies by Ousey and Kubrin.

**Exercise 2**
1. (example answer) Immigration to the U.S.; Reyna Grande's life and her memoir *A Dream Called Home*; an immigrant's struggle to belong and to achieve a dream (to become a writer). The student's own experience as an Asian immigrant is a minor topic.
2. "In her memoir, we can see how she struggled to find a sense of belonging in the U.S. and achieve her dream." It is the last sentence of the introduction. The sentence before it ("*A Dream Called Home* is about her hard life in the U.S.") introduces the book.
3. "Reyna felt like an outsider when she visited her country and wrote stories about her hometown which her creative writing teacher rejected." The paragraph's main idea: Reyna felt that she did not belong anywhere, in Mexico or in the U.S. This supports the "sense of belonging" part of the thesis.
4. (example answer) Major details: during ten days in Mexico she was heartbroken because people no longer saw her as Mexican, while in the U.S. people thought she was not American enough, so she felt she could not belong anywhere; her creative writing teacher rejected her stories about her hometown as "too outrageous" but loved her American classmates' stories; as the only Latinx student in the class at UCSC, she felt hopeless, thought about dropping out and questioned her dream. Minor details: the quotation about why she wrote the stories (74); the examples of her stories (the flood, with people on their roofs collecting dead animals in canoes; the barefoot girl hit for being left-handed); the topics of her classmates' stories (75). Note that the topic sentence joins two ideas (feeling like an outsider and the rejected stories); the book's table says a paragraph should focus on one point.

**Exercise 3**
1. Pathos (book). A personal story told with strong feeling: he learns that his "uncle" was a smuggler and that his suitcase had been packed for months. Readers share his shock.
2. Ethos (book). He names respected professionals, an editor "known for her keen journalistic eye" and "an ace fact-checker", who checked that all the facts were correct, so readers can trust his writing.
3. Logos (book). A statistic: a measurable amount (12 billion dollars a year) that readers can check.
4. (example answer) For an academic audience, quotation 3 is the most effective, because a number can be checked and is hard to argue with. For a general audience, quotation 1 may be more powerful, because readers remember stories and feel empathy.

**Exercise 4**
(example answer) In the TED talk:
- Pathos: the opening story: at 16, a clerk told him his green card was fake: "Don't come back here again." His grandfather, a security guard earning no more than eight dollars an hour, saved 4,500 dollars to bring him to America.
- Ethos: he presents himself as "a journalist and filmmaker"; he has asked his questions in front of diverse audiences across America; he quotes the writer Toni Morrison.
- Logos: historical facts and dates (for example, Native Americans were not considered U.S. citizens until the 1924 Indian Citizenship Act); facts about the citizenship test (officers ask up to 10 of 100 questions; applicants must answer at least six correctly); a clear structure built on three questions: "Where did you come from? How did you get here? Who paid?"

In "Deport Me":
- Pathos: his mother's weekend of work for 100 dollars ("She was priceless to me!"); "half-priced human beings".
- Ethos: the introduction gives his credentials (a BA in psychology, one of the first two DACAmented teachers through Teach for America, seven years of teaching); first-hand testimony.
- Logos: the numbers (contractors charge 20,000 dollars, his father asks 15,000 and receives about 10,000); "the billions in taxes we bring in every year"; the step-by-step "math lesson" structure.

## Writing task
**A short analysis of rhetorical appeals.** Write two paragraphs (180-250 words in total) about one text:
- Vargas's TED talk or "Deport Me";
- the editorial "A Win for Undocumented Immigrants is a Win for All" (Lesson 12 material);
- a psychology lecture: PSYC 110 Lecture 20, "The Good Life: Happiness", Chapters 3 and 4 (studies, examples and expert names).

Paragraph 1 (2-3 sentences): the author or speaker, the title, the topic and the main idea in your own words. Paragraph 2: one example of each appeal, with a short quotation or a precise description, and an explanation of how it persuades the audience. End with your judgment: which appeal is most effective, and why?

Checklist:
- Your first sentence names the author and the title; the main idea is in your own words.
- Each appeal is named correctly: pathos = emotions and values, ethos = credibility and trust, logos = logic and evidence.
- Each example is exact: quotations are copied word for word, in quotation marks.
- After each example, you explain how it persuades, not only which appeal it is.
- You end with a clear judgment and a reason.
- After the first mention, you use the writer's last name only.
