# Lesson 40: Argument: A Model Essay and the Rhetorical Appeals

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 5: Argument and Academic Style*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 5.1 Introduction; 5.2 Sample Student Research Essay - Fast Fashion; 5.3 Distinguishing Rhetorical Appeals.*

## In this lesson
You start the unit on argument: the methods writers use to get readers to believe an idea or take an action. You read a student research essay that argues for regulating the "fast fashion" industry, and you learn Aristotle's three rhetorical appeals: pathos, ethos and logos. Analyzing arguments helps you understand different perspectives in every academic discipline, and it is the first step toward writing your own argument.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain what rhetoric is and name the three rhetorical appeals.
- Identify pathos, ethos and logos in a text and say which clues show them.
- Describe how an argumentative research essay is built: thesis, support, concession, counterargument and conclusion.
- Write the introduction to an argument that uses all three appeals.

## Rules and explanations

### Why argument matters
- Analyzing arguments helps you understand different perspectives on a problem; writing arguments helps you convince readers and inspire them to act.
- It helps in every academic discipline, in your career and in civic life. It is your entry into "The Great Conversation": the ongoing discussion of what ideas are true and how we know this.
- In this unit you will identify, evaluate and write the three appeals; analyze deductive logic; refute logical fallacies; write concessions and counterarguments; and use hedging language.

### The theme: fast fashion
"Fast fashion" is clothing produced quickly and cheaply, following the latest design trends, to meet consumer demand, but not made to last. It raises controversial ethical issues: it causes environmental damage (pollution and wasted resources) and unfair labor practices. However, people need affordable clothing, and laws and boycotts against the industry sometimes have unintended negative consequences.

### Rhetoric and the three appeals
Rhetoric is the study of the art of persuasion and of how we apply it to effective speaking and writing. When you write an argument, you help your readers make a decision: to agree with you, to understand and accept your main idea, and perhaps to act on it. The Greek philosopher Aristotle (called "The First Teacher" by medieval Muslim scholars) described three **rhetorical appeals**:

| Appeal | It is about | The writer ... | Clues |
|---|---|---|---|
| pathos | feelings | connects to readers' values through emotion, senses and story | a story about a person, sensory details, emotional words |
| ethos | credibility | builds trust by presenting the argument fairly and thoughtfully, and by using information from expert sources | credentials, fair treatment of other views |
| logos | logic | sets up a solid structure of reasons with evidence | facts, statistics, reasons, "therefore" |

- An effective argumentative essay uses all three; they work together (the "rhetorical triangle").
- There is always some overlap, so look for the strongest clue.

### The model essay: "Deadly Fashion"
In "Deadly Fashion", two students, Maroua Abdelghani and Ruri Tamimoto, argue that fast fashion harms workers and the environment and needs government regulation. As you read, notice the personal story at the start, the thesis that names the other side, the cited evidence in each body paragraph, a paragraph that admits some progress (a concession), a paragraph that answers the opposing view (a counterargument), and a call to action at the end. It is a student essay, so a few small errors remain: for example, "In fact, According" in paragraph 1 has a wrong capital letter, and two sources (Drew in paragraph 2, Ozdamar-Ertekin in paragraph 6) are missing from the Works Cited list.

> **Psychology note:** The essay uses MLA citations, such as (274) after a sentence that names Joy. Psychology uses APA style, where the year is always added: (Joy et al., 2012, p. 274).

## Answer key

**Exercise 1**
1. With a personal story: the writer's $9.99 t-shirt that is no longer wearable after two washes, and later the questions about the person who sewed it. (example answer) It appeals mostly to pathos (a story and an everyday experience readers share); it also makes the writer seem honest and thoughtful, which builds ethos.
2. "Although some defend the fast fashion industry's aesthetic and economic contributions, it has devastating impacts on labor rights and the environment, and needs serious regulations by all nations to stop the damage." It is the last sentence of paragraph 1. The other side: "Although some defend the fast fashion industry's aesthetic and economic contributions".
3. Paragraph 2: factory workers are paid too little, and their requests for living wages are ignored. Paragraph 3: workers also suffer terrible daily working conditions. Paragraph 4 (concession): the industry has made some progress in labor rights since the Rana Plaza disaster, but low pay and inhumane daily conditions remain. Paragraph 5: producing cheap clothing causes significant pollution (pesticides on cotton, water pollution, carbon emissions). Paragraph 6 (counterargument): some argue that the industry gives work to people with few better choices; the writers reject this argument.
4. Workers are paid about $96 per month, but the government estimates $336 as the minimum for basic needs; a third of $336 is $112, so $96 is less than a third. This is logos (facts and a calculation that lead to a conclusion).
5. Any two credentials, for example: "anthropologist and marketing professor Annamma Joy"; "Deborah Drew, an associate at the World Resource Institute's Center for Sustainable Business"; "Ken Loo, secretary-general of the Garment Manufacturers Association of Cambodia"; "Sadika Akhter, an anthropologist and doctor of Environmental Public Health"; "Research scientist Luz Claudio"; "investigative reporter Adam Matthews"; "Benjamin Powell, the director of the Free Market Institute".
6. The machine operator's words: "We sew shirts with our tears and injure our fingers due to needle punctures. If you work at the garment factory it will give you some money but it will take your health. . . ."
7. The opposing argument: the fashion industry provides work to people with few better choices in developing countries; Benjamin Powell argues that this model is "part of the process that raises living standards and leads to better working conditions and development over time". The writers answer that the argument is "compelling from a distance" and may be true to some degree historically, but it disregards the humanity of current garment workers, who work long hours in brutal conditions while owners make huge profits. They call it "a cynical justification for greed".
8. Consumers should buy second-hand clothes, look for companies making apparel in the U.S., and be willing to pay more for higher-quality clothing that lasts. But the main change must come from stronger government regulations.

**Exercise 2** (book)
1. pathos · 2. ethos · 3. logos · 4. logos · 5. pathos · 6. ethos

Clues: 1, a dramatic story with sensory details (heat, smoke, the ledge, the fall); 2, an expert's credentials; 3, a general principle, a fact and "Therefore" (reasoning); 4, facts about polyester, production and emissions, from a cited source; 5, a story about a person, with sensory details and feelings ("thrilled", "nightmares"); 6, the writer is fair (admits "some incremental progress") and quotes a credible source with her credentials (former child worker, union organizer, leader of the Bangladesh Center for Worker Solidarity).

## Writing task
**Begin an argument.** Choose a controversial question:
- the book's topic: Should governments regulate the fast fashion industry?
- a psychology topic: Should high schools start later so that teenagers get enough sleep? Should schools teach students skills for coping with stress?
- your research topic from Lessons 32 to 39, turned into a question that people disagree about.

Write the introduction to an argument essay (180-250 words):
- a hook that uses pathos: a short story or a vivid example, as "Deadly Fashion" does;
- one or two sentences of background with a fact from a source, introduced with a credential and cited (ethos and logos); for a psychology topic, look in the OpenStax "Psychology 2e" textbook, Google Scholar or PubMed Central;
- a thesis statement at the end that takes a clear position and names the other side ("Although some ..., ...").

Below your paragraph, write one example of each appeal you used, labeled "pathos", "ethos" and "logos".

Self-editing checklist:
- My hook tells a story or gives a vivid example, and it is sincere, not exaggerated.
- My background fact comes from a source, has a credential and an in-text citation.
- My thesis is the last sentence, takes a clear position and names the other side.
- I can point to pathos, ethos and logos in my paragraph.
- My reporting verbs fit the type of evidence.
- My citation follows MLA (or APA for a psychology course), and I have a Works Cited entry for the source.

Paste your draft into the chat and ask your tutor for feedback. Then rewrite it.
