# Lesson 07: Semicolons, Colons and Dashes

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 1: Refining Sentences and Punctuation*
*Adapted from College ESL Writers: Applied Grammar and Composing Strategies for Success by Barbara Hall and Elizabeth Wallace, Georgia State University - Perimeter College (CC BY-NC-SA): 8.2: Semicolons; 8.3: Colons; 8.7: Dashes.*

## In this lesson
You will learn three marks that make a stronger break than a comma: the semicolon, which joins two closely related independent clauses or separates list items that already contain commas; the colon, which introduces a list, an example, an explanation or a quotation; and the dash, which sets off information for emphasis. Used well, they let you vary your sentences and show exactly how your ideas are connected, which readers of academic writing value.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Join two closely related independent clauses with a semicolon, and avoid a semicolon before FANBOYS.
- Separate list items that contain commas with semicolons.
- Use a colon after an independent clause to introduce a list, an example, an explanation or a quotation, and decide whether the next word needs a capital letter.
- Use one dash or a pair of dashes to set off information for emphasis.
- Choose between a period, a comma, a semicolon, a colon and a dash.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| semicolon (;) | a mark that joins two closely related independent clauses, or separates list items that contain commas | Be sure to wear clean, well-pressed clothes to the interview; appearances are important. |
| colon (:) | a mark after an independent clause that introduces a list, an example, an explanation or a quotation | The team will tour three states: New York, Pennsylvania, and Maryland. |
| dash (—) | a long line that sets off information for emphasis and shows a pause | Arrive to the interview early—but not too early. |
| independent clause | a subject and a verb that can stand alone as a complete thought | appearances are important |
| closely related | the second clause explains, continues or adds to the first | "appearances are important" explains why clean clothes matter |
| choppy | written in many short sentences that do not flow | ...to the interview. Appearances are important. |
| comma splice | two independent clauses joined with only a comma (the book calls it "an awkward run-on sentence") | ...to the interview, appearances are important. |
| FANBOYS | the coordinating conjunctions for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so | (no semicolon before them) |
| memo | a short business message | To: Human Resources / From: Deanna Dean |
| proper noun | the name of a particular person, place or thing; always capitalized | Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador |
| emphasis | special importance given to some words | Any of the suits—except for the purple one—should be fine to wear. |

## Rules and explanations

### Semicolons
**1. Between two closely related independent clauses**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| Correct | Be sure to wear clean, well-pressed clothes to the interview; appearances are important. |
| Choppy (correct, but less smooth) | Be sure to wear clean, well-pressed clothes to the interview. Appearances are important. |
| Incorrect | Be sure to wear clean, well-pressed clothes to the interview, appearances are important. |

- There must be an independent clause on both sides of the semicolon.
- Do not use a semicolon with FANBOYS (and, but, or ...): use a comma there (Lesson 06).
- As in all the book's examples, the word after a semicolon has no capital letter, unless it is a name or *I*.
- A semicolon gives variety to sentence length and structure while keeping the flow of ideas.

**2. Between list items that already contain commas**
- The color combinations we can choose from are black, white, and grey; green, brown, and black; or red, green, and brown.

With commas only, the reader cannot see where one set of colors ends and the next begins.

### Colons
Use a colon to introduce the following. The part before the colon must be an **independent clause**: a complete thought with a subject and a verb.
- a list: I have to take four classes this semester: Composition, Statistics, Ethics, and Italian.
- an example or an explanation: Our company offers many publishing services: writing, editing, and reviewing.
- a quotation: My mother loved this line from Hamlet: "To thine own self be true."

A colon also follows the greeting in a business letter and the headings of a memo: Dear Hiring Manager: / To: Human Resources / From: Deanna Dean

Do not use a colon after *such as* or *for example*. Incorrect: Our company offers many publishing services, such as: writing, editing, and reviewing.

**A capital letter after a colon?**

| What follows the colon | Capital letter? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a proper noun | yes | We visited three countries: Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador. |
| the beginning of a quotation | yes | My mother loved this line from Hamlet: "To thine own self be true." |
| an independent clause | yes (the book's rule) | There are drawbacks to modern technology: My brother's cell phone died and he lost a lot of phone numbers. |
| words that are not a complete sentence | no | Incorrect: The recipe is simple: Tomato, basil, and avocado. (Correct: ...simple: tomato, basil, and avocado.) |

(In the third example, Lesson 06's rule also needs a comma before *and*: ...died, and he lost ...)

**Usage varies:** many style guides (for example the Chicago Manual of Style) use a small letter for an independent clause after a colon. APA style, used in psychology, capitalizes it, as the book does.

### Dashes
A dash (—) sets off information for emphasis and shows a pause. Use one dash before information at the end of a sentence, or a pair of dashes around information in the middle.
- Arrive to the interview early—but not too early.
- Any of the suits—except for the purple one—should be fine to wear.

Do not put spaces between a dash and the words. In Microsoft Word, type two hyphens together to make a dash. A dash is longer than a hyphen (-) and has a different job (Lesson 08). **Usage varies:** British texts often use a shorter dash with a space on each side ( – ).

### Choosing the mark
| You want to ... | Use |
|---|---|
| join two closely related independent clauses with no conjunction | a semicolon |
| join two independent clauses with FANBOYS | a comma + FANBOYS |
| introduce a list, an example, an explanation or a quotation after a complete sentence | a colon |
| separate list items that contain commas | semicolons |
| add information with emphasis, or show a sudden pause | one dash or two dashes |

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
>
> - Arabic usually puts a colon after "he said" before the words someone said. In English, use a comma after *said* or *asked* (Lesson 06). Use a colon before a quotation only when a complete sentence comes before it: My mother loved this line from Hamlet: "To thine own self be true."

## Answer key
The book gives no key; these answers follow the book's rules.

**Exercise 1**
1. I did not notice that you were in the office; I was behind the front desk all day.
2. Do you want turkey, spinach, and cheese; roast beef, lettuce, and cheese; or ham, tomato, and cheese?
3. Please close the blinds; there is a glare on the screen.
4. OK
5. ...green, brown, and purple; green, black, and brown; or green, brown, and dark red.
6. Let's go for a walk; the air is so refreshing.

**Exercise 2**
1. Don't give up; you never know what tomorrow brings. (A colon is also possible, because the second clause explains the first: Don't give up: You never know ...)
2. ...admitted on March 9, 2010; January 13, 2010; and November 16, 2009.
3. Allow me to introduce myself: I am the greatest ice-carver in the world. (The second part is the introduction, so a colon is best; a semicolon is also correct.)
4. Where I come from, there are three ways to get to the grocery store: by car, by bus, and by foot. (The comma after *from* ends the introductory clause; it is correct but not part of this exercise.)
5. Listen closely; you will want to remember this speech. (A colon is also possible: Listen closely: You will want ...)
6. I have lived in Sedona, Arizona; Baltimore, Maryland; and Knoxville, Tennessee.
7. The boss's message was clear: Lateness would not be tolerated.
8. OK. Do not put a colon after *such as*.
9. My little sister said what we were all thinking: "We should have stayed home."
10. Trust me; I have done this before. (A colon is also possible: Trust me: I have done this before.)

**Exercise 3**
1. Which hairstyle do you prefer—short or long?
2. I don't know—I hadn't even thought about that.
3. Guess what—I got the job!
4. OK. (A dash is possible if you want to stress the condition: ...over the weekend—if I can have Monday off.)
5. ...we are looking for in a candidate—intelligence, dedication, and a strong work ethic. (A colon is also correct, because the list follows an independent clause.)

## Read aloud
Pause and take a breath at a semicolon; pause at a colon and before and after dashes.

## Writing task
Write a paragraph of 150 to 200 words on one of these topics:
- advice for someone going to a job interview or starting at a university, with reasons (like the book's interview examples);
- a topic from PSYC 110, for example three ways people cope with stress, or what helps us remember new information.

Include: two semicolons that join closely related independent clauses; one list with semicolons between items that contain commas (for example cities and countries, or researchers and their universities); one colon that introduces a list; one colon that introduces an explanation or a quotation; and one dash or a pair of dashes.

Checklist:
- Each semicolon has an independent clause on both sides.
- There is no semicolon before *and*, *but*, *or*, *so* or another FANBOYS word.
- I used semicolons in a list only where the items already contain commas.
- A complete sentence comes before every colon; there is no colon after *such as* or *for example*.
- After each colon, I used a capital letter for a proper noun, a quotation or an independent clause, and a small letter for words that are not a complete sentence.
- My dashes have no spaces around them, and they mark information I want to emphasize.
- I did not use a hyphen (-) where I needed a dash (—).
