# Lesson 39: Reporting Verbs and Quoting: Ellipses and Square Brackets

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 4.10 Language Toolkit; 4.11 Takeaways.*

## In this lesson
You review the grammar of reporting words: which ones take a "that" clause, which take a noun phrase, and which have special patterns such as "asks whether" or "writes about". You also learn to shorten and clarify quotations with ellipses and square brackets without changing their meaning. Finally, you look back at the whole unit and revise your research paragraph, so that your evidence reads accurately and smoothly.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Choose a reporting word for the kind of idea it introduces (opinion, fact, concession).
- Use the correct grammar pattern after a reporting word.
- Punctuate a direct quotation after a reporting word.
- Use ellipses and square brackets correctly, without changing the meaning of a quotation.
- Reflect on how you use and cite evidence.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| SVC | subject + verb + complement: a complete idea | (He asserts that) bilingualism is valuable. |
| noun phrase (NP) | a noun and the words attached to it, not a complete sentence | the health and legal aspects of decriminalization |
| asserts | states firmly that something is true | Le asserts that drugs should be decriminalized. |
| notes | notices or pays careful attention to something, often not the main idea | Le notes that the end of Prohibition did not result in greater alcohol abuse. |
| claims | states something, especially with little proof (it can show that you doubt it) | Opponents claim that drug use will increase with decriminalization. |
| speculates | guesses about possible causes or effects | Le speculates that the initial transition period might be difficult. |
| challenges | argues against; refuses to believe | Ruiz challenges the assumption that decriminalization encourages drug use. |
| acknowledges | admits something, often something that supports the other side | Kaur acknowledges some risks of the new policy. |
| independent clause | a clause with a subject and verb that can stand alone | Nguyen proposes a different idea |
| ellipsis (plural: ellipses) | three periods with a space between them (. . .) that show words were left out | far too many . . . children of immigrants |
| square brackets | [ ] around words you added or changed inside a quotation | In [Snow's] view |

## Rules and explanations

### Reporting words signal meaning
After "argues", readers expect an opinion; after "reports", a fact; after "acknowledges", a concession. Be careful with "believes" and "thinks": we can read authors' words, not their minds. And if you use "claims" for someone's opinion, readers will think you doubt it.

### Five grammar patterns
| Pattern | Reporting words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. verb + that + SVC | agrees (that), argues that, asserts (that), believes (that), cautions that, claims (that), concludes that, insists (that), notes that, reports that, says (that), speculates that, states that, thinks (that) | She warns that this law will have terrible consequences. |
| 2. verb + NP (or a "how" or "whether" clause, or verb-ing), never "that" | challenges, considers, criticizes, describes, discusses, examines, investigates, refers to, summarizes | The author questions the motives behind English-only policies. |
| 3. either pattern 1 or pattern 2 | acknowledges, adds, admits, comments that / comments on, explains, indicates, maintains, mentions, points out, proposes, remembers, recalls, regrets, suggests | Kaur acknowledges that the new policy has some risks. / Kaur acknowledges some risks of the new policy. |
| 4. special patterns | asks if/whether/why + SVC, asks about + NP; questions + NP, questions if/whether + SVC; recommends that + SVC (base verb), recommends + verb-ing; writes that + SVC, writes about + NP; wonders if/whether/why + SVC, wonders about + NP | Kim asks why current policies continue, despite evidence that they are not working. |
| 5. according to + source, SVC | a prepositional phrase, not a verb | According to Kim, decriminalization is the first step toward solving the drug crisis. |

- "(that)" in parentheses means "that" is optional; using it sounds more formal. Without parentheses, include "that" in academic writing.
- Pattern 2: to add a complete idea, use "the fact that", "with", or an adjective clause, without making the noun the subject of a sentence: The author describes an **experiment** that tested methods of memorization.
- "According to" can go at the beginning, in the middle or at the end of the sentence. Never add a reporting verb for the same idea.

### Reporting a direct quotation
With a full-sentence quotation, leave out "that" and use commas. The reporting word can go before the quotation, inside it (after the subject of the first sentence) or after it. (When a quoted phrase becomes part of your own sentence, "that" stays: Bak maintains that "the detractors have made errors in their experimental methods.") Use a colon only after an independent clause:
- Nguyen proposes, "The city should adopt harm reduction approaches."
- Nguyen proposes a different idea: "The city should adopt harm reduction approaches."

### Ellipses: leaving words out
- An ellipsis is three periods with a space between them (. . .). It shows that you left out words to make a quotation shorter and simpler.
- In the book's example from Catherine Snow, repetitive words are left out: "Notably, far too many . . . children of immigrants . . . lose fluency in their parents' language. . . . By the third generation, immigrants have completely lost fluency in their heritage languages" (Snow). When the gap comes after the end of a sentence, the sentence's period comes first, then the ellipsis.
- Do not change the meaning. "Notably, . . . children with the greatest potential . . . have completely lost fluency in their heritage languages" is wrong: Snow says this about the third generation of immigrants.
- Check that your sentence is still grammatical. Do not leave out the grammar and function words that the sentence needs.

### Square brackets: changing or adding words
- Use square brackets [like this] when the grammar of a quotation does not fit your sentence, or to make something clear. In the book's example, Ervin-Tripp "found that women [who were bilingual in English and Japanese] ended the sentences [which researchers gave them to complete] very differently depending on which language was used."
- To change a word at the beginning of a quotation, start the quotation later. In the same example, "She" is replaced by Ervin-Tripp's name before the quotation marks, so no brackets are needed.
- Do not change the meaning: "asking them [whether or not] to finish sentences" is not acceptable.

> **Psychology note:** In APA style (psychology), a direct quotation needs the author, the year and the page number, for example (Smith, 2020, p. 12).

**Exercise 3. Checking ellipses and square brackets.** These quotations are from "The True Failure of Foreign Language Instruction" by Catherine Snow. Decide if each example uses square brackets and/or ellipses correctly. (In the original, the sentence begins "In my view, it's ironic that ..." and continues exactly as in item 1.)
1. In fact, "In [Snow's] view, it's ironic that we have students walking up staircases at one end of their school building to attend Spanish foreign language classes while at the other end of the same building native Spanish speakers are being taught English and content in ways that lead to their loss of Spanish" (Snow).

**Exercise 3** (book)

Self-editing checklist:
- Each reporting word fits the kind of idea it introduces (fact, opinion, concession).
- Each reporting word has the correct pattern (that + SVC, a noun phrase, about, on, whether ...).
- "That" is included wherever it is not optional.
- There is no second reporting verb with "according to", and a complete sentence follows it.
- My ellipses are three spaced periods, and the sentence around them is grammatical.
- My ellipses and brackets do not change the meaning of the original.
- I start a quotation later rather than changing its first words.
- Every quotation still has its citation.
