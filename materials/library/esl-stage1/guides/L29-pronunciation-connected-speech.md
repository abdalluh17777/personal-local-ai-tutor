# Lesson 29: Pronunciation: Connected Speech

*English Stage 1 (B1) · Unit 3: Zero Waste*
*Adapted from Speaking, Listening and Pronunciation Projects for ELLs - Intermediate Level by Brittany Zemlick, Grossmont College (CC BY 4.0): 3.6 Connected Speech.*

## In this lesson
English speakers join words and change some sounds when they speak. This is connected speech. You will learn four rules: linking a final consonant to a vowel, saying two same consonants only once, /t/ that sounds like /d/, and d + y that becomes /ʤ/. Some of this is review from Units 1 and 2.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Link a final consonant to a vowel at the start of the next word.
- Say a shared consonant sound only once, as in "black coffee".
- Say /t/ between two vowels as a quick /d/, as in "a lot of".
- Change d + you to a /ʤ/ sound, as in "did you".
- Hear these changes when native speakers talk.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning in simple English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| connected speech | the way speakers join words and change sounds in natural speech | did you = diʤə |
| link | join the last sound of a word to the first sound of the next word | an apple = a-napple |
| final consonant | the last consonant sound of a word | the /k/ in "walk" |
| beginning vowel | the first sound of a word, when it is a vowel | the a in "away" |
| same consonant | the same sound at the end of one word and the start of the next | book club |
| sound change | a sound becomes a different sound when words join | matter = madder |
| /ʤ/ | the sound at the start of "juice" | did you = diʤə |
| similar sound | almost the same sound, like /d/ and /t/ | need to, sit down |
| native speaker | a person who learned English as a first language | Native speakers often link words. |

## Rules and explanations

### Rule 1: Final consonant + beginning vowel
When a word ends with a consonant sound and the next word begins with a vowel sound, link the consonant to the vowel. Why?
- The final consonant will be easier to say.
- Your speech will sound more natural.
- Your listening will improve, because you will start to hear when native speakers do this.

| Full form | Linked form |
|---|---|
| them all | thə-mall |
| an apple | a-napple |
| walk away | wal-kəway |
| (I'd like to ask you some) questions if (that's ok?) | question-sif |
| (Tell me a little) bit about (yourself.) | bi-təbout |
| (I) live in (San Diego.) | li-vin |
| (I have a younger brother) and an (older sister.) | an-dən |
| (I work) outside on (the farming plots.) | outsi-don |
| (What do you) like about (your job?) | li-kəbout |

### Rule 2: Final consonant + the same consonant
When a word ends with a consonant sound and the next word begins with the same (or a similar) consonant sound, say that sound only once.

| Full form | Linked form |
|---|---|
| black coffee | blakoffee |
| one night | wonight |
| weather report | weatherəport |

### Rule 3: /t/ between two vowels = a quick /d/
When the /t/ sound comes between two vowel sounds, it sounds like a quick /d/. (This is typical of American English.)

| Full form | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| matter | madder |
| a lot of | əloddə |

### Rule 4: Final /d/ + /y/ = /ʤ/
When the /d/ sound (as in "did") comes before the /j/ sound (the y sound in "you"), together they become a /ʤ/ sound (as in "juice").

| Full form | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| did you | diʤə |
| read your | reaʤər |

(The book prints "read you"; the r at the end comes from "your".)

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - In Arabic, a word never really begins with a vowel: there is a small stop (like a hamza) before it. If you put this stop before "apple" in "an apple", the words will not link. Move the consonant to the next word instead: a-napple.

## Speak with your tutor
- "Read these phrases slowly, then at natural speed: an apple, walk away, black coffee, a lot of, did you. I repeat after you."
- "Ask me questions about my life, like 'Where do you live?' and 'What do you like about your job?'. I answer in full sentences and try to link the words."
- "You are a waiter in a cafe. Ask me what I want. I will order black coffee, a water bottle with a lot of ice, and toast with a little butter."
- "Invite me to a party on Saturday. I will say no and give a reason. Then I invite you."
- "Ask me five 'Did you ...?' and 'Would you ...?' questions about my weekend. Then I ask you the same questions."
