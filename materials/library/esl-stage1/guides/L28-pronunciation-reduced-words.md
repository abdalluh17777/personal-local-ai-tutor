# Lesson 28: Pronunciation: Reduced Words

*English Stage 1 (B1) · Unit 3: Zero Waste*
*Adapted from Speaking, Listening and Pronunciation Projects for ELLs - Intermediate Level by Brittany Zemlick, Grossmont College (CC BY 4.0): 3.5 Reduced Words.*

## In this lesson
In natural English, small function words like "to", "and" and "of" are short and weak. We say they are reduced. You will learn how common function words sound when they are reduced, how speakers drop the "h" in words like "he" and "her", and how to hear the difference between "can" and "can't".

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Recognise reduced function words in natural speech.
- Say common function words in their reduced form, for example to = /tə/.
- Drop the "h" in he, him, her, his, have, has and had in the middle of a phrase.
- Hear and say the difference between "can" and "can't".
- Read sentences with stressed content words and reduced function words.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning in simple English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| reduced word | a function word said short and weak | to = /tə/ |
| schwa /ə/ | the most common English vowel: a short, relaxed sound, as in the first sound of "about" | can = /kən/ |
| function word | a small grammar word (article, preposition, pronoun, auxiliary, conjunction) | to, for, at, and, or |
| content word | a word with the important information; it is stressed | toothpaste, make, easy |
| de-stressed | not stressed; said quickly and quietly | "for" in "I had eggs for breakfast." |
| drop the h | not say the /h/ sound | What did he do? = What didee do? |
| link | join a word to the word before it | Send her = Sender |
| can | usually reduced, with a schwa | Lauren can make toothpaste. |
| can't | said with a clear /æ/ as in "apple" | I can't make toothpaste. |

## Rules and explanations

### Commonly reduced function words
Function words are usually not stressed, so they become short and weak. Most reduced forms use the schwa /ə/.

| Full form | Reduced form (what it sounds like) | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| to | /tə/ | You should talk to Charlie. |
| for | /fər/ | I had eggs for breakfast. |
| at | /ət/ | I'll see you at school. |
| and | /ən/, /ɪn/ or /n/ | I'll have the soup and sandwich, please. |
| or | /ər/ | Tea or coffee? |
| you | /yə/ | Do you like it? |
| him, her | /əm/, /ər/ | I called him last night. / I called her last night. |
| have | /əv/ or /ə/ | Where have you been? |
| can | /kən/ | I can sing. Can you? |
| do | /də/ | What do you want? |
| as | /əz/ | Light as a feather, stiff as a board. |
| of | /əv/ or /ə/ | I'd like a cup of tea, please. / He gave me a box of apples. |

- /ɪ/ is the short vowel as in "sit". In /yə/, the y is as in "yes".
- "Of": /əv/ is often used before a vowel sound (All /əv/ our friends were there). /ə/ is often used before a consonant sound (There were a lot /ə/ people).

### Drop the "h"
- When structure words such as **her, he, him, his, have, has** or **had** are in the middle of a phrase, the h sound is often dropped. The word then links to the word before it.
  - What did he do? = What didee do?
  - Will he call me? = Willy call me?
  - Send her an application. = Sender an application.
- You do not need to drop the h for people to understand you. But many native speakers drop it, so knowing this rule will help your listening.

### Can or can't?
- "Can" is usually reduced. You say it with a schwa: /kən/. Lauren can make toothpaste.
- "Can't" is usually said with a clear /æ/ sound, as in "apple" or "answer". I can't make toothpaste.
- So listen for the vowel: weak /ə/ = can; clear /æ/ = can't.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Many Arabic speakers say small words with a full, clear vowel, so "to" sounds like "too". Use the weak schwa in function words (/tə/, /kən/), but keep a clear /æ/ in "can't".
