## In this lesson
You will learn what a paragraph is, how to format it, and what its parts are, using the model paragraph "Missing My Childhood Days". Then you will study the title, with the rules for capital letters, and the topic sentence. Lesson 11 continues with supporting sentences, transitions and concluding sentences.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Define a paragraph and format one correctly.
- Identify the parts of a paragraph in a model text.
- Write titles that follow the capitalization and punctuation rules.
- Find the topic, the controlling idea and the predictors in a topic sentence.
- Write good topic sentences and correct weak ones.

## Rules and explanations
### What is a paragraph?
A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main idea. It tells the readers what you think of a topic (your main idea) and why (your supporting ideas).

### Paragraph format
- Center the title in the middle of the top line.
- Indent the first line (start with a few open spaces).
- Type or write double spaced.
- Each sentence follows the one before it. Do not start a new line for each sentence.
- Use font size 12 if you type.

### The parts of a paragraph
The book colour codes the parts. In "Missing My Childhood Days":
| Part | Its job | Colour | In the model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title | shows the topic and catches interest | pink | Missing My Childhood Days |
| Topic sentence | shows the main idea | red | Thanks to two people and one place, my childhood was filled with fun. |
| Supporting sentences | explain the main idea | green | The first special person was my cousin Hector. (+ two more) |
| Supporting details | explain the supporting sentences; the longest part | blue | I was the only child to my mom ... |
| Transitions | connect the ideas and guide the readers | yellow | first, Secondly, Lastly |
| Concluding sentence(s) | finish the paragraph | red | Nowadays I do not have Hector and Lisandra in my life ... priceless memories. |

Colour coding shows you that no part is missing, that the parts are in the right order, and that the details (blue) are the longest part.

**The hamburger way.** Sesame seeds = title; top bun = topic sentence; the middle = supporting sentences with details (more ingredients, more taste); sauces that hold it together = transitions; bottom bun = concluding sentence (the same bread in a different shape: it restates the topic sentence in a different way).

### Titles
1. A good title is short: often one word or a phrase, usually not a complete sentence: *The Best Invention; The Characteristics of a Good Boss*.
2. It catches the readers' interest but does not tell everything: *A Long-Kept Secret; To Mask or Not to Mask*.
3. It follows the capitalization and punctuation rules:
   - Capitalize the first word and all major words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs and pronouns).
   - Do not capitalize articles (a, an, the), short prepositions (in, on, from...), coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or...) or "to" before a verb, unless it is the first word: *The Story of Our Mother and the Little Cat; Five Interesting Ways to Make a Living*.
   - Never capitalize the entire title. No period at the end, no quotation marks, no underlining.

| Wrong | Correct |
|---|---|
| the day I arrived in Chicago | The Day I Arrived in Chicago |
| THE MAKING OF A DOCTOR | The Making of a Doctor |
| A Very Frustrating Experience. | A Very Frustrating Experience |
| "Advice from My Mother" | Advice from My Mother |
| The Mysterious Noise (underlined) | The Mysterious Noise |

### Capital letters in sentences (Appendix B)
Capitalize (but never write a whole text in capitals):
1. the first word of a sentence;
2. the pronoun "I";
3. continents, countries, nationalities, ethnicities and languages: *Asia, Egypt, Egyptian, Arabic*;
4. months and days of the week: *August, Monday* (but not seasons: *the fall semester*);
5. proper nouns, the specific names of people, places, colleges, religions, holidays, courses... (each word except prepositions, articles, coordinating conjunctions and "to"): *Harper College, Islam, Museum of Science and Industry*. Common nouns (general names) are not capitalized: *religions* but *Christianity*.

### The topic sentence
The topic sentence contains the main idea and usually comes first. It has two essential parts and one optional part:
- the **topic** of your paragraph;
- the **controlling idea**: your attitude or opinion about the topic;
- (optional but preferred) **predictors**: the points you are going to explain.

*I miss my high school life for three reasons: friends, sports, and time for myself.* → topic: my high school life; controlling idea: miss for three reasons; predictors: friends, sports, time for myself.

**Five rules**
| Rule | Wrong | Right |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A complete sentence, with a subject and a verb | My interesting writing class | My writing class is interesting for three reasons. |
| 2. A statement, not a question (if you ask a question, the answer in the next sentence is the topic sentence) | Why is learning English important? | Why is learning English important? It is so because good English skills benefit people in their study, work, and daily life. |
| 3. Narrow down the topic | Year 2020 was a difficult year for me. | The COVID pandemic in Year 2020 made it difficult for me to study. |
| 4. No announcement | In this paragraph, I am going to talk about the disadvantages to online learning. | There are three disadvantages to online learning. |
| 5. Not a fact (a fact has no controlling idea) | My classmates come from twelve different countries. | Representing twelve countries, my classmates are great resources for learning different cultures. |

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic has no capital letters, so capitalization needs extra attention: names of people and places, nationalities, languages, days, months, the pronoun "I", and the major words in a title ("arabic" → "Arabic", "my life in cairo" → "My Life in Cairo").

- Topic sentence: For me, writing is one of the most difficult skills to learn in English.

- Supporting details: all the other sentences between the supporting sentences, from "Many words are pronounced the same" to "lead to errors in verbs."

## Writing task
**A paragraph about your childhood (Warm-Up topic).** Answer the Warm-Up question "What are your most unforgettable childhood memories? Why are they unforgettable?" in one paragraph of about 150 to 200 words. Use "Missing My Childhood Days" as your model.
Include a centered title, a topic sentence with predictors, three supporting sentences with details and transitions (first, secondly, lastly), and a concluding sentence. Use the paragraph format.

Keep this paragraph: in Lesson 11 you will check it for unity, transitions and the concluding sentence.

**Self-editing checklist**
- Is my title short and correctly capitalized, with no period, quotation marks or underlining?
- Is my topic sentence a complete statement, narrow, and not a question, an announcement or a fact?
- Can I name its topic, controlling idea and predictors?
- Do my supporting sentences explain the predictors in the same order, with the details as the longest part?
- Did I capitalize names, places, nationalities, languages, days, months and "I"?
- Is the paragraph indented and double spaced, without a new line for each sentence?
