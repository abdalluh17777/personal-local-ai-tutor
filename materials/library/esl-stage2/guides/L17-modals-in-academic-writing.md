# Lesson 17: Modals in Academic Writing

*English Stage 2 (B2) · Unit 2: Sentences and Paragraph Types · American History*
*Adapted from Building Academic Writing Skills by Lin Cui, Harper College (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): Unit 15 Modals.*

## In this lesson
In Lesson 16 you learned the eight main modals, their form and their common errors. This lesson builds on it: you will learn modal-like expressions (be able to, be supposed to, ought to, had better, would rather), the difference between must not and do not have to, and how to choose a modal by its meaning, its time and its strength. Start with the Pretest in Practice, before you read the rules. Then practice with sentences about customs around the world, and write about customs in your own culture.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Use modal-like expressions (to + base verb) with be or have in the right form.
- Choose a modal by its meaning, its time (past, present or future) and its strength.
- Tell the difference between must not (it is not allowed) and do not have to (it is not necessary).
- Avoid forms that do not exist (mayn't, mightn't) and conversational expressions in formal writing.
- Write a paragraph about a custom with a variety of modals.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| modal-like expression | an expression with to that works like a modal: be able to, be supposed to, have to, have got to, ought to | They are supposed to explain the reasons if they are late. |
| time reference | the time a verb refers to: past, present or future | had to follow (past), must follow (present and future) |
| prohibition (n) | things that are not allowed | People must not whistle inside a Mongolian ger. |
| etiquette (n) | a rule or tradition for proper social behavior | Americans and Europeans have opposite dining etiquettes. |
| gesture (n) | a movement of hands, arms, or another part of the body to express an idea or emotion | the "OK" gesture with the thumb and index finger |
| utensil (n) | a dining tool such as a spoon or a fork | Kenyans do not use utensils. |
| communal (adj) | used or shared by everyone in the group | People in Morocco practice communal eating. |
| in isolation | separately, apart from each other | Try not to study modals in isolation. |
| taboo (n) | an unacceptable word or behavior especially in a group setting | Explain one of the taboos for a wedding ceremony. |
| improper (adj) | not appropriate, not right | They might think it improper for the children to see their intimacy. |
| reflect (v) | show, represent | A uniform reflects the school and its reputation. |

## Rules and explanations

### Review from Lesson 16
modal + base form of the main verb = complete verb. In the United States, people should call ahead before visiting someone. (not calls, called, calling, to call)

### Three more principles
1. **Contractions:** shouldn't, mustn't and can't are common, but may and might have no contraction. Write may not and might not, never "mayn't" or "mightn't": Small children may not stay alone at home in the United States.
2. **Modal-like expressions** contain the word to, and the base verb follows it: be able to, be supposed to, have to, have got to, ought to. The be or have part changes with the subject and the tense: People have to come on time for an appointment in the United States. They are supposed to explain the reasons if they are late.
3. **Conversation only:** some modals are used mainly in conversation, for example had better and have got to: In Thailand, people had better not touch the head of a statue. In an academic paragraph, should, must or have to are usually the better choice.

### Three challenges and one strategy
- The same modal can have different meanings: I can drive a car because I have a driver's license. (permission) / I can drive a car because I know how to drive. (ability)
- The same meaning can have different strengths: a person must have a driver's license (stronger) / has to have a driver's license (less strong).
- Some modals look like the past but mean the present or the future: Some traditions might change quickly. People had better learn the customs of another country when they travel there.
- **Strategy:** learn each modal as a "package" of meaning + time + form, not in isolation. In many countries in the past, young people had to follow the custom of the arranged marriage. (necessity, past: had to + follow) In some countries nowadays, some young people still must follow the custom. (necessity, present and future: must + follow)

### Meanings, modals and time
| Meaning | Present and future | Past | Example from the book |
|---|---|---|---|
| advice, suggestion | should, ought to (less common), can / could (softer) | | Customers should tip the waiters or waitresses for their service. |
| ability | can, be able to | could, was / were able to | People in ancient India could speak Sanskrit. |
| permission | can, may; not allowed: cannot, may not | could, could not | In Canada, college students can address / may address their professors by the first name. |
| necessity, obligation | must, have to | had to | Men have to stand to greet a woman when she enters the room. |
| no necessity | do not have to | | In Iraqi culture, people do not have to kiss each other as a way of greeting. |
| prohibition | must not | | People must not whistle inside a Mongolian ger. |
| possibility | must, may, might, could (+ not) | | This custom may change / might change / could change in the near future. |
| expectation | be supposed to | was / were supposed to | Last year, Natalia's husband was supposed to buy flowers for her, but he forgot. |
| preference | would rather (+ than), would rather not | | Some young people would rather not get into a marriage than marry someone they do not love. |

### Must not or do not have to?
- **must not** = it is not allowed: In Thailand and Arabic countries, people must not point their foot or shoe toward others.
- **do not have to** = it is not necessary; you can choose: In Iraqi culture, people do not have to kiss each other as a way of greeting. Handshaking is common.

## Writing task
Exercise 9: choose two of these topics and write a paragraph of 8-12 sentences for each.
1. one of the holiday dining traditions in your native country
2. some customs about naming a baby in your native country
3. one of the taboos for a wedding ceremony in your native country
4. Some people say that elderly parents should live with their adult children. Do you agree or disagree? Why?
5. In America, it is common to "go Dutch" (split the food bill in the restaurant). Is this practice common in your native culture? Who usually pays for the meals?
6. the joys and challenges of living in two cultures, and your biggest culture shock (if you have not lived abroad, write about a new city or school)

Each paragraph needs a topic sentence, supporting details and a concluding sentence. Use at least five different modals or modal-like expressions with at least three meanings (for example necessity, prohibition, expectation, preference, possibility).

Checklist:
- Is every modal followed by a base verb (must eat, not must eats)?
- Do your modal-like expressions have the right form of be or have (are supposed to, has to, was able to)?
- Did you write may not and might not (not mayn't, mightn't)?
- Did you use must not for things that are not allowed and do not have to for things that are not necessary?
- Is the time right (had to and could for the past)?
- Did you replace had better and have got to with should, must or have to?

Paste your draft into the chat and ask your tutor for feedback. Then rewrite it.
