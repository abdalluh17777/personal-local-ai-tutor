## In this lesson
In Lesson 8 you studied the paragraph format, its parts, the title and the topic sentence. Now you will study the rest: supporting sentences and details, transitions and concluding sentences, and three characteristics of a good paragraph: unity, cohesion and completion. You will finish with a complete paragraph of your own.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Plan supporting ideas by predicting the readers' question about the topic sentence.
- Check an outline or a paragraph for unity and remove irrelevant ideas.
- Recognize four types of supporting details.
- Use transitions to give a paragraph cohesion.
- Write a concluding sentence and check that a paragraph is complete.

## Rules and explanations
### Supporting sentences
Supporting sentences "hold up" the main idea, like a bridge holding up a man: they must support or explain the main idea in the topic sentence. A good strategy is to **predict the question the readers may ask** about your topic sentence. The answers are your supporting ideas, and the result is your outline (Lesson 4). Example: *Young people should not live in their parents' house after graduating from college.* Question: Why not? Supports: practice independence in daily life; experience real meaning of life; learn to appreciate parents.

### Unity
Unity comes from the verb "unite". In a paragraph, it means that all the supporting sentences serve the same purpose: explaining the main idea. One sunflower in a bouquet of roses looks out of place; so does one irrelevant idea in a paragraph. To check unity:
- Ask: "Does each supporting sentence explain the **controlling idea** in the topic sentence?" If not, delete or change it. Circle the controlling idea to make checking easier.
- Always make an outline first. Put any new idea into the outline and ask the same question.

**The book's example** (*Taking online classes helps college students in three significant ways.*): "hard to focus because of noisy children", "not get professor's in-person help" and "difficult materials, questions not answered right away" do not show how online classes **help**, and "manage time better" repeats Support 2. The book crosses these words out in the improved outline.

### Types of supporting details
- **facts:** numbers, general truths, scientific truths
- **reasons:** logical explanations
- **experts' opinions:** research findings, quotes by experts in the field
- **examples:** stories of well-known people, personal experiences, personal quotes

In "Good Roommates", the details are personal examples of Abia and the writer (all three supports), a quote by an expert (writer Barbara Dana, in Support 2), and a general truth and logic (Support 3).

### Transitions and cohesion
Cohesion means the ideas flow smoothly, like a waterfall. Transitions (connecting words) organize the writer's thoughts and guide the readers, between the supporting sentences and inside them.
- A transition for the **first** supporting idea is often optional.
- "Finally" shows the **last supporting idea** in the body. It is not used right before the conclusion.
- A transition before the concluding sentence is optional; it is more common without one.
- Most transitions are followed by a comma, but not all (different types have different punctuation rules).
- Do not overuse transitions, or the paragraph will sound unnatural.

| Meaning | Transitions |
|---|---|
| Addition | first, second, then, next, in addition, also, furthermore, moreover, last, finally |
| Contrast | but, yet, on the other hand, on the contrary, however, nevertheless, though, even though, although |
| Time | first, then, next, after that, soon, in the beginning, at first, at last, before, after, later, when, while, until |
| Reason | for, due to, because of, because, since, as |
| Result | so, therefore, thus, consequently, as a result |
| Location | near, beside, next to, in front, behind, around, under, opposite, above, below |
| Conclusion | in conclusion, in summary, to sum up, in brief, for those reasons |
| Example | like, such as, including, for example, for instance |

### Concluding sentences
A concluding sentence signals the end of the paragraph. You can write one to three sentences that:
- **restate** the main idea in different words or sentence structure;
- **summarize** the main points of the body;
- express an **opinion**, make a **prediction**, give a **recommendation**, or ask a **question** related to the topic.

A conclusion must **not** bring up a new topic. Wrong: *For those three reasons, I enjoy swimming the most. I will also start playing basketball next week.*

The book's examples for the topic sentence *Swimming is my favorite hobby.*:
- restate: For those three reasons, I enjoy swimming the most.
- restate + summarize: For the benefits of physical health, mental wellbeing, and social life, I enjoy swimming the most.
- restate + opinion: (restatement +) It is the activity that has kept me healthy in the last ten years.
- restate + question: (restatement +) Shouldn't everyone start this hobby, too?

### Paragraph completion
A paragraph is complete when it has a title, a topic sentence, details for every predictor, proper transitions and a concluding sentence. If one part is missing, it is incomplete. The book's jogging paragraph has no title, and its topic sentence promises physical, mental and social benefits, but the social benefits are never explained.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic texts often link many sentences with "and" (wa). In English, use the transition that shows the real relationship (however, for example, as a result...), and do not start sentence after sentence with "And".

**Self-editing checklist**
- Does every supporting sentence explain the controlling idea (unity)?
- Did I use at least two types of details (facts, reasons, experts' opinions, examples)?
- Did I use transitions with the right meaning, a comma after most of them, and not too many?
- Is "Finally" used for the last supporting idea? Does my conclusion restate the main idea without a new topic?
- Is the paragraph complete: title, topic sentence, every predictor explained, details, transitions, conclusion?
