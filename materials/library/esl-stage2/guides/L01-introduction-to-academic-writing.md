## In this lesson
You will learn what academic writing is, its four main characteristics, and ten wrong ideas about writing that are common among ESL students. You will also start a habit for the whole course: Non-Stop Non-Translation (NSNT) free writing, which trains you to think and write in English without translating. These are the foundations for every paragraph, essay and report you write in your psychology course.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Define academic writing and name the main purposes of writing.
- Explain the four characteristics of academic writing and how to achieve each one.
- Recognize the ten common misconceptions about writing and choose the advice that helps you most.
- Practise NSNT free writing, following its four steps.
- Write a short paragraph about academic writing or your own writing experience.

## Rules and explanations
### What is writing for?
The simplest answer: to communicate meaning. People write for different purposes:
- **to inform:** news stories, technical manuals
- **to entertain:** fiction, movie scripts
- **in daily life:** a short reminder note, a party invitation, a love letter
- **at work:** meeting memos, business reports
- **in colleges and universities:** paragraphs, essays, research papers, lab reports. This last type is **academic writing**.

### Four characteristics of academic writing
| Characteristic | What it means | How to achieve it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Logical and direct | A linear style, like a vertical line: main idea → supporting ideas → conclusion. | State your focus early. Do not "hide" your point or give hints until the end. |
| 2. Formal | Standard English. | Learn formal grammar and usage. Do not use colloquial or spoken English. |
| 3. Clear | Readers can understand your thoughts. | Do not translate from your native language. Always proofread. |
| 4. Concise | No unnecessary repetition. | Repeat a word or idea only when you need to emphasize a point. |

**Writing is a process.** Almost no one writes perfectly on the first try, and there is no such thing as perfect writing. Good writing takes time, repeated revisions, patience and continuous effort.

### Ten common misconceptions about writing
Read the full explanations in the lesson material (section IV). In short:
1. **"Translating sophisticated words and sentences makes my writing better."** No: meaning and clarity get lost. Start with the words and sentence structures you know. A simple text that readers understand is better than a difficult one they cannot follow.
2. **"I do not know what to write, so I google ideas."** Just start writing, and plan backwards from the due date. Relying on the Internet leads to plagiarism and limits your own thinking.
3. **"A general topic is easier."** Good writing is deep, not broad. Narrow the topic to a unique angle.
4. **"Prewriting is a waste of time."** Brainstorming gives you ideas; an outline helps you catch problems before you write.
5. **"I keep adding new things while I write."** First put the new idea into your outline and check that it supports the main idea.
6. **"Tutoring is only for weak students."** Tutoring is for every student; strong students use it most.
7. **"My professor will correct every error."** Professors often mark only the type of error; you learn to correct it yourself.
8. **"Family and friends are the best helpers."** They may do the work for you without explaining, so you do not learn from your mistakes.
9. **"Grammar is not important."** Grammar is the foundation of good writing.
10. **"I do not need to read in a writing class."** Good writers are almost always good readers.

### NSNT free writing (Appendix A)
**Why think in English while writing?**
- No two languages are exactly the same, so meaning gets lost or misunderstood in direct translation.
- Translating takes longer, and academic study means a lot of reading and writing.
- "Old habits die hard": the longer you translate, the harder it is to stop.

**Four steps**
1. Use a special notebook or computer file and write the date. Write nonstop in English. Try three minutes, four times a week, then slowly write longer and more often. Use a timer.
2. Choose your own topic or a prompt. It is OK to wander to a different topic. Keep only one language in your mind: English.
3. If you run out of ideas, keep writing: "I don't know what more to write..." or "Monday, Tuesday..." Do not lift your pen or stop typing.
4. Do not worry about grammar and spelling, and do not stop to use a dictionary. Correct mistakes afterwards if you want.

The book's example (1/15/2022) is typical of the first days or weeks: short ideas, many mistakes, but all in English. Progress may take a semester or more, like building muscles in a gym.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic has no "is/are" in present-tense sentences such as "the teacher kind", so word-for-word translation often leaves out "be": "My teacher very kind" → "My teacher **is** very kind."
> - Arabic writing often joins many ideas with "and". In English academic writing, long chains of "and" make a text unclear and not concise; end the sentence and start a new one.

## Writing task
**Part 1: A paragraph (Unit Review Practice).** Choose one topic and write one paragraph of about 150 to 200 words:
- What is academic writing? What are the characteristics of academic writing?
- What has been your best writing experience? Your most challenging writing experience?
- Which of the ten misconceptions apply to you? What advice will be the most helpful to you?

Put your main idea in the first sentence, explain it with two or three supporting ideas, and finish with a concluding sentence. Write directly in English; do not translate.

**Part 2: NSNT free writing.** Choose two Week 1 prompts from Appendix A (for example: What are your plans for this semester? How are you going to study differently from how you studied in the past? What does "academic honesty" mean to you? What are your expectations of this course?). Write nonstop for three to five minutes on each, without translating and without a dictionary.

**Self-editing checklist (Part 1)**
- Is my main idea in the first sentence (logical and direct)?
- Does every sentence explain the main idea?
- Did I use formal English (no colloquial or spoken English)?
- Did I write directly in English instead of translating?
- Did I avoid repeating the same words and ideas (concise)?
- Does every sentence have a subject and a verb, including "is/are" where needed?
- Did I proofread for spelling, capital letters and periods?

Paste your draft into the chat and ask your tutor for feedback. Then rewrite it.
