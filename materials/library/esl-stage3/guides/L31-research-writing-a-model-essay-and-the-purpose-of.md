# Lesson 31: Research Writing: A Model Essay and the Purpose of Research

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 3.1 Introduction; 3.2 Sample Student Research Essay: Faculty of Color; 3.3 Purpose of Research Writing.*

## In this lesson
You read a student research essay, "Where are the Professors Who Look Like Me?", think about why people do research, and compare how an academic paper and an online article show their sources. You also choose topics for your own small research project, which continues until Lesson 39. University courses, including psychology, expect you to find sources, judge them and use them to support your own ideas.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain why people do research and write research papers.
- Name the four steps that everyday research and academic research share.
- Find the thesis, the evidence and the writer's explanations in a model research essay.
- Compare how a research paper and an online article show where their information comes from.
- Choose possible topics for your own research project.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| research paper | an essay that uses information from sources to support the writer's own argument | Lily's essay on minority faculty |
| research question | the question that you try to answer through your research | Why is it important to have more minority faculty in colleges? |
| source | a book, article, website, video, etc. that gives information | an EdSource article |
| cite, citation | to show where you got information from; the reference that shows it | "... are people of color (Peele and Willis)." |
| in-text citation | the author's name (and page, if there is one) in the sentence that uses the source | (Stout et al) |
| Works Cited | the list of all sources at the end of a paper in MLA style | the last page of Lily's essay |
| credible, credibility | can be trusted; the quality of being trusted | Do the links give the writer more credibility? |
| faculty (of color) | the teachers of a college or university (who are not white) | Almost all of my professors have been white. |
| underrepresented | present in smaller numbers than in the population as a whole | Minority faculty members are underrepresented. |
| In other words, ... | introduces your own explanation of a source | In other words, minority faculty can inspire students. |
| We can infer that ... | introduces a conclusion that you draw from evidence | We can infer that Jesus and students like him would feel less "erased" ... |
| qtd. in | "quoted in": the words come from a person quoted in another writer's source | (qtd. in Peele and Willis) |

## Rules and explanations

### Why we research
- You already do research, for example to find the cheapest laptop: you search online, compare results, decide how much you trust each one, make a decision and explain it to others. Academic research has the same four steps: **find** information, **analyze** it, **make a decision**, and **share** it.
- In a research paper, you use the facts and opinions you find to support **your own argument**. You find out what others know and believe about a topic and use it to guide your own thinking. This helps you clarify your ideas, think critically and share your own voice persuasively.

### A model research essay: what to notice
Lily Liu De Li, an ESL student at Laney College in Oakland, California, wrote "Where are the Professors Who Look Like Me? Increasing Minority Faculty in Higher Education". The chapter follows her research journey.

| Part | What Lily does |
|---|---|
| Introduction | her own experience, a statistic with a citation, then the thesis (last sentence) |
| Body paragraphs | a topic sentence (her claim), evidence from sources (statistics, quotations, paraphrases) with citations, then her own explanation |
| Conclusion | the problem and its main cause again, then her personal hope |
| Works Cited | the full details of her sources, in alphabetical order (two are missing: see Exercise 3, question 8) |

The pattern of a research paragraph is **claim, cited evidence, explanation**. Claim: faculty of color "bring many benefits to their campuses". Evidence: "Students of color are more likely to graduate if they have more minority faculty (Stout et al)." Explanation: "This is an enormous benefit and should cause colleges to rethink their policies."

### How sources are shown
An academic paper uses **in-text citations** and a **Works Cited page**: "... only about 40 percent of the professors are people of color (Peele and Willis)." An online article uses **links**: in "About four in five public school teachers are white", the words "are white" link to official data. Both let readers check the information. Remember the rhetorical appeals (Lesson 13): **ethos** (the writer's credibility), **pathos** (emotion), **logos** (logic, facts, evidence).

### Your research project (Lessons 31 to 39)
While you follow Lily, you do a small project in the same steps: topics (Lesson 31); a narrow topic and research question (32); keywords, free sources and evaluation (33); reading articles and an annotated bibliography (34); evidence and citations (35 to 39). Choose any topic you care about. One psychology option, used as the example in these guides: **sleep and memory in university students** (PSYC 110 discusses memory; the free OpenStax textbook *Psychology 2e* covers sleep in chapter 4 and memory in chapter 8).

> **Psychology note:** Lily uses MLA style (Modern Language Association), common in English classes. Psychology uses APA style (American Psychological Association): the in-text citation includes the year, for example (Davis & Fry, 2019), and the list at the end is called References.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - The Arabic word for "research" has a plural, but English *research* is uncountable: "do research", "a research project", "a study, two studies". Do not write "a research" or "many researches".

**Exercise 4. Notice this! An online research article (3.3).** Read Lasana D. Kazembe's article in the lesson material, then skim the original online (see "Watch online"), which has links.

3. Kazembe writes, "Research indicates that students of color benefit from being taught by people who look like them." Which sentence in Lily's first body paragraph makes a similar point, and how does she show her source?

**Exercise 5. One student's research journey (end of 3.3).** Lily noticed that few of her instructors were Asian American, Black, Latinx or Native American. How would you research this topic? Write three or four questions you would want to answer.

**Exercise 6. Start your project.** Write down three topics that you care about and would like to read about for several weeks. Include at least one psychology topic.

## Read aloud
Sentences from Lily's essay. Pause briefly at each / and stress the words in bold.
1. While almost **75 percent** of California Community College students are people of color, / only about **40 percent** of the professors are people of color.
2. Students of color are **more likely** to graduate / if they have more minority faculty.
3. In other words, / minority faculty can **inspire** students / and show them a path forward.
4. As this quotation suggests, / individual people may **know** what needs to be done to make changes, / but they might not "reach out" and ask for changes / if they don't have **social support**.

## Writing task
Write one paragraph of 200 to 250 words. Choose one topic:
1. In your education, were your teachers of the same background as you? How did that affect your learning? Use one piece of evidence from Lily's essay and say where it comes from ("In her research essay, Lily Liu De Li reports that ...").
2. Describe one piece of everyday research that you did. Then explain how it is similar to academic research, using the four steps: find, analyze, decide, share.

Include a clear claim, specific support, an explanation ("In other words, ...") and a concluding sentence.

Self-editing checklist:
- Does my topic sentence make one clear claim?
- Is my support specific, not only general statements?
- Did I explain how my support proves the claim?
- If I used information from Lily's essay, did I say where it comes from?
- Did I put exact words from a text in quotation marks?
- Did I use "research" as an uncountable noun?
- Are my sentences complete, without run-ons or comma splices?

Paste your draft into the chat and ask your tutor for feedback. Then rewrite it.
