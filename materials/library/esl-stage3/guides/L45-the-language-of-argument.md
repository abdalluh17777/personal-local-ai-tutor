# Lesson 45: The Language of Argument

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 5: Argument and Academic Style*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 5.10 Language Toolkit; 5.11 Takeaways.*

## In this lesson
You will learn fifteen key academic words for describing arguments: words for disagreement, logical relationships, reasoning and evaluation. You will practice their meanings, word forms and grammar patterns, review the sentence patterns for concession and counterargument, and look back at the whole argument chapter. These words appear constantly in research writing, including psychology articles (correlation, factor, findings, valid).

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Use fifteen argumentation terms with the right meaning, word form and grammar pattern.
- Explain the difference between correlation and causation, and between random and arbitrary.
- Choose the right language for each part of a concession/counterargument paragraph.
- Analyze a persuasive text with the terms and strategies from Lessons 40 to 45.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| perspective (n) | a position, point of view or attitude; sometimes shaped by a person's experience or identity | My grandmother has lived long enough to have a broader perspective. |
| con·tro·VER·sial (adj) | causing a debate of strong opinions on different sides | Wilde was a controversial author; many loved him, but many hated him. |
| pro·PO·nent / op·PO·nent (n) | someone who supports / is against an idea, a plan or another person | Opponents of bilingual education may argue that it doesn't work, but they're mistaken. |
| cau·SA·tion (n) | proof that one event really did cause another | ...there wasn't enough evidence to show causation. |
| consistent (adj) | matching; making a predictable pattern; making sense together | The damage to the building is consistent with the theory that an electrical problem caused the fire. |
| cor·re·LA·tion (n) | two events, actions or amounts that are connected or happen at the same time | Social media use is correlated with low self-esteem. |
| factor (n) | one part of a cause that has more than one part | Two factors that influence the price of clothing are labor cost and materials cost. |
| random (adj) | not following any pattern or rules; unpredictable | Whether you get heads or tails when flipping a coin is purely random. |
| AR·bi·trar·y (adj) | chosen by a person without a particular reason or a consistent, fair rule | International law protects people from arbitrary imprisonment. |
| assumption (n) | an idea someone believes without having all the evidence | The professor made the assumption that the student didn't care about the class. |
| findings (n, usually plural) | the information or answers from research | The findings of the clinical trial contradicted our prediction. |
| contradiction (n) | two statements or actions by the same person that cannot both be true | She pointed out a contradiction between the government's ideas and its actual policy. |
| cred·i·BIL·i·ty (n) | the quality of deserving to be believed and trusted | After they were wrong about a major election, the polling organization lost credibility with the public. |
| valid (adj) | reasonable, sensible, acceptable, logical | The economist made a valid point that a livable minimum wage is a different amount in different countries. |

## Rules and explanations

### Word forms and grammar patterns
| Term | Related forms (from the book) | Patterns to notice |
|---|---|---|
| perspective | count noun | a perspective on something; a different / broader / historical perspective |
| controversial | controversy (count or non-count noun) | a controversial issue / author / decision |
| proponent, opponent | propose (verb); oppose (verb), opposition (non-count noun) | proponents of X; opponents of X; oppose X (no preposition); opposition from someone |
| cause | cause (usually a count noun; also a verb); causal (adj); causation (non-count noun) | one cause of X; X caused Y; a causal relationship between X and Y |
| consistent | inconsistent; consistency, inconsistency (nouns); consistently, inconsistently (adverbs) | consistent with X |
| correlation | correlated (adj) | a correlation between X and Y; X is correlated with Y |
| factor | count noun | an important factor in...; confounding factors |
| random | | a random sample |
| arbitrary | arbitrarily (adv) | an arbitrary decision; arbitrarily chosen |
| assumption | assume (verb + object or that-clause) | make the assumption that...; based on the assumption that... |
| findings | usually plural | the findings of a study indicated / contradicted... |
| contradiction | contradict (verb + object); contradictory (adj) | a contradiction between X and Y; X contradicts Y |
| credibility | credible (adj) | establish / lose credibility; a credible source |
| valid | validity (non-count noun) | a valid point / question; an argument lacks validity |

### Meanings to keep apart
- **Correlation vs. causation.** A correlation means two things happen together. Causation means one really caused the other "in a way we can prove." The book's example: at first, people thought drinking coffee caused lung cancer, but coffee drinking was correlated with cigarette smoking, which was the actual cause (Lesson 43: false cause).
- **Confounding factors.** "The study designers needed to make sure there were no confounding factors, so they made sure the subjects were evenly distributed between the groups in every way." A confounding factor is a third factor that could explain a result. Psychology experiments deal with such factors in the same way, by making the groups as similar as possible.
- **Random vs. arbitrary.** Both mean "without a pattern," but arbitrary describes a person's decision made without a consistent or fair rule, so it often suggests "unfair" when used about people in power. A coin toss is random. "Was there a system for choosing which students were called on? No, it was the teacher's arbitrary decision."
- **Valid.** In logic, a valid syllogism is one whose conclusion must follow from its premises (Lesson 42). In everyday academic use, a valid point or question is reasonable and acceptable.

### Review: the language of the chapter
- **Concession and counterargument** (Lesson 44): show whose perspective you are presenting, and signal each change of perspective. The same language also works in smaller pieces, inside a single sentence.
- **Refuting fallacies** (Lesson 43): source + reporting phrase + paraphrase; transition + fallacy; explanation.
- In this chapter you identified ethos, pathos and logos; analyzed syllogisms; found, named and refuted fallacies; compared perspectives with concession and counterargument; and practiced hedging.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Non-count nouns such as credibility, causation, validity, opposition (and evidence) have no plural in English: write "more evidence", not "evidences". Findings, in contrast, is almost always plural.

**Exercise 1: Argumentation terms review.** Choose the best word for each blank from the three choices in brackets. Use the meaning and the grammar pattern to help you decide.
1. The union was negotiating for a pay raise ________ with the cost of living for the region. (opponent/consistent/random)
2. Opponents of a boycott raised a ________ point, that the boycott might actually hurt the workers it was meant to help. (valid/findings/factor)
3. After the scientists recorded the amount of pesticides in the runoff from the cotton fields, they presented their ________ at an environmental health conference. (credibility/random/findings)
4. The decision to strike was ________; some of the workers were convinced it was the only path forward, but others were afraid to lose their jobs or even face retaliation. (contradiction/controversial/correlation)
5. The lack of economic opportunities in rural areas is one ________ driving many women to move to cities in search of factory jobs. (factor/arbitrary/assume)
6. Research on decision-making shows that when we feel a ________ between our behavior and our values, such as when we are shopping for unethically-made products, we feel very uncomfortable, and usually change our values to match our behavior. (consistent/credibility/contradiction)

## Writing task
Find an essay, article, advertisement or political speech, and write an analysis of 250-300 words: what persuasive appeals does the writer use, and are they effective? For a psychology option, choose a news article that reports a psychology study (for example, from the free psychology section of The Conversation) or "Untrustworthy Memories Make it Hard to Shop Ethically" from Lesson 42.

Include:
- the writer's thesis and the syllogism under it (major premise, minor premise);
- at least two appeals (ethos, pathos, logos), each with a specific example;
- any logical fallacy, named and explained;
- whether the writer addresses other perspectives, and how;
- an example of hedging, or a claim that should be hedged;
- your evaluation of how effective the text is;
- at least six terms from this lesson.

Checklist:
- Did you state the writer's thesis and premises clearly?
- Did you support each point about an appeal with a specific example from the text?
- Did you explain each fallacy, not just name it?
- Did you use the terms with the right forms and patterns (correlated with, consistent with, proponents of, oppose without "to")?
- Did you keep non-count nouns singular (evidence, credibility) and findings plural?
- Did you hedge your own evaluation where you cannot be certain?
