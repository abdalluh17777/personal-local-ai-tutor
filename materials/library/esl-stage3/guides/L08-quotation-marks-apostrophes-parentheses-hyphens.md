# Lesson 08: Quotation Marks, Apostrophes, Parentheses, Hyphens and Capital Letters

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 1: Refining Sentences and Punctuation*
*Adapted from College ESL Writers: Applied Grammar and Composing Strategies for Success by Barbara Hall and Elizabeth Wallace, Georgia State University - Perimeter College (CC BY-NC-SA): 8.4: Quotes; 8.5: Apostrophes; 8.6: Parentheses; 8.8: Hyphens; 8.9: Capitalization; 8.10: End-of-Chapter (Exercise).*

## In this lesson
You will learn to punctuate quotations and titles, use apostrophes, parentheses and hyphens, and follow the main rules for capital letters. Then you will review the whole chapter. These marks show whose words you are using and what belongs to whom, which is essential when you write about sources.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Punctuate direct quotations, quotations within quotations and titles, and tell direct from indirect quotations.
- Use apostrophes to show possession with singular and plural nouns and in contractions, and tell *it's* from *its*.
- Use parentheses for secondary information and hyphens for words that work as one adjective.
- Capitalize sentences, proper nouns, days, months, holidays and titles, but not common nouns.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| quotation marks (" ") | marks around a direct quotation (someone's exact words) or the title of a short work | Carly said, "I'm not ever going back there again." |
| indirect quotation | a report of someone's words, not exact; no quotation marks | Carly said that she would never go back there. |
| single quotation marks (' ') | marks for a quotation inside a quotation | "The instructions say, 'Tighten the screws one at a time.'" |
| apostrophe (') | a mark for possession (who or what owns something) or for missing letters | the dog's leash; don't |
| contraction | two words made into one, with an apostrophe for the missing letters; informal | we will = we'll |
| parentheses ( ) | a pair of marks around secondary information | ...the worst movie I have seen (so far). |
| hyphen (-) | a short mark that joins words working as one adjective, or divides a word at the end of a line | habit-forming |
| proper noun / common noun | the name of a particular person, place, thing or event (capital letter) / a general word (no capital) | Apollo Theater / theater |

## Rules and explanations

### Quotation marks
- A **direct quotation** gives someone's exact words in quotation marks; an **indirect quotation** restates them without quotation marks.
- You can identify the speaker at the beginning, in the middle or at the end:

| Position | Example |
|---|---|
| beginning | Madison said, "Let's stop at the farmers market to buy some fresh vegetables for dinner." |
| middle | "Let's stop at the farmers market," Madison said, "to buy some fresh vegetables for dinner." |
| end | "Let's stop at the farmers market to buy some fresh vegetables for dinner," Madison said. |

- Capitalize the first word of a quoted sentence. When the speaker comes in the middle, the second part continues the same sentence, so it starts with a small letter. (A short phrase that fits into your own sentence also starts small, as in the next example.)
- Put a comma between the identifying words and the quotation. Commas and periods go **inside** the closing quotation marks. Question marks and exclamation points go inside only if they belong to the quoted words: The new employee asked, "When is lunch?" / Did you hear her say you were "the next Picasso"?
- A quotation inside a quotation takes single marks: Theresa said, "I wanted to take my dog to the festival, but the man at the gate said, 'No dogs allowed.'"
- **Titles:** quotation marks for short works (essays, songs, poems, short stories, chapters): "Annabel Lee" is one of my favorite romantic poems. (The book spells Poe's title "Annabelle Lee".) Long works (books, magazines, albums, newspapers, novels) are in italics when typed and underlined when handwritten: *The New York Times*.
- **Usage varies:** these are American rules. British writers often use single marks first, and put commas and periods outside the marks when they are not part of the quotation.

### Apostrophes
| Noun | Add | Example |
|---|---|---|
| singular (also when it ends in s; some writers add only ' here: Jess') | 's | Jen's dance routine / Jess's sister |
| plural ending in s | ' | The drummers' sticks |
| plural not ending in s | 's | The people's votes |

- In a **contraction**, the apostrophe shows where letters are missing: do not = don't; let us = let's. Contractions are used in informal writing, not in formal writing.
- *it's* = it is (or it has); *its* = belonging to it. Test: if "it is" makes sense, write *it's*. It's cold and rainy outside. / The cat was chasing its tail.

### Parentheses
- Parentheses are always used in pairs and contain information that is secondary to the meaning of the sentence. The sentence must still make sense without the words in parentheses, so never put the subject or the verb of the sentence inside them.
- Your spinach and garlic salad is one of the most delicious (and nutritious) foods I have ever tasted!

### Hyphens
- Join words that work together as one description: The fifty-five-year-old athlete was just as qualified for the marathon as his younger opponents. / it can be habit-forming / the mid-year review
- Divide a word between two syllables at the end of a line. If you are not sure where, check a dictionary or move the whole word to the next line.

### Capital letters
(Pictures with some of these rules are missing from the lesson material.)
- Capitalize the first word of a sentence.
- Capitalize **proper nouns**: the names of specific people, places, objects, streets, buildings and events, and titles of individuals (Dr. Jackson, Uncle Javier).
- Always capitalize nationalities, races, languages and religions: American, African American, Hispanic, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist.
- Capitalize days of the week, months and holidays.
- In titles of works, capitalize the first word and the important words; short words such as *a*, *the*, *and*, *of*, *on* and *to* stay small unless they come first: *Pride and Prejudice*.
- Do not capitalize a **common noun** used in a general way: museum / The Art Institute of Chicago; country / Malaysia; college / Smith College; historical event / The Renaissance.
- The book capitalizes *Internet*, but never *e-mail* or *online*. Many style guides now write *internet*.
- Whole words in capitals read like shouting; do not use them for emphasis.

**Psychology note:** in an APA reference list, article and chapter titles have no quotation marks, and only the first word, the first word after a colon and proper nouns are capitalized.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
>
> - Arabic script has no capital letters, so capitals are easy to forget. Check every name, nationality, language, religion, day and month (Monday, March, Arabic, Muslim) and the pronoun *I*.

4. "I know," said Russell, "let's go to the Two Roads Juice Bar." (Because "I know" is a complete sentence, this is even better: "I know," said Russell. "Let's go ...")

7. "I didn't!" exclaimed Yasmin. "Which poem?"

9. "Oh!" said Yasmin. "Is that the one that starts with the line, 'Two roads diverged in a yellow wood'?" ## Writing task
**A one-page autobiography** (the book's writing application). Write 250 to 350 words about the people, places, dates and events that shaped your life. You may focus on one theme, for example how you learned English, or a person who influenced your development (a PSYC 110 topic). Include a direct quotation (something someone said to you), a title, two possessive nouns, a hyphenated description and information in parentheses.

Checklist:
- Names, places, nationalities, languages, religions, days, months and holidays have capitals; common nouns (university, uncle, river) do not, unless they are part of a name.
- Titles have capitals on the first and important words; short works are in quotation marks, long works in italics.
- Direct quotations have quotation marks, with commas and periods inside; indirect quotations have none.
- Possessive nouns have 's or (plural ending in s) only '; simple plurals and *its* have no apostrophe.
- I used contractions only inside quotations, because this is formal writing.
- Each sentence with parentheses still makes sense without the words inside them.
