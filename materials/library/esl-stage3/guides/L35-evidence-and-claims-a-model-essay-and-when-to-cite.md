# Lesson 35: Evidence and Claims: A Model Essay and When to Cite

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 4: Research and Evidence*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 4.1 Introduction; 4.2 Sample Student Research Essay: Heritage Languages; 4.3 Using Evidence to Support Claims; 4.4 Knowing When to Cite.*

## In this lesson
You read a student research essay on heritage languages and study how the writer supports her claims with different types of evidence. You learn when a sentence needs a citation, and you write a body paragraph for your research project. Good evidence, correctly cited, makes your writing persuasive and honest.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain why academic writers support claims with evidence and cite their sources.
- Identify five types of evidence in a text.
- Choose evidence from an article for a topic sentence.
- Decide whether a sentence needs a citation.
- Write a body paragraph that combines cited evidence with your own explanation.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| claim | a statement that the writer wants readers to accept; it needs support | Families should keep trying to maintain their mother tongue. |
| evidence, support | information that shows why a claim is true | facts, statistics, quotations, examples |
| statistic | a fact that relies on numbers | Roughly 3 in 4 of these English learners speak Spanish. |
| anecdotal evidence | evidence from personal stories and experience | my cousins in Switzerland |
| general knowledge | something that everybody knows | Spanish is spoken in many countries. |
| heritage language | the language of a person's family, often an immigrant family (also: home language, mother tongue) | "Keeping Korean means keeping our roots." |
| According to X, ... / X insists that ... | introduce a source's words or ideas | According to Ruth Lingxin Yan, ... |
| [ ] and . . . | square brackets show a change to a quotation; an ellipsis shows words left out | "We . . . spen[t] a whole summer in Korea" |

## Rules and explanations

### Why use evidence and cite it?
- Most academic writing uses other texts (books, articles, films) as evidence for **your** argument. Choose the best evidence and connect it smoothly to your own ideas.
- Showing where information comes from makes your writing more persuasive, lets readers check it, and shows **academic honesty**: you do not claim others' ideas as your own.
- A reflective or response essay uses more personal experiences, observations and examples. A research paper relies on quotations, reasons and facts; personal experience only adds to points that your research already supports.

### Five types of evidence (the book's examples)
| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| General facts (cited if you learned them through research) | Some parents believe that it is more important to learn the new language of the new country where they live (Brown 31). |
| Statistics (cited) | More than ten percent of children in the US public school system are not native speakers of English (Mata-McMahon). |
| Quotations or paraphrases from experts (cited) | Catherine Snow, Professor of Education at Harvard University, insists that "[i]t's ironic ..." (full quotation in the lesson material) |
| Examples (cited if you learned them through research) | A Korean father, Mr. Rhee, explains that his family "spen[t] a whole summer in Korea" and that his son's Korean "was improved a lot" (qtd. in Kang 435). |
| Personal experience or observations | the writer's cousins in Switzerland learned Portuguese easily; her niece in the United States understands it but cannot speak it |

### Citation has two parts
- An **in-text citation**: the author's name (or the title, if there is no author), with a page number if there is one: (Brown 31). "qtd. in" marks words of a person quoted in another source: (qtd. in Brown 33).
- The **Works Cited** page: a detailed list of the sources at the end of the paper (see Lesson 38).

### When to cite
| You must cite | You do not need to cite |
|---|---|
| exact words from a source (in quotation marks) | general knowledge: things that everybody knows |
| a paraphrase of a source | statements that are obviously true |
| information or ideas from a source that you did not know before your research | anecdotal evidence from your own experience |

Be especially careful with numbers and statistics: without a citation, it will look as if you made them up.

> **Psychology note:** Psychology uses APA style: author, year and page, for example (Brown, 2011, p. 31). For words that you found in another source, APA writes "as cited in" instead of "qtd. in": (San Diego et al., as cited in Tran, 2010, p. 261).

## Answer key

## Read aloud
Pause briefly at each / and stress the words in bold.
1. "Heritage language is the **essence** of who we are," / writes Clara Lee Brown.
2. As one immigrant father explains, / "We need to talk about our **roots** as immigrants. / Keeping Korean means keeping our **roots**."

## Writing task
Write one body paragraph of 150 to 220 words for a research paper. Choose a topic sentence (or write your own):
1. Your project: "Getting enough sleep is one of the most effective ways for university students to remember what they study." Use two sources from your annotated bibliography (Lesson 34).
2. The book's topic: "Public schools should help immigrant children to maintain their heritage language." Use the Mata-McMahon article.

Include your claim, at least two types of cited evidence (one paraphrased), personal experience only as extra support (optional), an explanation, a concluding sentence, and a Works Cited (or References) entry for each source.

Self-editing checklist:
- Does my topic sentence make a clear claim?
- Did I use at least two different types of evidence?
- Does every quotation, paraphrase and statistic have a citation?
- Are exact words in quotation marks, and is my paraphrase in my own words with the exact meaning?
- Did I explain how the evidence supports my claim?
- Is there a Works Cited (or References) entry for every source?

Paste your draft into the chat and ask your tutor for feedback. Then rewrite it.
