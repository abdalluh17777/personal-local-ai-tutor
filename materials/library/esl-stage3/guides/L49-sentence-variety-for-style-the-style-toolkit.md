# Lesson 49: Sentence Variety for Style; The Style Toolkit

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 5: Argument and Academic Style*
*Adapted from Reading, Writing, Research, and Reasoning: An Advanced ESL Text by Gabriel Winer and Elizabeth Wadell, ASCCC Open Educational Resources Initiative (CC BY-NC 4.0): 6.12 Using Sentence Variety; 6.13 Language Toolkit; 6.14 Takeaways.*

## In this lesson
You will review the four sentence types and use them to make your writing livelier and to show clearly how your ideas are related. You will punctuate coordination and subordination correctly, count and revise the sentence types in a student's paragraph, and use the chapter's Language Toolkit to check your drafts. Varied, well-connected sentences make an academic argument easier to follow.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Identify simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences.
- Join independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) or with a semicolon and a transition, and punctuate them correctly.
- Join a dependent clause to an independent clause and decide whether a comma is needed.
- Revise a paragraph for sentence variety and emphasis, using the Language Toolkit as a checklist.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| independent clause | a subject and a verb that express a complete thought; it can stand alone | California is one of the world's leading almond producers. |
| dependent (subordinate) clause | a subject and a verb that do not express a complete thought | although the state is dry for much of the year |
| coordination | joining two independent clauses with related ideas of equal importance | a compound sentence |
| subordination | joining a dependent clause to an independent clause, so that one idea gets more emphasis | a complex sentence |
| coordinating conjunction | for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (FANBOYS) | ..., and each year California exports many of its almonds. |
| subordinating conjunction | a word that begins a dependent clause | although, even though, since, because, when |
| transition | a word or phrase that shows the logic between two clauses or sentences | as a result, however, moreover |
| sentence variety | using different sentence types, lengths and rhythms | Sentence variety can help maintain your readers' interest. |
| monotony | dull sameness | Writers avoid monotony by varying their sentence structures. |

## Rules and explanations
### Four sentence types
| Type | Structure | The book's example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | one independent clause | California is one of the world's leading almond producers. |
| Compound (coordination) | two independent clauses of equal importance | California is one of the world's leading almond producers, and each year California exports many of its almonds. |
| Complex (subordination) | one independent clause + one or more dependent clauses | California is one of the world's leading almond producers although the state is dry for much of the year. |
| Compound-complex | at least two independent clauses + one or more dependent clauses | Although it is dry for much of the year, California is one of the world's leading almond producers; each year, it supplies about 80% of the world's almonds. |

- In a complex sentence, the idea you want to emphasize usually goes in the **independent clause**. (Your material says the almond sentence "emphasizes that demand for water is increasing". This is a slip: its independent clause, and so its emphasis, is that California is a leading almond producer.)
- A dependent clause can also begin with **who**, **that** or **which** (a relative clause), and it also makes a sentence complex.

### Joining two independent clauses
**1. Comma + coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS)**

| Logic | Conjunction | Logic | Conjunction |
|---|---|---|---|
| cause | for | contrast | but, yet |
| addition | and | choice or alternative | or |
| negative alternative | nor | effect | so |

Put a comma before the conjunction only when it joins two clauses, not when it joins two words or phrases. After **nor**, the auxiliary verb comes before the subject, as in a question.

**2. Semicolon + transition + comma**
"There was a shortage of PPE in 2020**; as a result,** farmworkers did not have access to important protective equipment." (PPE = personal protective equipment)

| Logic | Transitions (Toolkit table) |
|---|---|
| Addition | also, furthermore, moreover, similarly, likewise |
| Contrast | instead, however, otherwise |
| Emphasis | namely, certainly, indeed |
| Cause and effect | accordingly, as a result, consequently, hence, thus, therefore |
| Time | finally, next, subsequently, then |
| Condition | otherwise |
| Choice or alternative | alternatively, on the other hand, conversely |

(The Toolkit introduces this table as "subordinating conjunctions", but these words are transitions. Its Time row also lists "after", which is a subordinating conjunction or preposition; the transition is "afterward".)

### Joining a dependent clause to an independent clause
- Subordinating conjunctions include **although, even though, since, because, when**.
- **Dependent clause first: use a comma.** Although it is dry for much of the year**,** California is...
- **Dependent clause second: no comma.** California is one of the world's leading almond producers although the state is dry...

### Varying your sentences
- Writing with mostly simple sentences can be repetitive and boring, and it may lack the transitions that show how ideas are related.
- Mix lengths and rhythms. Use parallel structures for parallel ideas, but vary your structures to avoid monotony.
- A very short sentence in a paragraph can emphasize an idea.
- When you revise, ask: Are my sentences mostly simple? Mostly compound? Or varied?

### The Style Toolkit: a checklist for your drafts
| Check | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Strong verbs: a weak verb ("be", "do", "have") + noun becomes one verb | We have a strong belief in food equity. | We strongly believe in food equity. |
| Clear pronouns (it, they, this, these...) | ...can take longer to prepare. This is a potential disadvantage. | ...These factors are potential disadvantages. |
| Active voice | The lettuce was washed thoroughly by her aunt. | Her aunt washed the lettuce thoroughly. |
| Connotation | Not sure a word fits? | Look it up in example sentences on the internet, or ask your tutor. |

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic texts often join many clauses with "wa" (and) or "fa" (so, then). In English, a long chain of "and" clauses sounds unplanned: use subordinating conjunctions and transitions to show the exact relationship, and keep some sentences short.
> - A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses in English (a comma splice). Use a comma + FANBOYS, or a semicolon + transition + comma.

**Exercise 4. Coordinating conjunctions.** Complete each sentence from the Toolkit with a FANBOYS conjunction that shows the logic in brackets.
1. Many Americans suffer from hunger, ________ food scarcity is a serious social issue in this country. (cause)
2. The neighborhood has never had a grocery store, ________ has it had a community garden. (negative alternative)
3. California is suffering from a catastrophic drought, ________ most of the nation's produce is grown here. (contrast)
4. Some environmental experts have commented that California's water supply can support its population, ________ it can support its agriculture -- but not both. (choice)

**Exercise 5. Transitions.** Join each pair into one sentence with a semicolon, a transition that shows the logic in brackets, and a comma.
1. Community gardens are places that allow people to provide food for themselves. This food is frequently more nutritious than what can be bought at grocery stores. (addition)
2. Research on Google Maps shows that there are at least ten corner liquor stores in West Oakland. There are no supermarkets like Whole Foods. (contrast)
3. It is important to draw attention to food inequity. Many people are unaware that it exists at such high levels in the United States. (emphasis)
4. There are insufficient outlets for fresh food in their community. It's hard for residents of West Oakland who live in food deserts to access healthy produce. (cause and effect)
5. First, we are going to take a survey of the neighborhood's needs. We can write a proposal. (time)

Self-editing checklist:
- Is there a balance of simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences?
- Does each connector show the right logic (cause, contrast, addition, effect...)?
- Is there a comma before FANBOYS only when it joins two clauses?
- Does a semicolon come before, and a comma after, each transition that joins two clauses?
- Is there a comma after a dependent clause that comes first, and none before one that comes second?
- Is my most important idea in an independent clause?
- Toolkit: strong verbs, clear pronouns, active voice where possible?
