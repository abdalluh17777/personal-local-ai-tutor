# Lesson 23: Outlining

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 3: Paragraphs, Essays and Cohesion*
*Adapted from College ESL Writers: Applied Grammar and Composing Strategies for Success by Barbara Hall and Elizabeth Wallace, Georgia State University - Perimeter College (CC BY-NC-SA): 4.3: Outlining.*

## In this lesson
You will learn how to put your ideas in an order that fits your purpose (chronological order, spatial order or order of importance) and how to set out a formal outline, both as a topic outline and as a sentence outline. You will outline the model essay on stereotype threat from Lesson 20 and then outline your own Unit 3 essay. Many university instructors ask for an outline before a major paper, and a good outline makes the drafting in Lessons 24 to 26 much easier.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Choose chronological order, spatial order or order of importance to fit a purpose.
- Format a formal outline with the thesis at the top, Roman numerals, capital letters and Arabic numerals.
- Write a topic outline with parallel headings, and turn it into a sentence outline.
- Test an outline with the book's checklist.

## Rules and explanations
### Order and purpose
Order is your choice of what to present first, second, third and so on. In some courses your assignments are your only direct contact with your instructor, so a well-ordered paper makes a good impression. Choose the order that best fits your purpose (to inform, to explain, to persuade, or a combination):

| Order | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Chronological order | to explain the history of an event or a topic; to tell a story or relate an experience; to explain how to do or make something; to explain the steps in a process |
| Spatial order | to help readers visualize something as you want them to see it; to create a main impression using the senses (sight, touch, taste, smell and sound) |
| Order of importance | to persuade or convince; to rank items by their importance, benefit or significance |

A longer paper may use different orders in different parts.

### Informal and formal outlines
- An **informal outline** is a short list of key ideas in order. It keeps you focused in a stressful situation, such as an essay question on a test.
- A **formal outline** is a detailed guide for a longer assignment. It shows which ideas are of equal importance and which are of lesser importance. If you must submit it with your final draft, revise it to match the changes you made while writing.

### The format of a formal outline
- Put your **thesis** (not your introduction) at the top.
- Use **Roman numerals** (I, II, III) for the main points that develop the thesis.
- Use **capital letters** (A, B, C) to divide main points into parts.
- Use **Arabic numerals** (1, 2, 3) to divide an A, B or C into smaller parts: the examples and specifics.
- **Never divide a point into only one part:** for every I there must be a II, for every A a B, and for every 1 a 2.
- Use indentation to show how the ideas are related. The outline may end with suggestions for the concluding paragraph.

```
Thesis statement
I. Main point 1  -> topic sentence of body paragraph 1
   A. Supporting detail  -> support sentence of body paragraph 1
      1. Subpoint
      2. Subpoint
   B. Supporting detail
   C. Supporting detail
II. Main point 2  -> topic sentence of body paragraph 2
   A. Supporting detail
   B. Supporting detail
III. Main point 3  -> topic sentence of body paragraph 3
   A. Supporting detail
   B. Supporting detail
```

Note: in the lesson material, the main points of the skeleton appear as "1., 2., 3." because of the web page's formatting. In a formal outline, write I, II, III.

### Topic outlines and sentence outlines
Both types use the same format. A **topic outline** uses words or phrases, which keep it short and easy to understand; all its headings must be in **parallel structure** (for example, all noun phrases). A **sentence outline** uses complete sentences, which can later become your topic sentences and supporting sentences.

### Checklist for an effective topic outline
- Do I have a controlling idea that guides the development of the entire piece of writing?
- Do I have three or more main points that I want to make? Does each main point connect to my controlling idea?
- Is my outline in the best order (chronological order, spatial order or order of importance) for me to present my main points?
- Do I have supporting details that will help me inform, explain or prove my main points?
- Do I need to add more support? If so, where?
- Do I need to make any adjustments in my working thesis statement before I consider it the final version?

**Exercise 3** (example answer)

```
Thesis: Overall, McRaney and her colleagues make an understandable and
compelling argument for the existence of stereotype threat; the information
they present is engaging, seems balanced, and helped me make sense of my own
experiences.
I. An engaging and understandable presentation
   A. Personal questions at the start
   B. A conversational tone
      1. Use of the second person
      2. An example: "As you may have gathered ..."
   C. Paraphrases and summaries instead of direct quotations
II. A balanced treatment of criticism
   A. The early criticism: lab studies, not natural settings
   B. The response: naturalistic research confirming the lab studies
   C. The result: a rounded view and greater credibility
III. A personal connection
   A. The chapter's point about feedback and negative stereotypes
   B. The writer's freshman math class
      1. No questions in class
      2. Worse test results
   C. A new understanding of her poor performance
Conclusion: the three points restated; steps to lessen stereotype threat
```

The headings at each level are noun phrases (parallel structure). The essay follows the order of the three parts of its thesis. Of the book's three methods, it is closest to order of importance, because its purpose is to persuade; it ends with its most personal point.

## Writing task
**A formal outline of your Unit 3 essay (about one page).** Use the narrowed topic from Lesson 21 and the revised working thesis from Lesson 22 (for example, on stereotype threat and how schools can reduce it, stress and coping in first-year students, or sleep and memory).
1. Write your thesis at the top.
2. Choose an order (chronological, spatial or order of importance) and note why it fits your purpose.
3. Write a topic outline with three main points (I, II, III). Give each main point at least two supporting details (A, B), and add subpoints (1, 2) for examples and specifics. Note where each piece of evidence will come from (for psychology: the reading in Lesson 20, the OpenStax textbook *Psychology 2e*, or articles from Google Scholar or PubMed Central).
4. End with a suggestion for your concluding paragraph.
5. Rewrite your three main points as complete sentences: these are the topic sentences of your body paragraphs.

**Self-editing checklist**
- My thesis, not my introduction, is at the top, and every main point connects to it.
- I have three main points in the best order for my purpose.
- The format is correct: I, II, III; A, B, C; 1, 2, 3, with indentation.
- No point is divided into only one part.
- The headings at each level are parallel.
- Each main point has enough supporting details, and I know where more support is needed.
- I adjusted my working thesis if the outline changed my ideas.
