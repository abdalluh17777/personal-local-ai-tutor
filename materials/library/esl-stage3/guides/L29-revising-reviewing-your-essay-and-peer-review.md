# Lesson 29: Revising: Reviewing Your Essay and Peer Review

*English Stage 3 (C1) · Unit 3: Paragraphs, Essays and Cohesion*
*Adapted from College ESL Writers: Applied Grammar and Composing Strategies for Success by Barbara Hall and Elizabeth Wallace, Georgia State University - Perimeter College (CC BY-NC-SA): 5.1 Reviewing Your Essay; 5.2 Completing a Peer Review.*

## In this lesson
You learn the difference between revising (improving your ideas) and editing (improving how you express them), and you revise your essay draft for unity, coherence, concision and word choice. The book's peer review becomes a self-review with its checklist, followed by a second review from your tutor; then you decide which feedback to use. Revising is where a first draft becomes a good university essay.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain the difference between revising and editing.
- Check a draft for unity and coherence, in separate readings.
- Cut wordiness and choose specific, formal words.
- Review a draft with the book's peer-review questions.
- Evaluate feedback by the purpose of the assignment and the audience.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| revise | take a second look at your ideas: add, cut, move or change information | delete off-topic sentences |
| edit | take a second look at how you expressed your ideas: words, grammar, punctuation, sentence structure, style | correct a verb form |
| unity | all the ideas in each paragraph and in the essay belong, in a logical order | no digressions |
| coherence | the ideas flow smoothly; the wording shows how one idea leads to the next | transitions within and between paragraphs |
| digression | an unplanned move away from the main topic | Tuyet's comments about the sales staff |
| wordiness | using more words than you need | "as to whether or not" |
| concise | short and clear, with no unnecessary words | "The Biology Department sponsors two major experiments." |
| cliché | an overused expression that has lost its meaning | green with envy, better late than never |
| connotation | the feeling a word carries | proud (positive), conceited (negative) |
| peer review | feedback on a draft from another writer | "Your peer reviewer is your first real audience." |
| constructive criticism | comments that help a writer improve | "Ask someone you trust for feedback and constructive criticism." |
| recursive | done again at several stages, not only at the end | Reviewing, like planning and drafting, is recursive. |

## Rules and explanations

### Revising and editing
- When you **revise**, you look again at your **ideas**: you add, cut, move or change information to make them clearer, more accurate, more interesting or more convincing.
- When you **edit**, you look again at **how you expressed** your ideas: you add or change words and fix grammar, punctuation and sentence structure (Lesson 30).
- Be critical of yourself in a positive way: have high expectations and train your eye to see what needs fixing.
- To see your draft with fresh eyes: take a break (a few hours or a day); ask someone you trust for constructive criticism (here, your tutor); pretend you are one of your readers.

### Unity and coherence
- **Unity**: every idea belongs and is in a logical order. When writers are rushed or tired, they drift from the controlling idea. Unplanned **digressions** usually harm writing: Tuyet deleted her complaint about the sales staff at an electronics store, because her paragraph was about televisions.
- **Coherence**: the ideas flow, and the wording shows how one idea leads to the next, within and between paragraphs.
- Read your draft aloud and note where you feel confused.
- Look for each type of problem in a **separate sweep**: read once for unity, and a second time for coherence.
- Transitions improve coherence. Hall and Wallace group them by purpose:

| Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|
| sequence or time | at first, meanwhile, afterward, finally |
| position | above, beside, beyond, opposite |
| a conclusion | indeed, hence, in the final analysis, thus |
| continuing a line of thought | furthermore, additionally, moreover, in the same way |
| changing a line of thought | however, nevertheless, on the contrary, on the other hand |
| importance | above all, in fact, more important, most important |
| final thoughts | finally, last, most of all |
| all-purpose openers | admittedly, certainly, generally speaking, undoubtedly |
| examples | for instance, for example, specifically |

On a printed draft, writers use a caret (^) to show where to insert an addition.

### Clear and concise
Almost every first draft can be clearer and more concise. Look for:
- sentences that begin "There are ... that";
- unnecessary modifiers and repeated meanings ("famous and well-known");
- phrases that add little: *in terms of, with a mind to, on the subject of, as to whether or not, more or less, as far as ... is concerned*;
- the passive voice and forms of *to be*: a subject that performs the action, with a strong verb, is clearer;
- "which is" clauses that can become short phrases, and repeated words that can be combined.

### Specific, appropriate words
College essays use formal English. The book's other examples are in Exercise 3.
- Avoid **slang** (kids) and **overly casual** language: a formal tone needs formal words.
- Avoid **contractions**: write *do not* for *don't* and *I am* for *I'm*.
- Avoid **clichés**, overused expressions that are empty of meaning (green with envy, better late than never).
- Check **sound-alike words** in a dictionary: allusion/illusion, complement/compliment, concurrent/consecutive, founder/flounder, historic/historical.
- Choose the **connotation** you want: *proud* is positive; *conceited* is negative.
- Replace **vague words** (people, nice, good, bad, interesting) with specific words or details.

### Peer review, adapted for self-study
Writers are often too close to their own work: pride gets in the way, and a sentence that makes perfect sense to you may be "total gibberish" to someone else. Reviewing other people's writing trains you to review your own. Reviewing is **recursive**: you can ask for feedback on an introduction before you write the rest.

You study alone, so do it in three steps:
1. **Self-review** your draft (or a model) with the book's questions, "Another Format for Peer Review" (the box "Questions for Peer Review" is a picture and is not in your material):
   - **Organization:** strengths and weaknesses; confusing parts; the most important point; broad to specific; every sentence supports the topic sentence and every paragraph the thesis; transitions; flow.
   - **Focus and style:** one subject throughout; the thesis as a promise ("In this paper, the writer will ...") and whether it is kept; the writer's position; a style that suits the assignment and the audience.
   - **Development:** a title that shows the topic and catches interest; an opening that draws readers in; a conclusion that brings the main points together, without new topics.
   - **Conventions:** grammar, spelling, punctuation and other mechanics.
2. **Tutor review:** paste the draft and ask your tutor to review it with the same questions, starting with one positive comment and one question for more information, as the book asks reviewers to do.
3. **Evaluate the feedback.** You do not have to use every suggestion. Accept or reject each one by two criteria: (1) does it support the **purpose of the assignment**? (2) is it appropriate to the **audience**? If the same comment appears again and again, act on it in future assignments too.

**Psychology note:** like Hall and Wallace, APA style prefers the active voice, but psychology writers use the passive when the person who acts is unimportant, especially in Method sections: "Participants were randomly assigned to two groups."

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic rhetoric often uses pairs of near-synonyms and repetition for emphasis. In English academic writing, this usually reads as wordiness: the book's "famous and well-known" becomes "well-known".

## Writing task
Revise your complete essay draft from Lessons 24 to 28 into a second draft. (If you are starting a new topic, first write a short essay of four or five paragraphs on stereotypes or on a PSYC 110 topic such as stress and coping, then revise it.)

1. Take a break of at least a few hours.
2. **Unity sweep:** read the essay aloud. Mark any sentence that does not support its topic sentence or the thesis; cut or move it.
3. **Coherence sweep:** check transitions and links within and between paragraphs (Lessons 27 and 28).
4. **Concision and word choice sweep:** cut wordy phrases; replace slang, contractions, clichés and vague words.
5. **Self-review:** answer the four groups of review questions in writing about your own essay. Write one positive comment and one question.
6. **Tutor review:** paste your essay and ask for a peer review (see Speak with your tutor).
7. **Decide:** list each suggestion as accepted or rejected, with your reason (purpose or audience). Then revise.

Self-editing checklist:
- Every paragraph supports my thesis, and every sentence supports its topic sentence.
- My paragraphs are in a logical order, and transitions show how the ideas connect.
- My thesis makes a promise, and the body paragraphs keep it.
- I removed wordy phrases, unnecessary modifiers and repetition.
- I used formal, specific words: no slang, contractions or clichés.
- My title shows the topic, and my conclusion adds no new topics.
- I accepted or rejected each suggestion for a clear reason.
