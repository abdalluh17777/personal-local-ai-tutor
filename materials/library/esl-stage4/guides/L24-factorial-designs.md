## In this lesson
You will learn how factorial designs combine two or more independent variables and how to interpret their three kinds of results: main effects, interactions and simple effects. In English, you will practise the precise wording these results need: *on average*, *depends on*, *but only if*.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Read a design's notation (IVs, levels, conditions) and draw a factorial design table.
- Choose between- or within-subjects manipulation for each factor, and limit causal conclusions to manipulated variables.
- Identify main effects, spreading and crossover interactions, and simple effects in a table of means.
- Report factorial results accurately with *on average*, *depends on* and *only*.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| factorial design | Each level of one independent variable (IV) is combined with each level of the others to produce all possible combinations; each combination is a condition. | Cell phone use (yes, no) × time of day (day, night) |
| factorial design table | Rows = levels of one IV, columns = levels of a second IV, each cell = a condition. | A 2 × 2 table has four cells. |
| mixed factorial design | At least one IV is manipulated between subjects and at least one within subjects. | Phone use within, time of day between |
| non-manipulated independent variable | A variable (usually a participant variable) treated as an IV but measured, not manipulated. | Private body consciousness |
| main effect | The effect of one IV averaged across the levels of all other IVs. | Driving was better, on average, without a phone. |
| interaction | When the effect of one IV depends on the level of another IV. | Disgust mattered only for people high in private body consciousness. |
| spreading interaction | One IV has an effect at one level of the other IV and a weak effect or no effect at the other level. | A strong phone effect at night, a weaker one by day |
| crossover interaction | One IV has opposite effects at different levels of another IV. | Caffeine raises extraverts' verbal scores but lowers introverts' |
| simple effects | The effect of each IV at each level of the other IV; used to break down an interaction. | Caffeine in introverts, then in extraverts |
| the effect of X depended on Y | Reports an interaction | "the effect of disgust depended on private body consciousness" |
| but only if | Restricts a finding to one group | "harsher moral judgments—but only if they scored relatively high in private body consciousness" |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**Why several IVs?** Schnall, Haidt, Clore and Jordan (2008) manipulated disgust (testing in a clean or a messy room) and measured *private body consciousness*, the attention people pay to their own bodily sensations. Participants rated the moral acceptability of behaviours (e.g., eating one's dead dog) from 1 to 7. In the messy room they were more disgusted and judged more harshly, "but only if they scored relatively high in private body consciousness". Interactions like this are "often among the most interesting results in psychological research".

**Notation.** Each number is one IV (also called a *factor*); its value is that IV's number of levels; their product is the number of conditions: 2 × 2 = 4 conditions, 3 × 2 = 6, 4 × 5 = 20, 2 × 2 × 2 (three IVs) = 8, 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 (four IVs) = 24. More than three IVs is unusual: the conditions become unmanageable, and so does the number of participants needed for adequate statistical power.

**Assigning participants.** Each IV can be between subjects (simpler, no order or carryover effects, less time per participant) or within subjects (more efficient, controls extraneous participant variables). In the book's mixed design, phone use is within subjects (counterbalanced) and time of day between subjects, so each participant is tested in two of the four conditions.

**Non-manipulated IVs.** Brown and colleagues (1999) manipulated word type (health-related words such as *tumor* vs. unrelated words such as *election*) and measured hypochondriasis (excessive concern with ordinary bodily symptoms). Such variables are usually participant variables and therefore between-subjects factors. The study is still an experiment if at least one IV is manipulated, but causal conclusions apply only to the manipulated IV. Schnall's team could conclude that disgust affected moral judgments, but not that private body consciousness did: a third variable such as neuroticism might cause both a strict moral code and bodily awareness. If *no* IV is manipulated (measured mood × measured self-esteem), the study is non-experimental.

**A worked example.** *Invented data for practice*, following the pattern the book describes for its hypothetical driving study (higher = better driving). The table is also the study's factorial design table, with a mean in each cell.

| | No cell phone | Cell phone | Mean |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day** | 8 | 7 | 7.5 |
| **Night** | 6 | 3 | 4.5 |
| **Mean** | 7 | 5 | |

- **Main effect of phone use**: averaged across time of day, 7 vs. 5 (better without a phone).
- **Main effect of time of day**: averaged across phone use, 7.5 vs. 4.5. There is one main effect per IV, and main effects are independent of each other.
- **Interaction**: the phone costs 1 point by day (8 − 7) but 3 at night (6 − 3), so its effect depends on time of day: a *spreading* interaction.
- **Simple effects**: the phone effect by day and at night; the time effect with and without a phone.

With real data, a factorial ANOVA tests whether the main effects and the interaction are statistically significant.

**More interactions.** Everyday: whether you go to a film may depend on the film *and* on who is coming. Research: Brown's high-hypochondriasis participants recalled more health-related words, but not more neutral words. In a **crossover** (Gilliland, 1980), introverts outperformed extraverts on a verbal test without caffeine, but extraverts outperformed introverts after 4 mg of caffeine per kilogram of body weight; in a line graph the lines literally cross.

**Simple effects.** An interaction suggests that main effects "may be a bit misleading". In Gilliland's crossover, averaging would wipe out a main effect of caffeine, although caffeine clearly matters; Schnall's main effect of disgust held for only half the participants. A simple effects analysis, needed only when there is an interaction, examines each IV at each level of the other. A 2 × 2 design has 2 main effects and 4 simple effects; 2 × 3 has 2 and 5 (3 + 2); 3 × 3 has 2 and 6.

### Language focus
**Main effect or interaction? The wording must match the result.**
- Main effect: "driving performance was better, *on average*, when participants were not using cell phones than when they were." Signals: *on average*, *overall*, *averaging across*.
- Interaction: "the effect of disgust *depended on* private body consciousness"; "*If* they were high in private body consciousness, *then* those in the messy room made harsher judgments. *If* they were low..., *then* whether the room was clean or messy *did not matter*."

When there is an interaction, do not report a main effect alone; add the restriction (*but only*, *only for*).

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic has no indefinite article, so *a / an* is often left out. Write "there was *an* interaction", "*a* main effect of disgust", "*a* 2 × 2 factorial design".

## Answer key
**1. Reading questions**
1. Disgust was manipulated (a clean or a messy room); private body consciousness was measured with a questionnaire; the dependent variable was the harshness of moral judgments (ratings from 1 to 7) (9.1).
2. 3 × 3: two IVs, nine conditions. 2 × 2 × 2: three IVs, eight conditions (9.2).
3. People are either high or low on them (e.g., hypochondriasis); they cannot be tested in both conditions (9.2).
4. People high in hypochondriasis would recall negative health-related words more accurately than people low in hypochondriasis, but recall non-health-related words about the same (9.3).
5. Averaged across personality, caffeine's positive effect on extraverts would be wiped out by its negative effect on introverts. No: the interaction shows that caffeine's effect depends on personality (9.3).
6. Only when there is an interaction; otherwise "the main effects will tell the complete and accurate story" (9.3).

**2. From the book**
1. Age and eater type cannot be manipulated; need for closure, a personality characteristic, was probably measured.

| Title | Independent variables | Dependent variable |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | temporal delay; orientation | haptic (touch) object recognition |
| 2 | intergroup contact; need for closure | prejudice |
| 3 | expectancies; coping | pain-induced intentions to smoke |
| 4 | age; divided attention | spontaneous recognition |
| 5 | food size; package size; restrained vs. unrestrained eater | consumption behaviour |

2. (example answer) A 2 × 2 table: room temperature (comfortable vs. hot) in the rows, noise level (quiet vs. noisy) in the columns; four conditions. Both IVs between subjects, with random assignment: taking a long exam four times would cause practice, fatigue and other carryover effects. Other justified answers are acceptable, e.g., noise within subjects with two equivalent test versions in counterbalanced order, to control differences in ability.
3. Example cell means (many others are correct; invented means have no sampling error). A main effect of A: the A means differ. A main effect of B: the B means differ. An interaction: the effect of B at A1 differs from its effect at A2.

| Result | A1B1, A1B2, A2B1, A2B2 | A1 / A2 means | B1 / B2 means | Effect of B at A1 / A2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 none | 5, 5, 5, 5 | 5 / 5 | 5 / 5 | 0 / 0 |
| 2 A | 5, 5, 8, 8 | 5 / 8 | 6.5 / 6.5 | 0 / 0 |
| 3 B | 5, 8, 5, 8 | 6.5 / 6.5 | 5 / 8 | +3 / +3 |
| 4 A, B | 4, 6, 7, 9 | 5 / 8 | 5.5 / 7.5 | +2 / +2 |
| 5 A, B, A × B | 5, 5, 5, 9 | 5 / 7 | 5 / 7 | 0 / +4 |
| 6 A, A × B | 4, 6, 8, 6 | 5 / 7 | 6 / 6 | +2 / −2 |
| 7 B, A × B | 4, 8, 6, 6 | 6 / 6 | 5 / 7 | +4 / 0 |
| 8 A × B | 4, 8, 8, 4 | 6 / 6 | 6 / 6 | +4 / −4 |

Results 5 and 7 are spreading interactions; 6 and 8 are crossovers (8 resembles Gilliland's pattern).

**3. Language**
1. (example answer) The effect of hypochondriasis on recall depended on word type: people high in hypochondriasis recalled more health-related words than people low in hypochondriasis, but there was no difference for non-health-related words.
2. Those in a messy room made harsher moral judgments, but only if they were high in private body consciousness.
3. There was **an** interaction between caffeine and personality, which is **an** example of **a** crossover interaction.
