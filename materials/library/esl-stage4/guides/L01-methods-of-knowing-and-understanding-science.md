# Lesson 01: Methods of Knowing and Understanding Science

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 1: Psychology as a Science*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 1.1 Prelude to the Science of Psychology; 1.2 Methods of Knowing; 1.3 Understanding Science.*

## In this lesson
You will compare five ways of acquiring knowledge, learn the three features that make psychology a science, and see how science differs from pseudoscience. On the language side, you will practice defining terms the way the textbook does and expressing cautious conclusions with hedges.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Describe the five methods of knowing, with one strength and one problem of each.
- Explain the three fundamental features of science and why psychology is a science.
- Decide whether a claim is falsifiable and explain why a belief is pseudoscientific.
- Write a one-sentence academic definition of a research term.
- Recognize and use hedges in conclusions.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| intuition | relying on our guts, emotions or instincts; believing what feels true | Your friend will not look at you, so you decide she is lying. |
| authority | accepting ideas because an authority figure (parent, doctor, professor, the media) says they are true | Your parents told you to make your bed. |
| rationalism | using logic: stating premises and following logical rules to reach conclusions | All swans are white; this is a swan; so it is white. |
| empiricism | acquiring knowledge through observation and experience | The world looks flat, so people believed it was flat. |
| scientific method | a process of systematically collecting and evaluating evidence to test ideas and answer questions | counting the words that 369 students actually spoke |
| science | a general way of understanding the natural world featuring systematic empiricism, empirical questions and public knowledge | Psychology takes this approach to behavior. |
| systematic empiricism | learning about the world through careful observation | planning, making, recording and analyzing observations |
| empirical question | a question about the way the world actually is that can be answered by making systematic observations | Do women talk more than men? |
| public knowledge | detailed descriptions of research that are available to others, usually through publication in a professional journal | Mehl's study appeared in *Science*. |
| replication | conducting a study again, exactly or with modifications, to see if the same results are observed | The handwashing effect did not appear again. |
| pseudoscience | beliefs or activities claimed to be scientific that lack one or more of the three features of science | biorhythms, astrology |
| falsifiable | a claim is falsifiable if there is an observation that would, if it were made, count as evidence against it | "Women talk more than men" is falsifiable. |
| on the basis of available evidence | judging from the evidence we have now | "We therefore conclude, on the basis of available empirical evidence, that ..." |
| To be clear, ... | stops the reader from drawing a conclusion that is too strong | "To be clear, at this stage we are still unable to definitively conclude ..." |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**Why psychology needs science (1.1).** A widely cited estimate says that women speak 20,000 words a day and men 7,000. Mehl and colleagues found that no one had actually counted. In their study, 369 college students wore audio recorders: women spoke an average of 16,215 words a day and men 15,669, an extremely small difference that could easily be explained by chance. They concluded that the stereotype is "unfounded".

**Five methods of knowing (1.2)**

| Method | Main problem | But ... |
|---|---|---|
| Intuition | driven by cognitive and motivational biases, not logic or evidence | intuitive decisions are sometimes better than analysis (Gladwell's *Blink*) |
| Authority | authorities may be wrong, may rely on intuition, or may want to mislead you (a made bed is a warm, damp home for mites) | we cannot check everything ourselves; we can evaluate their credentials, methods and motives |
| Rationalism | wrong premises or errors in logic give invalid conclusions (there are black swans in Australia) | correct premises and logic make it a sound method |
| Empiricism | our experience is limited, our senses deceive us, and prior experience changes what we perceive | it is at the heart of the scientific method |
| Scientific method | not always feasible (time, resources); answers only empirical questions | the most likely method to produce valid knowledge |

Scientists may use the first four to generate ideas, but they test them with systematic empiricism (careful observation under controlled conditions) and use rationalism to reach valid conclusions.

**Three features of science (1.3; Stanovich, 2010).** The sciences share neither subject matter nor equipment; they share a general approach to understanding the natural world.
1. **Systematic empiricism.** Scientists check their ideas against planned, recorded observations. When Mehl's data conflicted with the stereotype, the researchers trusted the data.
2. **Empirical questions.** "Is this stereotype accurate?" can be answered by observation. "Is it wrong to hold inaccurate stereotypes?" is a value judgment about how the world *ought* to be, which science cannot answer.
3. **Public knowledge.** Scientists publish their methods and results, increasingly in open access journals that anyone can read. Publication matters because science is a social process (a collaboration across time and space) and because it makes science **self-correcting**.

**Self-correction in action.** Schnall and colleagues (2008) reported that washing one's hands leads people to judge moral transgressions as less wrong. With the same materials, nearly identical procedures and a much larger sample, Johnson, Cheung and Donnellan did not find the effect, suggesting that the original result may have come from a small sample. Still, we cannot definitively conclude that the effect does not exist.

> **Accuracy note:** The book presents this replication as part of the "Many Labs" project, which tested 13 effects. In fact, the handwashing effect was not one of those 13: Johnson, Cheung and Donnellan's study was a separate replication, published in the same journal issue as Many Labs. The point about self-correction is unchanged.

**Science versus pseudoscience.** A belief is pseudoscientific if (a) its supporters claim or imply that it is scientific but (b) it lacks one or more features of science:
- no systematic empiricism: there is no research, or it is ignored (biorhythms, supposed 23-, 33- and 28-day cycles, remain popular although the evidence indicates they do not exist);
- no public knowledge: research is claimed but never published for others to evaluate;
- no empirical questions: the claims are not **falsifiable** (Karl Popper). If psychic powers "disappear when observed too closely", success and failure in a test are both consistent with the claim.

Pseudoscience matters to psychology because many pseudosciences (astrology, graphology, magnet therapy) claim to explain behavior, and because such beliefs can do real harm (homeopathy instead of an empirically supported treatment).

### Language focus
**1. Defining a term.** A good definition names a general class (a process, an attitude, a question) and then what makes the term different. The book's patterns:
- *X involves + -ing*: "Rationalism **involves using** logic and reasoning to acquire new knowledge."
- *X refers to + noun*: "Pseudoscience **refers to** activities and beliefs that are claimed to be scientific ... but are not."
- *X is a + class noun*: "The scientific method **is a process of** systematically collecting and evaluating evidence ..."

**2. Hedging a conclusion.** Careful writers show exactly how strong their evidence is.
- "We therefore conclude, **on the basis of available empirical evidence**, that ... the stereotype ... is unfounded."
- "..., **suggesting that** the original finding **may have stemmed from** the relatively small sample size"
- "To be clear, **at this stage we are still unable to definitively conclude** that the handwashing effect does not exist."

Hedges include modal verbs (*may, might, can*), verbs such as *suggest*, adverbs such as *allegedly*, and limits such as *at this stage*.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - General academic subjects take no article: "Psychology is a science", "Science relies on observations" (not "The psychology is the science"). Use *the* for something specific: "the scientific method".
> - After *involve*, use the -ing form: "Empiricism involves acquiring knowledge" (not "involves to acquire").

## Answer key
**1. Reading questions**
1. Women spoke an average of 16,215 words a day and men 15,669, an extremely small difference that could easily be explained by chance. Their systematic observations did not support the claimed 20,000 versus 7,000 (1.1).
2. We do not have time to question and research everything ourselves. We can evaluate their credentials, the methods they used to reach their conclusions, and whether they have reasons to mislead us (1.2, "Authority").
3. It is not always feasible, because it can require considerable time and resources, and it can answer only empirical questions (1.2, "The Scientific Method").
4. A general approach to understanding the natural world (1.3, "What Is Science?").
5. Science is a social process, a large-scale collaboration among researchers across time and space; and publication makes science self-correcting, because others can detect and correct errors (1.3).
6. Its adherents present it as scientific, with scientific-sounding terms such as *sinusoidal wave*, but it lacks systematic empiricism: the relevant research indicates that biorhythms do not exist, and it is ignored (1.3, "Science Versus Pseudoscience").
7. It makes the claim unfalsifiable: success would support it, and failure would also be "consistent" with it, so no possible observation could count against it (1.3).
8. It brings the features of science into sharper focus; pseudoscientific beliefs are widely held and can cause great personal harm, so we need to identify and evaluate them; and many claim to explain behavior, so psychology must distinguish itself from them (1.3).

**2. Book task.** Making your bed: authority (your parents); here it is wrong, because a made bed gives mites the warm, damp environment in which they thrive. White swans: empiricism (the swans you have seen); wrong, because there are black swans in Australia. A lying friend: intuition; she may simply be holding in gas or be preoccupied with something else (1.2). Your own examples will vary.

**3. Language exercise**

A. (example answers)
1. Systematic empiricism refers to learning about the world through structured, careful observation.
2. Intuition involves relying on our guts, emotions and instincts and believing what feels true.
3. Replication is the process of conducting a study again, exactly or with modifications, to see if the same results are observed.

B. 1. "If reliable" (a condition: the effect is not established) and "might" (a possibility, not a fact). 2. "Allegedly" (the author reports what believers claim without accepting it).

C. (example answer) "The replication suggests that the handwashing effect may not be reliable, but at this stage we cannot conclude that it does not exist."

## Writing task
**Project warm-up.** Your research project (a proposal on sleep and memory in university students, or a topic of your own) starts in Lesson 04. Write one paragraph (150 to 200 words) about what you already believe about your topic and how you know it.

Include:
- two or three beliefs about the topic (for example, "Students remember less after a night without sleep");
- for each belief, the method of knowing behind it and one problem with that method;
- whether each belief is an empirical question and whether it is falsifiable;
- one definition of a key term, using a pattern from the Language focus;
- a final hedged sentence about what you would need to find out.

Self-editing checklist:
- Did I name a method of knowing for each belief, with one problem?
- Did I say whether each belief can be tested by systematic observation?
- Does my definition use a class noun, *involves* or *refers to*?
- Did I use *involve* + -ing and no article before general subjects ("memory", "sleep")?
- Is my final sentence hedged?
