## In this lesson
No statistical decision is guaranteed to be right. You will learn about Type I and Type II errors, statistical power, criticisms of null hypothesis testing, psychology's "replicability crisis" and the open science practices that respond to it. You will practise the language of concession and hedging, and complete the results section of your project.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Define Type I and Type II errors and explain how α, power and the file drawer problem affect them.
- Use the book's power table to plan a sample size.
- Summarize the criticisms of null hypothesis testing and the recommended responses.
- Explain the replicability crisis, questionable research practices and open science, using concession and hedging.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Type I error | rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true | concluding there is a relationship when there is not |
| Type II error | failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is false | missing a real relationship with too small a sample |
| file drawer problem | statistically significant results are more likely to be submitted and published than nonsignificant results | non-significant results put away "in a file drawer" |
| *p*-hacking | making research decisions until the results give a desirable *p* value | arbitrarily removing outliers |
| statistical power | the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis for a given sample size and expected relationship strength | Power of .80 is adequate. |
| confidence interval | a range computed so that, usually 95% of the time, the population parameter lies within it | 160 to 240 calories |
| replicability crisis | the inability of researchers to replicate earlier research findings | only 36 of 100 replications were significant |
| open science practices | practices that make research transparent and open | pre-registration, open data, open materials |
| although ..., ... | concession: admits one point before the main claim | Although not widely adopted, this rejection ... has continued the conversation. |
| does not necessarily | a hedge that limits a conclusion | A failure to replicate a result by itself does not necessarily discredit the original study. |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**Errors.** Two decisions (reject, retain) × two states of the world (the null hypothesis is false, true) give two correct decisions and two errors.
- **Type I error:** rejecting a true null hypothesis. If the null hypothesis is true and α = .05, this happens 5% of the time (α is the "Type I error rate").
- **Type II error:** retaining a false null hypothesis, mainly because the design lacks power (for example, a small sample).
- α = .01 reduces Type I errors but increases Type II errors; α = .10 does the opposite; .05 keeps both acceptable. Any single study may be an error, so researchers **replicate**.

**The file drawer problem** (Rosenthal, 1979). Significant results are published; non-significant ones stay in the "file drawer". So the literature contains more Type I errors than expected and overstates effects (if the true correlation is +.10, studies may find −.10 to +.40, but mainly the stronger ones are published). Responses: **registered reports** (journals evaluate a study without knowing its results), sharing non-significant results, and the *Journal of Articles in Support of the Null Hypothesis*. Simonsohn, Nelson and Simmons (2014) also accused researchers of ***p*-hacking**: removing outliers arbitrarily, choosing which dependent variables to report, presenting only significant results.

**Statistical power** = 1 − the probability of a Type II error. With *N* = 50 and *r* = +.30, power is .59, so the Type II risk is .41. Aim for .80 **before** collecting data. Sample sizes for power of .80 (two-tailed):

| Relationship strength | Independent-samples *t* test | Test of Pearson's *r* |
|---|---|---|
| Strong (*d* = 0.80, *r* = .50) | 52 | 28 |
| Medium (*d* = 0.50, *r* = .30) | 128 | 84 |
| Weak (*d* = 0.20, *r* = .10) | 788 | 782 |

With 20 participants per condition and *d* = 0.50, power is only .34. To increase power, strengthen the relationship (a stronger manipulation, better control, a within-subjects design) or, usually, enlarge the sample.

**Criticisms of null hypothesis testing**
- *Misunderstood:* *p* is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and 1 − *p* is not the probability of replication (in Oakes's study, 60% of researchers thought *p* = .01 meant a 99% chance of replicating).
- *Illogical:* a rigid line makes *p* = .04 publishable and *p* = .06 "not significant".
- *Uninformative:* rejection only says the relationship is not exactly zero. Defenders (Abelson, 1995) answer that it gives a principled way to show that results are not mere chance. In 2015, the editors of *Basic and Applied Social Psychology* banned null hypothesis testing (they remove *p* values before publication); cite them as Trafimow and Marks (2015), not "Tramimow" as the book misprints.
- **Recommended:** an **effect size** with every test; **confidence intervals** (*M* = 200 calories, 95% CI 160 to 240, so the mean differs significantly from 250, which lies outside); or **Bayesian statistics**. Null hypothesis testing with effect sizes and confidence intervals remains dominant.

**The replicability crisis.** The Many Labs project failed to replicate Schnall et al.'s (2008) finding that hand washing makes moral transgressions seem less wrong. In the **Reproducibility Project**, over 270 psychologists repeated 100 published experiments: 97 originals were significant, but only 36 replications, with effect sizes on average half as large. A failed replication does not necessarily discredit the original (power, populations, procedures or moderating variables may differ), but some see **questionable research practices**: selective deletion of outliers, selective reporting ("cherry-picking"), **HARKing** (hypothesizing after the results are known), *p*-hacking (checking significance before deciding to collect more data) and, as fraud, fabrication (Stapel).

**Responses:** sufficient power; publishing null and significant findings; methods described in enough detail to replicate; high-quality replications; and **open science**, such as badges in *Psychological Science* for pre-registered hypotheses and analysis plans, open materials and open data, and the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) guidelines adopted by more than 500 journals.

### Language focus
**Concession and hedging in critical discussion.** The book admits the other side before making its point, and it limits its claims.
- Concession: "**Although** they did not propose a better statistical test ..., the editors emphasized the importance of descriptive statistics and effect sizes." / "**To be fair**, many researchers have come to the defense of null hypothesis testing." / "**Even those who** defend null hypothesis testing recognize many of the problems with it."
- Hedging: "the published literature **probably** contains a higher proportion of Type I errors"; "**is likely to** overstate the strength of that relationship"; "**does not necessarily** discredit the original study".

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Do not add *but* after *although*: "Although the result was significant, the effect was small" (not "Although ..., but ...").

## Answer key
**Part 1**
1. Type I: even with no relationship in the population, sampling error occasionally produces an extreme result. Type II: mainly a lack of statistical power (for example, a sample that is too small).
2. Mostly significant results are published, so the literature contains more Type I errors and overstates relationships. Registered reports are judged on the question and method before the results are known, so a non-significant result is as publishable as a significant one.
3. 1 − .59 = .41.
4. 128 for the *t* test; 84 for the test of *r*.
5. 97 of the 100 original studies had significant effects, but only 36 replications did, and the replications' effect sizes were on average half as large.
6. Hypothesizing after the results are known: mining the data without an *a priori* hypothesis and then claiming that a significant result had been predicted, so a possible chance result looks like a confirmed prediction.

**Part 2**
1. (a) Concluding that one therapy is more effective than the other when in the population they are equally effective. (b) Concluding that there is no evidence of a difference when one therapy really is more effective (for example, because the samples were too small).
2. (example answer; other answers are possible) Benefits: pre-registration makes HARKing and *p*-hacking visible, because readers can compare the plan with the report; shared data and materials let others check the analyses and replicate; more null results become visible, reducing the file drawer problem. Drawbacks: extra time and work; participants' privacy must be protected when data are shared; researchers may report fewer unexpected findings (which can still be reported as exploratory).
3. (example answer) By changing the choices (which party, which politicians, which measure of the economy, whether to exclude recessions), you can usually find a combination that gives *p* < .05. Each choice looks reasonable on its own, which is why *p*-hacking is easy to do without noticing. Pre-registering the choices would prevent it.

**Part 3** (example answers)
1. The failure to replicate does not necessarily mean that the original study was wrong.
2. Although null hypothesis testing is criticized, it is still the dominant approach.
3. The published literature is likely to overstate the strength of relationships.
4. Pre-registration may help to reduce the replicability crisis.

**Writing task** (example answer) "The hypotheses, the exclusion criterion and both analyses were pre-registered. One participant in the wake condition was excluded because she fell asleep during the rest period. As predicted, participants in the nap condition recalled significantly more word pairs (*M* = 22.00, *SD* = 2.54) than those in the wake condition (*M* = 17.00, *SD* = 3.09), *t*(18) = 3.95, *p* < .001, *d* = 1.86, 95% CI for the difference [2.34, 7.66]. In the nap condition, the positive correlation between minutes asleep and recall was not statistically significant, *r*(8) = .45, *p* = .20. Because only 10 participants contributed to this analysis, whereas about 28 are needed to detect a strong correlation with power of .80, a Type II error cannot be ruled out."
