# Lesson 21: Survey Research: Constructing Surveys

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 3: Research Designs*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 7.1: Prelude to Survey Research; 7.2: Overview of Survey Research; 7.3: Constructing Surveys.*

## In this lesson
Survey research measures thoughts, feelings and behaviors through self-reports from carefully selected samples. You will learn what defines a survey, why answering a question is a psychological process open to context effects, and how to write good items with the BRUSO model. In English, you will practice participle clauses that interpret results and the precise question forms that good items need.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Define survey research by its two characteristics and explain how a survey can also be an experiment.
- Describe the five steps of survey responding and two kinds of context effect.
- Choose between open-ended and closed-ended items and evaluate items with BRUSO.
- Build balanced rating scales and mutually exclusive, exhaustive categories, and order a questionnaire.
- Interpret results with participle clauses (*suggesting that*, *showing how*).

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| survey research | measuring variables with self-reports, with close attention to sampling | the National Comorbidity Survey |
| respondents | the usual term for participants in survey research | nearly 2,000 respondents aged 13 to 88 |
| context effect | an unintended effect of surrounding items or the response scale on responses to an item | middle options read as "normal" |
| item-order effect | the effect of responding to one item on responses to a later item | dating frequency asked before life satisfaction |
| open-ended item | asks a question and lets respondents answer however they want | "Is there anything else you would like to tell us about?" |
| closed-ended item | asks a question and provides response options to choose from | "How old are you?" with age ranges |
| rating scale | an ordered set of response options to a closed-ended item | Never, Rarely, Sometimes, Often, Always |
| Likert scale | agreement ratings (5 points) on several favorable and unfavorable statements, summed | an attitude toward a group |
| BRUSO | a model for good items: brief, relevant, unambiguous, specific, objective | "Have you ever owned a gun?" |
| double-barrelled item | asks about two separate issues but allows only one response | "...feeling anxious and depressed" |
| mutually exclusive / exhaustive | categories that do not overlap / that cover all possible responses | *Protestant* and *Catholic* (exclusive, not exhaustive) |
| suggesting that | introduces an interpretation of a result | "...only −.12, suggesting that the two variables are only weakly related" |
| At best ... At worst ... | the mildest and the most serious outcome | "At best, these influences add noise to the data. At worst, they result in systematic biases" |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**What survey research is.** Two characteristics: (1) variables are measured with *self-reports* (questionnaires or interviews); (2) sampling gets close attention, with a strong preference for large random samples, which give the most accurate estimates of what is true in the population. Beyond these, "almost anything goes" in length, mode and topic. Most surveys are non-experimental: they describe single variables or relationships (e.g., income and health). But Lerner et al. (2003) surveyed nearly 2,000 Americans aged 13 to 88 online after the September 2001 attacks and found that those primed to feel anger perceived less risk than those primed to feel fear. Self-reports and a large national sample make this survey research; manipulating emotion makes it an experiment.

**History and uses.** Surveys grew from social surveys of poverty, government surveys, market research and election polling. In 1936, *Literary Digest* sent ballots to millions and predicted a landslide for Landon; Gallup, with much smaller scientific samples, predicted Roosevelt's victory, and was right. In psychology, surveys are tied to the study of attitudes, stereotypes and prejudice, and they estimate the lifetime prevalence of disorders (the National Comorbidity Survey interviewed nearly 10,000 adults at home in 2002 and 2003).

**Answering is a cognitive process** (Sudman, Bradburn & Schwarz, 1996). Respondents (1) **interpret** the question, (2) **retrieve** information from memory, (3) form a tentative **judgment**, (4) **format** it into a response option and (5) **edit** the response if necessary. The item "How many alcoholic drinks do you consume in a typical day?" (from "a lot more than average" to "a lot fewer than average") causes problems at every step: Does beer count? Is a "typical day" a weekday? What is "average"? A heavy drinker may edit the answer to avoid looking bad.

**Context effects** come from an item's context, not its content:
- *Item order*: when life satisfaction was rated first, its correlation with dating frequency was −.12; when dating frequency came first, it was +.66, because dating was then more accessible in memory (Strack et al., 1988).
- *Response options*: a scale from "less than once a year" to "more than once a month" makes people think of major irritations and report few; people also treat the middle option as normal, reporting more television when it is 4 hours than when it is 2.
- *Remedy*: when there is no natural order, rotate, counterbalance or randomize items and options.

**Open-ended or closed-ended?**

| | Open-ended | Closed-ended |
|---|---|---|
| Use when | answers are unknown, influence must be avoided, or the question is still vague | likely answers are known; the variable is well defined |
| Researcher | easy to write; hard to analyze | harder to write; easy to analyze |
| Respondent | slower; often skipped | quick and easy |

**Rating scales** have 3 to 11 options, most often 5 or 7: 5 for *unipolar* scales (one construct, e.g., frequency: Never ... Always), 7 for *bipolar* scales (Like very much ... Dislike very much), where *branching* ("Do you generally like or dislike ice cream?" first) improves reliability and validity. Show verbal labels; convert them to numbers in the analysis. A *Likert scale*, strictly, sums agreement ratings on several favorable and unfavorable statements; a single 0-to-10 item is just a rating scale.

**BRUSO** (Peterson, 2000):

| Criterion | Poor item | Effective item |
|---|---|---|
| Brief | "Are you now or have you ever been the possessor of a firearm?" | "Have you ever owned a gun?" |
| Relevant | "What is your sexual orientation?" | Leave it out unless clearly relevant. |
| Unambiguous | "Are you a gun person?" | "Do you currently own a gun?" |
| Specific | "How much have you read about the new gun control measure and sales tax?" | "How much have you read about the new sales tax?" |
| Objective | "How much do you support the new gun control measure?" | "What is your view of the new gun control measure?" |

A *pilot test* shows how people interpret an item: ask a few people to explain how they understood it.

**Response options.** Categories should be *mutually exclusive* (*Christian* and *Catholic* overlap) and *exhaustive* (add *Other*, with space to write in). Balance the extremes around a neutral midpoint: *Extremely Unlikely | Somewhat Unlikely | As Likely as Not | Somewhat Likely | Extremely Likely*. A middle option is optional: leaving it out encourages people to think more deeply; including it lets them choose "neither."

**Formatting.** An *introduction* encourages participation (purpose, sponsor, incentives) and establishes informed consent (topics, time, the right to withdraw, confidentiality). Then come clear *instructions*, the *most important items* (while respondents are most interested and least tired), items grouped by topic or type, *demographic items last*, and *thanks*.

### Language focus
**1. Interpreting a result with a participle clause** (comma + *-ing* clause):
- "When the life satisfaction item came first, the correlation between the two was only −.12, *suggesting that* the two variables are only weakly related."
- "...the participants who were primed to feel anger perceived less risk than the participants who had been primed to feel fear—*showing how* risk perceptions are strongly tied to specific emotions."

The verb sets the strength of the claim: *suggesting* (tentative) or *showing*, *demonstrating* (strong).

**2. Plain, precise questions.** BRUSO is partly a language rule:
- Prefer a verb to a heavy noun phrase: "be the possessor of a firearm" becomes "own a gun."
- Use question word order (auxiliary + subject + verb): "*Do you* currently own a gun?"
- One question, one issue: *and* in an item may signal a double-barrelled item.
- Neutral wording: "What is your view of ...?", not "How much do you support ...?"

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic questions can keep statement word order, but English needs an auxiliary before the subject: "How many hours *do you* sleep?", not "How many hours you sleep?"
> - For experience up to now, Arabic uses the past tense, while English normally uses the present perfect, as in the book's item "*Have you ever* in your adult life *been* depressed for a period of 2 weeks or more?"

## Answer key
**1. Reading questions**
- 1.1 Variables are measured with self-reports (questionnaires or interviews), and close attention is paid to sampling, with a strong preference for large random samples (7.2, first paragraph).
- 1.2 It used self-report measures and a large national sample (survey research) and manipulated an independent variable, anger vs. fear, to assess its effect on risk judgments (experiment).
- 1.3 Major depressive disorder (16.9%). Higher in males: alcohol abuse (19.6% vs. 7.5%) and drug abuse (11.6% vs. 4.8%).
- 1.4 Reporting dating frequency first made that information more accessible in memory, so respondents based their life satisfaction rating on it: an item-order effect.
- 1.5 Any two: they take more time and effort; they are harder to analyze (answers must be transcribed and coded); respondents skip them more often.
- 1.6 To encourage participation and to establish informed consent. Demographic items are least interesting but easy to answer when respondents are tired or bored.

**2. Applying the section**
- 2.1 (example answer) "On a typical weekday (Monday to Friday), how many alcoholic drinks (beer, wine or spirits) do you have? ___ drinks," plus the same item for a typical weekend day. Defined terms remove the ambiguity, and a number replaces the vague "average" options. Other good versions are possible.
- 2.2 *Specific*: it is double-barrelled. Write one item about feeling anxious and one about feeling depressed.
- 2.3 *Christian* and *Catholic* overlap (not mutually exclusive), and the list is not exhaustive. Use non-overlapping categories (*Protestant*, *Catholic*, *Jewish*, *Hindu*, *Buddhist* ...) plus *Other (please specify): ____*.
- 2.4 *Extremely Unlikely | Somewhat Unlikely | As Likely as Not | Somewhat Likely | Extremely Likely* (the book's version).

**3. Language**
- (a) "..., suggesting that those who date more have a strong tendency to be more satisfied with their lives" (the book's sentence). (b) "..., suggesting that they treat the middle option as typical."
- *Suggesting* is better: each result fits the interpretation but does not prove it. In (a), the +.66 was itself produced partly by item order, so a strong claim would be risky.

## Writing task
**Research project: survey items, first draft (250-350 words).** Draft a short questionnaire for your study of sleep and memory in university students; you will pilot and finish it in Lesson 22.
1. An introduction (60-90 words): purpose, sponsor (e.g., your university), time needed, the right to withdraw, confidentiality.
2. Instructions, with an example for any unusual scale.
3. Eight items, the most important first: at least four closed-ended items, including a 5-point frequency scale (e.g., how often students feel sleepy in morning classes) and a balanced scale; one categorical item; one open-ended item; demographic items at the end.
4. A thank-you.
5. One sentence on how you will avoid an item-order effect between sleep items and memory items.

Model item: "On a typical weeknight (Sunday to Thursday), about how many hours do you sleep? ___ hours." If you work on your own topic, write items for its key variables.

**Self-editing checklist**
- Is every item brief, relevant, unambiguous, specific and objective?
- Does any *and* make an item double-barrelled?
- Are time frames and key words defined?
- Are categories mutually exclusive and exhaustive?
- Are rating scales balanced, with verbal labels?
- Does the introduction cover informed consent?
- Does every question use correct question word order?
