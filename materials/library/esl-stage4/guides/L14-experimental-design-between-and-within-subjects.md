# Lesson 14: Experimental Design: Between and Within Subjects

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 3: Research Designs*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 5.3: Experimental Design.*

## In this lesson
You will compare the two basic ways of designing an experiment: testing each participant in one condition (between subjects) or in all conditions (within subjects). You will see how random assignment and counterbalancing stop extraneous variables from becoming confounds, and practise the language of comparison.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain the difference between between-subjects and within-subjects experiments and list the pros and cons of each.
- Distinguish random assignment from random sampling, and describe simple and block randomization.
- Define order, carryover, practice, fatigue and context effects, and explain how counterbalancing deals with them.
- Choose and justify a design, comparing the two options in clear academic sentences.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| between-subjects experiment | an experiment in which each participant is tested in one condition | Half write about a traumatic event, half about a neutral event. |
| within-subjects experiment | an experiment in which each participant is tested in all conditions | The same participants judge an attractive and an unattractive defendant. |
| random assignment | assigning participants to conditions by a random procedure | flipping a coin for each participant |
| random sampling | selecting a sample from a population at random (rare in psychology) | Do not confuse random assignment with random sampling. |
| block randomization | random assignment in which all conditions occur once, in random order, before any is repeated | A C B / B C A / C B A |
| matched-groups design | participants are matched on the DV or an extraneous variable before being assigned to conditions | pairs of equally healthy participants |
| carryover effect | an effect of being tested in one condition on behaviour in later conditions (one kind of order effect) | judging more harshly because of boredom |
| practice effect / fatigue effect | better / worse performance in later conditions because of practice / tiredness or boredom | doing better on the second attempt |
| context effect (contrast effect) | an earlier condition changes how participants perceive stimuli or interpret their task | An average-looking defendant seems worse after an attractive one. |
| counterbalancing | systematically varying the order of conditions across participants | Half do A then B; half do B then A. |
| the former ... the latter | the first ... the second of two things just mentioned | The former are called between-subjects experiments and the latter are called within-subjects experiments. |
| the primary advantage / disadvantage of X is that ... | introduces the most important strength / weakness | The primary disadvantage of within-subjects designs is that they can result in order effects. |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
#### Between-subjects experiments and random assignment
In a **between-subjects experiment**, each participant experiences one level of the IV (for example, 60 people with severe agoraphobia, 20 in each of three treatments). The groups must be highly similar on average (sex, IQ, motivation, health), so that these participant variables do not become confounds.

- **Random assignment**, strictly, meets two criteria: each participant has an equal chance of each condition, and each is assigned independently (a coin flip for two conditions; a random integer from 1 to 3 for three).
- **Block randomization** avoids unequal group sizes (equal groups are statistically most efficient): within each block every condition occurs once, in random order. The book's table assigns nine participants as A C B, B C A, C B A. Never throw away collected data to make groups equal.
- Random assignment can fail by chance, but for three reasons this is not a major concern (Reading question 4). It is always considered a strength of a design.
- **Matched groups:** rank participants on a variable (for example, health), then randomly assign each pair of similar participants to different conditions, so that this variable cannot be confounded with the IV.

#### Within-subjects experiments and counterbalancing
In a **within-subjects experiment**, each participant is tested in all conditions. **Primary advantage:** maximum control of participant variables (the same people, so the same IQ, social class and so on), less noise, and fewer participants needed. **Primary disadvantage:** order effects, when responses are affected by the order of conditions.
- **Carryover effects:** a practice effect, a fatigue effect, or a context effect.
- Participants may also guess the hypothesis and respond as they think they are expected to, or try to be "fair".
- If the attractive defendant always comes first, order becomes a confounding variable.

**Counterbalancing** tests different participants in different orders, assigned randomly. "If a study does not involve random assignment in one form or another, it is not an experiment."

| Method | How it works | Orders needed |
|---|---|---|
| Complete counterbalancing | every possible order, equally often | 3 conditions: 6 (ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA); 4: 24; 5: 120; 6: 720 |
| Latin square | each condition appears once in each position | as many orders as conditions (6 for 6 conditions) |
| Random counterbalancing | a random order for each participant | for many conditions; adds random error |

Counterbalancing does two things: it stops order from being a confound, and it makes carryover effects detectable (analyse each order separately).

**A caution on the Latin square.** The book says that in its square "each condition precedes and follows each other condition one time". Check the square: A always comes immediately before B (rows 1, 3 and 4), and B never comes immediately before A. The first claim (each condition once in each position) is true; the second is true only of a *balanced* Latin square, for example ABDC, BCAD, CDBA, DACB.

**Box "When 9 Is 'Larger' Than 221":** in Birnbaum's (1999) between-subjects study, the number 9 received a mean rating of 5.13 and 221 a mean of 3.10 on a 1-10 scale of size. Participants compared 9 with other one-digit numbers and 221 with three-digit numbers. Birnbaum argues that the lack of context in between-subjects designs is often a bigger problem than the context effects of within-subjects designs.

**Simultaneous within-subjects designs** mix the conditions in one sequence, for example 10 attractive and 10 unattractive defendants in mixed order.

#### Choosing a design
| | Between subjects | Within subjects |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | conceptually simpler; less testing time per participant; no carryover effects | controls participant variables; less noise; fewer participants |
| Disadvantages | more noise from participant differences; more participants | order and carryover effects; participants may guess the hypothesis |

**Rule of thumb:** if a within-subjects design (with counterbalancing) fits the time available and carryover is not a serious concern, it is probably best. Choose between subjects when time per participant is short (a doctor's waiting room) or when a treatment produces long-term change: after a prejudice-reduction treatment works, participants are no longer suitable for the control condition. Researchers can also use both designs in different studies.

### Language focus
**Comparing and contrasting.** The section compares two designs throughout. Useful patterns:
- *The former ... the latter:* "The former are called between-subjects experiments and the latter are called within-subjects experiments."
- *Advantage / disadvantage:* "Between-subjects experiments **have the advantage of being** conceptually simpler ..."; "The **primary disadvantage** of within-subjects designs **is that** they can result in order effects."
- *Comparative structures:* "Within-subjects experiments also require **fewer** participants **than** between-subjects experiments"; "This is **not as powerful a** technique **as** complete counterbalancing."

Note: *have the advantage of* + *-ing*; *not as* + adjective + *a* + noun + *as*.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - After a preposition English needs the *-ing* form: "the advantage of **being** simpler", not "the advantage of be simpler".

## Answer key
### 1. Reading questions
1. Random sampling selects a sample from a population; random assignment assigns the participants in a sample to conditions. Random sampling is rarely used in psychology ("Random Assignment", paragraph 1).
2. Each participant has an equal chance of being assigned to each condition, and each is assigned independently of the others (paragraph 2).
3. Strict methods such as coin flipping often give unequal group sizes, and equal groups are statistically most efficient. Participants 4, 5 and 6 receive B, C and A: each block contains every condition once (paragraph 3 and table).
4. It works better than expected, especially with large samples; inferential statistics take its fallibility into account; a resulting confound would probably be detected when the experiment is replicated (paragraph 4).
5. Measure participants' health, rank them, then randomly assign the two healthiest to different writing conditions, then the next two, and so on ("Matched Groups").
6. 24 orders; a Latin square needs 6 ("Carryover Effects and Counterbalancing").
7. The number 9 got a mean rating of 5.13 and 221 a mean of 3.10. Participants compared 9 with one-digit numbers and 221 with three-digit numbers (box).
8. If the treatment works, participants' prejudice is reduced, so they are no longer suitable for testing in the control condition ("Between-Subjects or Within-Subjects?").

### 2. Book exercise
(example answers; other well-argued choices are possible)
- a. Between: no carryover, but more participants and more noise from fitness differences. Within: controls fitness differences, but the first program changes runners' fitness for months (a long-term change, like the prejudice example). **Better: between subjects**, perhaps with groups matched on fitness.
- b. Within: controls individual rating tendencies and needs fewer participants, but participants may guess the hypothesis, and context effects are possible. Between: avoids this, but needs more participants. **Better: within subjects**, with smiling and non-smiling photos in a mixed, counterbalanced order (a simultaneous within-subjects design).
- c. Within: passersby cannot be tested twice in a field setting and would notice a change of clothes. Between: simple, little time per person, no carryover. **Better: between subjects**, with the condition for each time period chosen by block randomization.
- d. Within: controls differences in memory ability and needs fewer participants; order effects are reduced by one mixed list of concrete and abstract nouns (a simultaneous within-subjects design). Between: needs more participants and adds noise. **Better: within subjects.**

### 3. Language exercise
(example answers)
1. In a between-subjects experiment each participant is tested in one condition, whereas in a within-subjects experiment each participant is tested in all conditions.
2. Unlike random sampling, which is rarely used in psychological research, random assignment is an important element of all experimental research.
3. Complete counterbalancing and a Latin square can both be used with six conditions, but the former needs 720 orders and the latter only six.
4. Although within-subjects experiments control extraneous participant variables, they can produce carryover effects.
5. Between-subjects experiments have the advantage of requiring less testing time per participant.
