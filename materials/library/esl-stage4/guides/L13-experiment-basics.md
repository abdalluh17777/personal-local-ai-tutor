# Lesson 13: Experiment Basics

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 3: Research Designs*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 5.1: Prelude to Experimental Research; 5.2: Experiment Basics.*

## In this lesson
You will learn what makes a study an experiment: the researcher manipulates an independent variable and controls extraneous variables, so that only the independent variable can explain differences between conditions. You will also practise the passive voice and the language of definitions.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Identify the independent variable, its conditions and the dependent variable in an experiment.
- Distinguish manipulation from control, and explain why pre-existing groups do not make an experiment.
- Explain how extraneous variables add noise or become confounding variables.
- Compare no-treatment, placebo, wait-list and best-alternative control conditions.
- Describe a procedure in the passive voice and define research terms precisely.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| experiment | a study in which an independent variable is manipulated and a dependent variable is measured while extraneous variables are controlled | Darley and Latané's study was an experiment. |
| independent variable (IV) | the variable whose changes are thought to cause a change in another variable | the number of witnesses that participants believed to be present |
| dependent variable (DV) | a variable that is thought to be the effect of the independent variable | whether the participant left the room to seek help |
| conditions | the levels of the independent variable | one, two or five other students |
| extraneous variable | anything that varies in a study other than the IV and the DV | writing ability, diet, time of day |
| control | holding extraneous variables constant | All participants were tested in the same room. |
| confounding variable | an extraneous variable that differs across the levels of the IV | higher average IQ in one condition |
| random assignment | assigning participants to conditions by a random procedure, such as flipping a coin | They randomly assigned their participants to conditions. |
| placebo effect | the positive effect of a placebo (a simulated treatment with no active ingredient) | a "sugar pill" that helps |
| placebo control condition / waitlist control condition | a control condition in which participants receive a placebo / receive the treatment after the study | an identical-looking pill without the active ingredient / They must wait. |
| randomized clinical trial | an experiment that tests the effectiveness of a psychological or medical treatment | a trial of a new psychotherapy |
| in other words | introduces a clearer restatement | In other words, whether changes in one variable cause a change in another. |
| over and above | more than; in addition to | improvement over and above that in the placebo group |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
#### The bystander experiment (Section 5.1)
- **Hypothesis** (Darley & Latané, 1968): the more witnesses there are to an accident or a crime, the less likely any of them is to help. **Theory:** diffusion of responsibility, meaning each witness feels less responsible.
- Real cases such as the murder of Kitty Genovese fitted the idea but proved nothing: would the witnesses have helped had there been fewer of them?
- **Method:** each student sat alone in a small room for an intercom "discussion" in which another "student" seemed to have a seizure (the comments were prerecorded). Participants were told that the discussion involved one, two or five other students.
- **Result:** helping fell from 85% to 62% to 31% as the number of "witnesses" increased. Because this was the only difference between the groups, it must have caused the difference in helping.
- **Box "The Parable of the 38 Witnesses":** the standard Genovese story is inaccurate (Manning, Levine, & Collins, 2007), and it may have drawn attention away from the conditions in which people do respond collectively.

#### Two fundamental features (Section 5.2)
1. **Manipulation:** the researcher systematically varies the level of the IV. Each level is a condition, often given a short name ("traumatic condition", "neutral condition"). Darley and Latané had *one* IV with *three* levels, not three IVs: a **single factor multi-level design**. One IV with two conditions is a **single factor two-level design**.
2. **Control:** the researcher minimises variability in extraneous variables (same room, same emergency) and randomly assigns participants so the groups start out similar.

In research, you **manipulate** the IV and **control** other variables; the two words are not synonyms.

Manipulation needs active intervention. Comparing people who already keep a journal with people who do not is not an experiment: journal keepers may also be more conscientious, more introverted or less stressed, and any of these could explain a difference in health. Variables that cannot be manipulated for practical or ethical reasons (such as early illness experience) need nonexperimental approaches.

#### Extraneous variables: noise and confounds
- **Participant variables** (writing ability, diet, gender) and **situation or task variables** (time of day, hand or computer, weather).
- **Noise:** in the mood and memory table, every happy participant in the idealised data recalls 4 happy events and every sad participant 3. The realistic data have the same means (*M* = 4, *M* = 3), but the scores vary widely, so the effect is harder to see.
- **Holding variables constant** reduces noise (same location, identical instructions, only right-handed participants in language studies), but a very narrow sample lowers **external validity**: how far results generalise beyond the people studied.
- **Confounds:** IQ varies in almost every experiment; that is acceptable if average IQ is similar across conditions. If the positive-mood group had higher IQs, nobody could tell whether mood or IQ caused its better memory. The general solution is random assignment (Lesson 14).

#### Treatment and control conditions
| Control condition | Participants receive | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| No-treatment | nothing | The treatment group may improve only because they expect to (placebo effect). |
| Placebo control | something like the treatment, without the active element | Expectations are similar, so extra improvement is caused by the treatment. |
| Wait-list control | the treatment later | Control participants still expect to improve (eventually). |
| Best available alternative | an existing treatment, e.g., exposure therapy for simple phobia | Asks "Does it work better than what is already available?" |

Informed consent requires telling participants that they may receive a placebo. In the box "The Powerful Placebo", knee patients given sham surgery improved as much as those given real surgery (Moseley et al., 2002).

### Language focus
**1. The passive voice in method descriptions.** Methods focus on what was done, not who did it, so they use *be* + past participle:
- "Each of their university student participants **was isolated** in a small room and **told** that he or she would be having a discussion ..."
- "These comments **had been prerecorded** and **were played back** to create the appearance of a real emergency."

Use the present passive for a general procedure ("participants **are randomly assigned**") and the future passive in a proposal ("Participants **will be assigned**").

**2. Defining terms.**
- *X is called Y:* "The different levels of the independent variable **are called** conditions."
- *X (referred to as Y):* "changes in one variable (**referred to as** an independent variable)"
- *Y is a + general class + that ...:* "A placebo **is a** simulated treatment **that** lacks any active ingredient ..."

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic needs no *be* in the present, so learners sometimes drop it from the passive. "Participants **were** assigned" is a passive; "participants assigned" means the participants did the assigning.
> - *Research* is uncountable: "much research", "two studies", never "researches".

## Answer key
### 1. Reading questions
1. The more witnesses there are to an accident or a crime, the less likely any of them is to help; the process is diffusion of responsibility (5.1, paragraphs 1-2).
2. IV: the number of other students participants believed were present; conditions: one, two or five other students; DV: whether the participant left the room to seek help (5.1, paragraph 4; 5.2, "What Is an Experiment?").
3. They were only consistent with the idea; there was no way to know whether the witnesses would have helped had there been fewer of them (5.1, paragraph 2).
4. Journal keeping was not manipulated. The groups already differed and probably differ in other ways (conscientiousness, introversion, stress), which are alternative explanations (5.2, "Manipulation of the Independent Variable").
5. Both data sets: happy *M* = 4, sad *M* = 3. Three sad-mood participants (scores 5, 7 and 5) recalled more than 4 (5.2, noise table).
6. It lowers external validity: results may not generalise beyond the people studied (5.2, paragraph after the table).
7. When average IQ differs across conditions; then IQ, not the IV, could explain the difference in the DV (5.2, "Extraneous Variables as Confounding Variables").
8. Placebo effects: the treatment group may improve because they expect to, and the no-treatment group does not (5.2, "Treatment and Control Conditions").

Self-editing checklist:
- One IV, with its levels called conditions.
- The IV is actively manipulated.
- The DV is measurable and clearly measures memory.
- Each extraneous variable has a specific method of control.
- One confound is named and dealt with.
- Every passive verb has *be* (will be assigned).
- Terms are defined; "research" has no plural.
