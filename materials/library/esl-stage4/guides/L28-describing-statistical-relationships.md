# Lesson 28: Describing Statistical Relationships

*English Stage 4 (Psychology) · Unit 4: Statistics in Research Reports*
*Adapted from Research Methods in Psychology (4th American edition) by Rajiv S. Jhangiani, I-Chant A. Chiang, Carrie Cuttler and Dana C. Leighton (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 12.2 Describing Statistical Relationships.*

## In this lesson
Most research questions in psychology are about relationships between variables. You will learn to describe differences between groups (means, standard deviations, Cohen's *d*) and correlations between quantitative variables (Pearson's *r*), and you will practise the English of comparison and correlation, including "the more ..., the more ...".

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Describe a group difference with means, standard deviations and Cohen's *d*, and judge its size.
- Explain why an "effect size" does not prove cause and effect.
- Compute Pearson's *r* from *z* scores, interpret it, and recognize when it misleads.
- Write comparisons and correlations in clear academic sentences.

## Key language
| Term or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| statistical relationship | a difference in the average score on one variable across levels of another variable | Mean fear ratings differed across the three conditions. |
| effect size | the strength of a statistical relationship | an "effect size" of *d* = 0.35 |
| Cohen's *d* | a measure of effect size for a difference between two groups or conditions | a large difference (*d* = 0.82) |
| bar graph | a graph used to show differences between the mean scores of two or more groups | the heights of the bars represent the means |
| positive / negative relationship | higher scores on one variable go with higher / lower scores on the other | self-esteem a week apart / name position and response time |
| linear / nonlinear relationship | points fit a straight line / a curved line | sleep and depression: an upside-down "U" |
| line graph / scatterplot | a point for the mean of *Y* at each level of *X* / a point for each individual | four quartiles of names / 25 students' self-esteem scores |
| Pearson's *r* | a measure of the strength of the correlation between two quantitative variables (−1.00 to +1.00) | *r* = −.77 |
| restriction of range | a limited range of scores on *X* or *Y*, which makes a relationship look weaker than it is | *r* = 0 for 18- to 24-year-olds only |
| the further ..., the faster ... | "the + comparative ..., the + comparative" for a correlation | The further toward the end of the alphabet students' last names were, the faster they tended to respond. |
| tend to be associated with | are linked with (no claim of cause) | Higher scores on one variable tend to be associated with higher scores on the other. |

## Rules and explanations
### The ideas
**Differences between groups.** Report each group's mean and standard deviation, and show the means in a bar graph. **Ollendick et al. (2009)** randomly assigned children with an intense fear (for example, of dogs) to one-session exposure (facing the feared object with a therapist), education (learning about phobias and coping strategies) or a wait-list control. A clinician who did not know the condition rated severity from 1 to 8: exposure *M* = 3.47 (*SD* = 1.77), education *M* = 4.83 (*SD* = 1.52), control *M* = 5.56 (*SD* = 1.21). Both treatments worked, but exposure worked better.

**Cohen's *d*** = (*M*1 − *M*2) / *SD*
- *M*1 is usually the treatment group (or the larger mean); *d* is reported as positive.
- *SD* is usually the **pooled within-groups SD**: √[(sum of squared differences in Group 1 + sum in Group 2) / (*n*1 + *n*2)]. With equal group sizes, this is about √[(*SD*1² + *SD*2²) / 2]. Informally, either group's *SD* can be used.
- *d* is a difference in *SD* units, like a *z* score: *d* = 0.50 means the means differ by half a standard deviation. **Cohen's guidelines:** about 0.20 small, 0.50 medium, 0.80 large. Exposure versus education: *d* = 1.36 / √[(1.77² + 1.52²) / 2] = 1.36 / 1.65 = 0.82, a large difference.
- *d* means the same on any scale, so results can be compared and combined across studies.
- **Caution:** "effect size" sounds causal. If exercisers are happier than non-exercisers (*d* = 0.35), only an experiment shows that exercise *caused* it; a cross-sectional study shows only that exercisers *were* happier, by a small to medium amount.
- **Hyde (2007)** expressed sex differences as *d* (positive = men higher): mathematical problem solving +0.08, reading comprehension −0.09, smiling −0.40, aggression +0.50, attitudes toward casual sex +0.81, leadership effectiveness −0.02. Most differences are small, many below 0.10 ("trivial"); hence her "gender similarities hypothesis".
- **PSYC 110:** in Lecture 14 (What Motivates Us: Sex), Paul Bloom says that males are more physically violent "on average", which does not mean "you can't find violent women or non-violent men": an average difference like Hyde's +0.50. He also reports Larry Summers's claim that men and women have the same average aptitude but men show more *variation*: a claim about standard deviations, not means.

**Correlations between quantitative variables**
- **Carlson and Conard (2011)** offered MBA students free basketball tickets by email: the later in the alphabet their last names were, the faster they responded (a **negative** relationship between name position and response time). A **line graph** shows the mean response time for each quartile of names; a **scatterplot** of 25 students' self-esteem scores one week apart shows a **positive** relationship. Use a line graph when *X* has few values, a scatterplot when it has many.
- **Pearson's *r*:** −1.00 to +1.00, with 0 meaning no relationship. **Cohen's guidelines:** about ±.10 small, ±.30 medium, ±.50 large. The sign shows direction, not strength.
- **Computing *r*:** turn every score into a *z* score, multiply each person's two *z* scores (the cross-product) and take the mean: *r* = Σ(*z*x*z*y) / *N*. Book example: *X* = 4, 7, 2, 5, 2 (*M* = 4.00, *SD* = 1.90); *Y* = 30, 54, 23, 43, 50 (*M* = 40.00, *SD* = 11.78); *r* = 2.64 / 5 = .53. (The book prints 0.26 as the *z* score of 43; it is 0.25, which does not change *r*.)
- **Pearson's *r* misleads** when the relationship is **nonlinear** (sleep and depression: people who sleep about eight hours are least depressed, yet *r* would be near zero), so make a scatterplot first; and with **restriction of range** (age and enjoyment of hip hop: *r* = −.77 overall, but 0 for 18- to 24-year-olds only).

### Language focus
**1. Comparing groups:** give the direction, then the size.
- "Both treatments worked, **but** the exposure treatment worked **better than** the education treatment."
- "There was **a large difference** (*d* = 0.82) **between** the exposure and education conditions."

**2. Describing correlations:** "**the + comparative ..., the + comparative ...**" with *tend to* (a tendency, not a rule).
- "**The higher** students scored on the first occasion, **the higher** they **tended to** score on the second occasion."
- For correlational or cross-sectional data, write "is associated with", not "causes".
- APA numbers: *d* = 0.82 has a zero before the point (*d* can exceed 1); *r* = .53 has none (*r* cannot exceed 1), as in the book's "*r* = −.77".

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - In "the + comparative" sentences, both halves need *the* and a comparative, followed by subject and verb: "The more hours students slept, the more pairs they recalled" (not "More students slept, more they recalled").

## Answer key
**Part 1**
1. Exposure, education and wait-list control; a clinician who did not know the child's treatment, on a 1-to-8 scale.
2. The two means differ by 1.20 standard deviations: a very large difference in psychological research.
3. It suggests that the difference is caused by being in one group, but only an experiment with random assignment shows that.
4. Attitudes toward casual sex (+0.81). Most sex differences are small or trivial, so men and women can be seen as fundamentally similar.
5. When the *x*-axis variable has (or is organized into) a small number of distinct values, such as four quartiles.
6. Yes; the sign shows only the direction of the relationship.
7. When the relationship is nonlinear, and when a variable has a restricted range.
8. √[(1.77² + 1.21²) / 2] = 1.52; *d* = (5.56 − 3.47) / 1.52 = 1.38, a very large difference.

**Part 2**
- Exercise 4. Japan: *M* = 25.00, *SD* = 3.90 (dividing by *N*) or 4.11 (*N* − 1). United States: *M* = 31.00, *SD* = 4.36 or 4.59. Bar graph: bars of 25 and 31 on a *y*-axis starting at 0. Pooled *SD* (book's formula) = √[(152 + 190) / 20] = 4.14; *d* = 6.00 / 4.14 = 1.45 (with *N* − 1: 1.38). (example answer) The American students scored about one and a half standard deviations higher: a very large difference, well above 0.80.
- Exercise 5. *r* = .42. Scatterplot: extraversion on the *x*-axis, friends on the *y*-axis; the points rise from lower left to upper right with much scatter, and one student (10, 315) lies far above the rest. (example answer) There was a medium-to-strong positive correlation: more extraverted students tended to have more Facebook friends.
