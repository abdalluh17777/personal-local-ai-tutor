# Lesson 13: Sentence Essentials 1: Phrases, Sentence Parts and Simple Sentences

*English Stage 2 (B2) · Unit 2: Sentences and Paragraph Types · American History*
*Adapted from Building Academic Writing Skills by Lin Cui, Harper College (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): Unit 7 Sentence Essentials: I. Pretest; II. Differences Between a Phrase and a Sentence; III. Basic Sentence Parts and Sentence Patterns; IV. Sentence Structure #1: Simple Sentences.*

## In this lesson
This is the first of two lessons on Unit 7. You learn to tell a phrase from a sentence, to name the four basic parts of a sentence, to use the five basic sentence patterns, and to recognize simple sentences. Every correct sentence in academic writing is built on a "subject + verb" structure, so this is the base for the compound and complex sentences in Lesson 14.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Tell a phrase from a sentence by looking for a "subject + verb" structure, not at the length.
- Identify the subject, verb, object and subject complement in a sentence.
- Write sentences in the five basic patterns and expand them with adjectives, adverbs and phrases.
- Recognize a simple sentence, including one with a compound subject or a compound verb.
- Divide run-together text into simple sentences with correct capital letters and periods.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| phrase | two or more words used together for part of an idea; no "subject + verb" structure | in the classroom |
| sentence | a "subject + verb" structure that expresses a complete idea | He woke up. |
| subject | who or what does the action, or what the sentence is about | The students work hard in this class. |
| verb | the action, or the word that explains the idea about the subject | The homework assignments seem interesting. |
| object | the receiver of the action; it comes after the verb | They have received good grades. |
| indirect object | the person who receives the direct object | The professor gives the students homework assignments. |
| linking verb | a verb that connects the subject with a word that describes it (be, seem, grow, turn) | The weather turns cold. |
| subject complement | a noun or adjective after a linking verb that tells about the subject | This is a writing class. |
| simple sentence (independent clause) | a sentence with only one "subject + verb" group; it expresses a complete, independent idea | Learning is important. |
| compound subject | two or more subjects that share the same verb | Maria and Sung have three classes. |
| compound verb | two or more actions done by the same subject | They are taking classes and working part time. |
| energetic (adj) | active, full of energy | There are twenty young and energetic students. |
| premier (adj) | first in rank, leading | one of the nation's premier and largest community colleges |

## Rules and explanations

### Phrase or sentence?
A **phrase** is two or more words used together for part of an idea, not a complete idea. There is no "subject + verb" structure in a phrase.
- Noun phrases: boys and girls, an excellent school, picture books
- Verb phrases: play together, read a story, learn shapes and colors
- Prepositional phrases (a preposition such as in, on or from + a noun): in the classroom, on the wall, from home to school

A **sentence** may contain different phrases. It:
- must have a "subject + verb" structure;
- always starts with a capital letter;
- must end with a period (.), a question mark (?) or an exclamation mark (!);
- must express a complete idea: "Are they learning shapes and colors in the classroom?"

Do not judge by length. Look for the "subject + verb" structure:
- Phrase: after a long nap on a beautiful mat in the corner of the nap room
- Sentence: He woke up.

### Four basic parts of a sentence
| Part | What it does | Example (the part in bold) |
|---|---|---|
| subject | shows who or what does the action, or what the sentence is about; usually comes first | **The writing class** is important. |
| verb | usually comes after the subject; shows the action or explains the idea | The teacher **meets** the students in Room D-144 every week. |
| object | usually receives the action; comes after the verb | All the students have finished **homework** on time. |
| subject complement | usually follows a linking verb and shows what the subject is about; a noun or an adjective | This is **a writing class**. (noun) The students grow **confident** in writing. (adjective) |

### Five basic sentence patterns
| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| 1. subject + verb | The semester has started. / Professors teach in colleges. |
| 2. subject + linking verb + subject complement (noun or adjective) | This is a writing class. / The weather turns cold as the semester goes on. |
| 3. subject + verb + direct object | We are taking a writing class. / I like my classmates. |
| 4. subject + verb + indirect object + direct object | The professor gives the students homework assignments. / The students left the professor a note yesterday. |
| 5. there + a form of "be" | There are twenty students in this class. / There was a quiz last week. |

In pattern 4, the indirect object (the students) is the person who receives the direct object (homework assignments).

**Expanding a sentence.** Add adjectives, adverbs, phrases and clauses to make a basic sentence more interesting. The added parts are in bold:
- The students grow confident in writing. → The students **gradually** grow **more** confident in writing **as the semester goes on**.
- There are twenty students in this class. → There are twenty **young and energetic** students **from different countries** in this **writing** class.

### Simple sentences
A simple sentence has only one "subject + verb" group, from the capital letter at the beginning to the end mark: "Students take classes." It is also called an **independent clause** (a clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb), because it explains a complete, independent idea. The book compares it to a single adult who lives an independent life.

A simple sentence can have more than one subject or verb, but they form only one group:
- Compound subject (two or more subjects share the same verb): "Are Maria, Sung, and Anna busy?"
- Compound verb (the same subject performs two or more actions): "They are taking classes and working part time."
- Both: "Maria and Sung study and work every day."

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
>
> - An Arabic sentence in the present can have no verb at all ("the class important"). An English sentence always needs a verb. Before an adjective or a noun that describes the subject, use a linking verb: "The writing class is important."
> - Arabic writing often joins many clauses with "and" and commas. In English, end each complete sentence with a period and start the next one with a capital letter (Exercise 3).

## Read aloud
Capitals show word stress. The mark | shows a short pause after a long subject; do not pause after a short one.
1. The Early Childhood Laboratory School at Harper College | provides fun learning to children.
2. The professor gives the students homework assignments.
3. The students gradually grow more confident in writing as the semester goes on.
4. There are twenty young and en·er·GET·ic students from different countries in this writing class.
5. Harper College is now one of the nation's pre·MIER and largest community colleges.

## Writing task
Write a paragraph of 8 to 10 sentences about your university, college or psychology course, like "Some Information about Harper College" in Exercise 3.
- Use only simple sentences (one "subject + verb" group in each). You will revise this paragraph for sentence variety in Lesson 14, so keep it.
- Use each of the five basic patterns at least once, and write the pattern number (1-5) after each sentence.
- Include one compound subject and one compound verb.
- Expand at least three sentences with adjectives, adverbs or phrases.
- Underline the subject of each sentence and write the verb in bold.

Self-editing checklist:
- Every sentence has a subject and a verb.
- Every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a period, a question mark or an exclamation mark.
- No phrase stands alone as a sentence.
- A linking verb comes before each subject complement: "The library is quiet."
- Each sentence has only one "subject + verb" group.
- All five patterns are there.
