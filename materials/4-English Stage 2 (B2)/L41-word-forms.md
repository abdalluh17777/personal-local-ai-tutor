# Lesson 41: Word Forms

*English Stage 2 (B2) · Unit 4: Words, Word Order and the Essay*
*Adapted from Building Academic Writing Skills by Lin Cui, Harper College (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): Unit 18 Word Forms.*

## In this lesson
You learn to recognize nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs by their position in a sentence and by their suffixes (endings such as -tion, -ful and -ly). You practise -ed and -ing adjectives and the word forms used in comparisons, with texts about social media, phones and family life. Choosing the right form (success, succeed, successful, successfully) makes your academic sentences correct and clear.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Name the usual positions of nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs in a sentence.
- Use common suffixes to recognize the part of speech of a word.
- Choose between -ed adjectives (feelings) and -ing adjectives (the cause of the feelings).
- Make comparisons with "the same ... as", "as ... as", -er / more ... than and the -est / the most.
- Find and correct word form errors in a paragraph.

## Key language
| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| part of speech | the job of a word in a sentence: noun, verb, adjective or adverb | "Success" is a noun; "succeed" is a verb. |
| word form | the form a word takes for each part of speech | success, succeed, successful, successfully |
| word family | words from the same base with different suffixes | care, careful, carefully, careless |
| suffix | something such as "ly" or "er" added to the end of a word | -tion, -ment, -ful, -ly |
| linking verb | a verb such as "be" or "grow" that links the subject to an adjective | Instagram has grown **popular**. |
| comparative | the form for comparing two: -er / more ... than | My brother runs **faster than** my sister. |
| superlative | the form for comparing three or more: the -est / the most | My sister is **the shortest** of all in my family. |
| dexterity | the ability to move freely and naturally | the dexterity of your tongue |
| articulate | speak clearly | articulating sentences in English |
| simplify | make something simple | identify, notify, simplify, verify |
| refugee | a person who has to leave his or her country because of politics, religion, war, natural disaster, etc. | employee, interviewee, refugee |
| opponent | a person from the opposite team | kicking the ball into their opponents' goal |

## Rules and explanations

### Four strategies
1. Learn words in context (in sentences): "present" can be a verb or a noun, with different meanings.
2. Try not to translate: "success, succeed, successful, successfully" may work alike in some languages, but not in English.
3. Learn word families and write your own sentences with each form.
4. Read as much as you can, and read aloud.

### Where each part of speech goes
- **Nouns** (names of people, places, things, ideas): before a verb (subject); after a verb (object); after a preposition ("On **Facebook**"); after another noun ("the timeline **page**"); after an adjective ("New **features**"); after a determiner ("His **videos**").
- **Verbs** (actions or states): after the subject ("Some people **like** Facebook"); after "to" (to + base verb, called the infinitive: "to **post** their news"); after the causative verbs "make, let, get" ("Parents do not always let their young children **use** the social media"; with "get", add "to": "get them **to use**").
- **Adjectives** (describe nouns): before a noun ("a more **popular** site"); after a linking verb ("Instagram is **full** of images").
- **Adverbs** (describe verbs, adjectives, other adverbs or whole sentences): before or after a verb ("News ... can **quickly** spread. It travels **fast**."); before an adjective ("**very** good news"); before another adverb ("**extremely** frequently"); before or after a whole sentence ("**Unfortunately**, not everyone ... likes my cousin's posts").

### Common suffixes
| Part of speech | Suffixes (examples) |
|---|---|
| Noun: people | -er/-or (employer, doctor), -ee (employee), -ist (scientist), -ian (musician), -ant (assistant) |
| Noun: things and ideas | -ance/-ence (importance, difference), -cy (democracy), -hood (childhood), -ion/-tion/-sion (decision, communication), -ment (government), -ness (happiness), -ship (friendship), -ty (ability) |
| Verb | -ate (create), -en (strengthen), -ify (simplify), -ize (realize) |
| Adjective | -able/-ible (valuable, responsible), -al (social), -ant/-ent (important, different), -ary/-ery (primary), -ful (helpful), -ic (historic), -ing/-ed (interesting/interested), -ish (foolish), -ive (creative), -less (careless), -ly (friendly), -ous (dangerous), -y (healthy) |
| Adverb | -ly (carefully, happily); many adverbs have no -ly: always, often, very, late, hard, fast, well |

There are many exceptions, so look at the context. Not all -ly words are adverbs: "all the **daily** posts" (adjective) / "I do not read everything **daily**" (adverb).

### -ed and -ing adjectives
- **-ed** adjectives describe someone's feelings: "The young players were **excited**." "The baby was **frightened** by the loud noise."
- **-ing** adjectives describe the person or thing that produces the feelings: "The soccer game was very **exciting**." "The loud noise ... was **frightening** to the baby."
- "All in all, the game was very **interesting**, and everyone was **interested** except the baby!"

### Comparisons
| Meaning | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the same | the same + noun (+ as) | My hair has the same color as my sister's. |
| the same | as + adjective/adverb + as | My hair grows as quickly as my sister's. |
| two things | more (count or noncount), fewer (count), less (noncount) + noun + than | My sister watches fewer soccer games than my brother. |
| two things | 1-syllable adjective/adverb + -er than; 2-syllable adjective in -y: -ier than | My brother runs faster than my sister. My brother is heavier than my sister. |
| two things | more/less + long adjective (3+ syllables) or -ly adverb + than | My sister swims more slowly than my brother. |
| three or more | the most + noun | My sister has the most patience of us all. |
| three or more | the + 1-syllable -est; the + 2-syllable -y adjective: -iest | My sister is the shortest of all in my family. |
| three or more | the most/the least + long adjective or -ly adverb | My sister is the least athletic in my family. |

Irregular forms: good/well - better - best; bad - worse - worst; far - farther/further - farthest/furthest; little - less - least; many/much - more - most.

Common mistakes: "more better" (better), "fastly" (fast), "more slow" (more slowly), "best ways" (the best ways).

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
>
> - Arabic uses one form (the pattern of "akbar") for both "bigger" and "the biggest". English needs two forms: -er / more ... than for two things, and the -est / the most, with "the", for three or more.

## Read aloud
The stress stays on the same syllable in this word family: suc·CESS (noun), suc·CEED (verb), suc·CESS·ful (adjective), suc·CESS·ful·ly (adverb).
- Mark Zuckerberg and his friends launched Facebook suc·CESS·ful·ly.
- The young players were excited /ɪd/ during the soccer game yesterday.
- The parents were amused /d/ by the children's behaviors.
- The baby was frightened /d/ by the loud noise from the soccer field.
- The kids were exhausted /ɪd/, and the parents, too.
- I feel relaxed /t/ when I lie on the beach on a summer evening.

## Writing task
Write a comparison paragraph of 150 to 200 words, with at least eight sentences of comparison (Exercise 10). Choose one topic: two or more social media sites, phones, members of your family, vacations you have had, books you have read, or kinds of food.
- Include a title, a topic sentence, supporting sentences with details, and a concluding sentence.
- Use "the same ... as", "as ... as", two comparatives (-er / more ... than), two superlatives (the -est / the most), and at least one -ed and one -ing adjective.

Self-editing checklist:
- Each word is the right part of speech for its position (noun after "a/the", adjective before a noun, adverb to describe a verb).
- Adverbs that describe verbs have the right form (carefully, well, fast).
- -ed adjectives describe feelings; -ing adjectives describe the cause.
- Comparatives have "than"; superlatives have "the".
- No double forms ("more better", "more cheaper").
- I compared two things with -er / more and three or more with the -est / the most.
