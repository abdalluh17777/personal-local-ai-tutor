# Lesson 04: The Writing Process

*English Stage 2 (B2) · Unit 1: Academic Writing Essentials · Psychology*
*Adapted from Building Academic Writing Skills by Lin Cui, Harper College (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): Unit 2 The Writing Process.*

## In this lesson
You will follow one paragraph assignment from the first idea to the final copy through the three stages of the writing process: pre-writing, writing and post-writing. You will practise narrowing down topics, sorting ideas, and revising and editing a student's draft. These steps will make all your assignments, including your psychology papers, better organized and more accurate.

## Learning goals
After this lesson you should be able to:
- Explain the three stages of the writing process and the steps in each stage.
- Understand an assignment, narrow down a broad topic and brainstorm ideas.
- Make a paragraph outline with a full topic sentence and supporting ideas in words and phrases.
- Revise, edit and proofread a draft with the Self Checklist.

## Rules and explanations
### The three stages
If you miss or reverse one step when you bake bread, the bread fails. Writing is the same: it follows a step-by-step order called the writing process.

| Stage | Steps |
|---|---|
| **1. Pre-writing:** get ideas and make an outline | Understand the assignment. Choose your topic and narrow it down if necessary; brainstorm by free writing, listing, or mind mapping. Decide on the main idea and make an outline. |
| **2. Writing:** write the ideas into a paragraph | Write the ideas into sentences based on the outline. Include the basic parts: title, topic sentence, supporting sentences with details, transitions, concluding sentence. |
| **3. Post-writing:** revise, edit, proofread, and submit | Revise the ideas and the organization (unity, cohesion, completion). Edit the grammar. Check format, spelling, punctuation and capitalization. Make a new copy, proofread, save and submit. |

### Stage 1 in detail
**Understand the assignment.** Ask seven questions: Purpose? How? Who reads? Final product? Format? Due? Where? See the book's example table in section IV.

**Choose and narrow your topic.**
- Do: write from your heart. Ask: Am I interested in this topic? How much do I know about it? Do I have an experience or perspective that most classmates might not have?
- Do not: pick a seemingly "easy" topic that you know little about, or choose a topic because the Internet has a lot of information on it.

**Brainstorm.** Write as fast as you can. Do not worry about grammar, spelling or punctuation, and do not judge your ideas. Read the book's three examples: free writing, a list and a mind map.

**Make an outline.** Decide on the main idea, and keep or throw out ideas.
- Write the topic sentence as a **complete sentence** at the top.
- Write the supporting ideas and details in **words and phrases** (symbols such as # and $ are fine), not complete sentences. Then you can easily spot an idea that does not support the topic sentence.
- A new idea while you write? Put it in the outline first and ask: Does it support my topic sentence? Which part does it belong to? Is there already a similar idea?
- The title is often written last.

### Stages 2 and 3
**The draft** is the first complete copy, written from the outline. Mistakes are normal at this stage.

**Post-writing** has four steps:
1. Revise the ideas and organization: add examples and transitions, delete irrelevant sentences, add any missing title or concluding sentence.
2. Edit grammar and sentence structure.
3. Check format, spelling, punctuation and capitalization. Do step 2 before step 3.
4. Go through the Self Checklist (see the Writing task). Then make a new copy, **proofread** it, save it and submit it.

> **Note for Arabic speakers**
> - Arabic has no capital letters, so check capitals carefully in Stage 3: sentence beginnings, the pronoun "I", and names ("i" and "english" in the book's draft are errors).
> - In Arabic the subject pronoun is often left out because the verb shows the person. English needs a subject in every sentence: "Can let my thoughts flow" → "I can let my thoughts flow".

## Writing task
**Exercise 4 in the book: from brainstorming to paragraph.** Choose three of these topics: driving in the U.S. (or in your country); my first class at college; the neighborhood where I grew up; the person I admire the most; a childhood memory; a disappointing holiday; advantages or disadvantages of cell phones; characteristics of a good shopping mall.
1. Brainstorm each topic in a different way: free writing (NSNT style), a list, a mind map. Narrow down the topic if necessary.
2. Write an outline for each topic: the topic sentence as a full sentence, the supporting ideas and details in words and phrases.
3. Choose one outline and write a paragraph of about 200 words with a title, a topic sentence, 3 supporting ideas with details, transitions and a concluding sentence.
4. Format: typed, double spaced, font size 12, 1-inch margins, first line indented; your name, class and date at the upper right.

**Self Checklist** (from the book)
- Is my paragraph clear to understand and interesting to read?
- Did I include a topic sentence with a clear topic and a controlling idea?
- Did I include 3 supporting ideas with details, transitions and a concluding sentence?
- Did I try my best to use correct sentence structure, verbs, and other grammar aspects?
- Did I try my best to have correct spelling, punctuation, and capitalization?
- Did I follow the proper paragraph format?
